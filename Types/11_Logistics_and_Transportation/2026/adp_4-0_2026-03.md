                                Foreword

In just a few years, the number of conflicts globally has increased in number and intensity. Insights gained
from observing these conflicts will shape how the joint force utilizes advancements in military technology to
sustain and conduct future operations. ADP 4-0 provides the doctrinal foundation for the sustainment
warfighting function. It discusses principles that enable the provision of logistics, financial management,
personnel services, and health service support. As the Army’s keystone sustainment doctrine, it equips leaders
with the guidance necessary to sustain operations in today’s dynamic environment.

The modern battlefield presents challenges across the competition continuum and Army strategic contexts.
In the Indo-Pacific theater, China’s expanding military power threatens U.S. allies, and escalating tensions
in the region may shift the Pacific theater from strategic competition to conflict. In the European theater, the
Army must address Russia's military resurgence and long-range threats. In the Central Command theater, we
must manage the complexities of the post Israel-Hamas conflict in Gaza, while the Syrian civil war further
destabilizes the region. In Central and South America, political instability, narcoterrorism, and violence have
triggered widespread displacement, creating humanitarian crises and significant regional hardships. ADP 4-0
outlines how the Army prepares to address these challenges and supports joint operations through the
integration and synchronization of sustainment activities. It provides a framework for overcoming the
unprecedented threats of strategic competitors in today’s complex operational environment.

Multidomain operations drive the need for Army sustainment operations to evolve to meet the challenges
described in ADP 3-0. As we prepare to meet and overcome these challenges, Army forces are expanding
their capabilities to enable success in future operating environments. Maneuver units are expanding their
capabilities to conduct warfare through advanced sensors, increased lethality in long-range precision fires,
unmanned aerial systems, and survivability. Army sustainment forces are expanding their capabilities
through the development of a data-centric unified network and predictive sustainment capabilities. This
allows precision sustainment to gain efficiencies in decision dominance, autonomous distribution, demand
reduction, advanced power, and maritime operations. The dilemmas posed by the integration of new
technologies into the future battlefield will continue to change the way the Army and the joint force sustain
operations. In the future, sustainment will be more complex, demanding, vulnerable, and visible to the enemy.
Therefore, sustainment capabilities must be more integrated, synchronized, responsive, and dispersed than
ever before.

Sustainment modernization efforts prioritize the Pacific theater and are focused on deterring adversaries,
resolving conflicts, and winning our nation’s wars. These efforts include developing theater-specific
sustainment solutions tailored for disaggregated operations and providing scalable support options to meet
mission demands. The Army demonstrates its readiness and commitment to unified action partners through
building partner capacity and modernization of sustainment capabilities. Sustainment doctrine continues to
evolve in response to operational demands, fostering agile sustainment professionals and increasing
interoperability across the total force and with unified action partners. It focuses on interorganizational
cooperation and generating innovative Army and joint sustainment capabilities, concepts, and doctrine to
support large-scale combat operations, paving the way for a more agile, lethal, and modern force.

                              Introduction

ADP 4-0 defines how the elements of sustainment (logistics, financial management, personnel services, and
health service support) ensure operational success by enabling freedom of action, extending operational
reach, and prolonging endurance. It serves as the Army’s doctrine for sustainment during operations and
describes how sustainment contributes to unified action in contested environments. Sustainment ensures that
Army and joint forces maintain tempo and effectiveness during operations, supporting maneuver forces
across air, land, sea, space, and cyberspace. By integrating sustainment functions at all levels of warfare,
commanders can sustain operations until mission accomplishment.

Sustainment is inherently joint, as it depends on strategic and theater-level supply chains, joint strategic lift
capabilities (air and sea), and host-nation infrastructure to ensure forces receive the necessary support at the
right time and place. Joint interdependence allows combatant commanders to maximize the Army’s
sustainment capabilities within the operational environment, ensuring both maneuver forces and follow-on
sustainment operations remain effective. Success of joint operations occur through constant coordination and
collaboration with unified action partners. Army sustainment is enhanced by joint interdependence and
inclusion of unified action partners.

A mission command approach to command and control enables sustainment commanders and their staffs to
operate effectively in a decentralized and complex environment. Through command and control, sustainment
commanders can support joint campaigns across multiple areas of responsibility. Command and control
ensures the delivery of sustainment in the appropriate quantity and quality to maintain operational success.
ADP 4-0 provides sustainment commanders and leaders with guiding principles and a structured framework
for sustainment operations, ensuring that operations and sustainment remain seamlessly linked.

ADP 4-0 contains three chapters:

Chapter 1 discusses the sustainment warfighting function and the four elements of sustainment: logistics,
financial management, personnel services, and health service support. This chapter lays out the fundamental
principles governing each of these essential elements.

Chapter 2 describes the important roles that the U.S. military and unified action partners play during the
sustainment of Army forces. It describes joint interdependence and how sustaining the Army and joint forces
are linked. It also describes interorganizational cooperation and multinational operations in providing
sustainment.

Chapter 3 discusses sustainment during multidomain operations and continues with a description of
command and control and mission command of sustainment forces. This chapter concludes with describing
how sustainment enables freedom of action, extends operational reach, and prolongs endurance of combat
forces.

The sustainment warfighting function and its elements are depicted in the logic chart. The chart emphasizes
the central idea of unified action and defines multidomain operations as the Army’s primary operational
approach. It provides a visual representation of the strategic contexts in which forces operate, highlighting
how the sustainment warfighting function’s capabilities are intrinsically joint in nature. The chart also
demonstrates the function’s reliance on other Services’ capabilities to effectively support unified action. The
chart concludes by portraying how sustainment supports the Army strategic roles by adhering to the
foundational sustainment principles that enable freedom of action, extend operational reach, and prolong
endurance necessary to accomplish missions, consolidate gains, and win our nation’s wars.

                                                 Chapter 1

                     Fundamentals of Sustainment

    This chapter begins with a discussion of the sustainment warfighting function and its elements.
    It then covers the principles of sustainment and the principles of each element that makes up
    the sustainment warfighting function.

SUSTAINMENT WARFIGHTING FUNCTION

     1-1. The sustainment warfighting function is one of the six warfighting functions: command and control,
     movement and maneuver, intelligence, fires, sustainment, and protection. The sustainment warfighting
     function is the related tasks and systems that provide support and services to enable freedom of action, extend
     operational reach, and prolong endurance (ADP 3-0). Successful sustainment enables freedom of action,
     extends operational reach, and prolongs endurance by increasing the number of options available to the
     commander. Sustainment is essential for retaining and exploiting the initiative. The sustainment warfighting
     function consists of four elements: logistics, financial management, personnel services, and health service
     support.

LOGISTICS

     1-2. Logistics is planning and executing the movement and support of forces. It includes those aspects
     of military operations that deal with: design and development, acquisition, storage, movement,
     distribution, maintenance, evacuation, and disposition of materiel; acquisition or construction,
     maintenance, operation, and disposition of facilities; and acquisition or furnishing of services. Army
     logistics elements are maintenance, transportation, supply, field services, distribution, operational contract
     support, and general engineering support. Explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) tasks support the sustainment
     and protection warfighting functions. For additional details, see FM 4-0.

     1-3. Maintenance is the logistics function that directly provides equipment serviceability and operational
     readiness to commanders for mission accomplishment. Maintenance occurs at every level of operations and
     is performed by operators, assigned maintenance technicians, or by maintenance units. For more information
     on maintenance operations see FM 4-0, ATP 4-31, ATP 4-33, and ATP 4-98.

     1-4. Transportation operations include movement control and associated activities to incorporate military,
     commercial, and multinational motor, rail, air, and water mode assets in the movement of units, personnel,
     equipment, and supplies in support of the concept of operations. These operations encompass fort-to-port,
     port-to-port, port-to-end user, and return movements. Army transportation supports theater distribution and
     maneuver units, requiring integration with joint and strategic partners. For more information on
     transportation operations see FM 4-0, ATP 4-11, ATP 4-12, ATP 4-13, ATP 4-14, ATP 4-15, and ATP 4-16.

     1-5. Supply is the process of providing items necessary to equip, maintain, and operate a military command
     (FM 1-02.1). It involves requesting, receiving, issuing, and maintaining or establishing accountability of
     individual, organizational, and expendable/durable supplies and equipment required to execute a unit’s
     assigned mission. For more information see FM 4-0, ATP 4-0.6, ATP 4-35, and ATP 4-42.

     1-6. Field services include aerial delivery, food service, shower and laundry, mortuary affairs, and
     water purification. These services enhance unit effectiveness and mission success by providing for
     Soldier basic needs. Field services are performed at the theater strategic, operational, and tactical levels.
     Field services are essential to supporting forces throughout the entire spectrum of operations during
     competition, crisis, and conflict. For more information see FM 4-0, ATP 4-41, ATP 4-42, ATP 4-46,
     ATP 4-48, and ATP 4-25.12.

     1-7. In the Army, liquid logistics operations cross both the supply and field services logistics elements
     captured in water support and petroleum supply operations. Water support operations consist of treatment,
     storage, distribution, and issue of potable and non-potable water in a theater of operations. Petroleum supply
     operations are integrated processes that link the operational requirements of petroleum products to the
     sustainment capabilities required to support fuel demands. They focus on when, where, and how to provide

     1-8. Distribution is the operational process of synchronizing all elements of the logistics system to deliver

FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT

     1-12. Financial management is an element of the sustainment warfighting function that leverages fiscal
     policy and economic power across operations through the two core competencies of finance operations and
     resource management (FM 4-80).

     1-13. Financial management has a total of ten primary functions. Disbursing, banking, and payment support
     specifically pertain to finance operations. Programming, budget formulation, budget distribution, and budget
     execution specifically pertain to resource management. Accounting, internal controls, and financial data
     analytics are overarching functions executed in both finance operations and resource management.

     1-14. Financial management operations have transformed its units to better support the combatant
     commander by integrating all financial management capabilities across theater and each area of support. The
     financial management mission is to analyze resource requirements, ensure commanders are aware of existing
     resource implications for them to make resource-informed decisions, and then obtain the necessary funding
     that allows the commander to accomplish the overall unit mission. For additional information, see FM 4-80.

PERSONNEL SERVICES

     1-15. Personnel services are sustainment functions that man and fund the force, maintain Soldier and
     family readiness, promote the moral and ethical values of the nation, and enable the fighting qualities
     of the Army. Personnel services support is the management and execution of personnel-related functions
     including human resources, legal, religious, and Army band support. For additional details, see the
     publications below:

          • Human Resources (FM 4-1).
          • Army Bands (FM 4-1, ATP 1-19).
          • Legal Support to Operations (FM 3-84).
          • Religious Affairs (FM 3-83).

     1-16. Human resources is a critical part of determining current combat capabilities and projecting future
     requirements to sustain or build combat power throughout all phases of large-scale combat operations.
     Success on the battlefield will depend, in part, on the effective execution of human resource competencies.
     Human resources support includes all activities and functions executed within the eight Army personnel
     development system life cycle management functions (structure, acquisition, distribution, development,
     deployment, compensation, sustainment, and transition). These functions man the force and provide human
     resources support and services to Soldiers, their Families, military retirees, and DOD Civilians. The
     principles of human resources support are nested within the personnel services element of the sustainment
     warfighting function since personnel services and human resources support are often interrelated and
     interdependent functions.

     1-17. Army bands provide support to the force by tailoring music support through military operations. Music
     instills in Soldiers the will to fight and win, fosters the support of America’s citizens, and promotes its
     interests at home and abroad. Music serves as a useful tool to reinforce relations with host-nation populations
     and favorably shapes the civil situation throughout the peace-building process. Inherently capable of
     providing a climate for international relations, Army bands serve as ambassadors in multinational operations
     or to the host-nation population. Army Bands also play a significant role in providing casualty management
     operations augmentation support to human resources capabilities. For additional information see FM 4-1 and
     ATP 1-19.

     1-18. Legal support is a critical element of the Army’s success, and it enables the good order and discipline
     that is the hallmark of enduring institutions, great units, and successful operations. A disciplined and fair
     environment is necessary for sustaining an Army culture centered on building trust between Soldiers who
     can then focus on accomplishing missions. This Army culture extends to families and military communities
     where trust and cohesion are essential to the long-term success of individuals, units, and institutions. For
     more information see FM 3-84. Judge advocate legal services personnel follow four guiding principles in the
     delivery of legal services to the Army and in joint operations:

          • Principled counsel.
          • Stewardship.
          • Mastery of the law.
          • Servant leadership.

     1-19. The mission of the Army Chaplain Corps is to execute religious affairs to the Army across the range of
     military operations by assisting the commander in providing for the free exercise of religion and providing
     religious, moral, ethical, and morale advisement and leadership (from DODI 1304.28). The term “provide”
     is understood as either personal delivery or coordination of required support from other chaplain sources. For
     additional information see FM 3-83.

HEALTH SERVICE SUPPORT

     1-20. Health service support is the support and services performed, provided, and arranged by Army
     Medicine to promote, improve, conserve, or restore the behavioral and physical well-being of personnel by
     providing direct patient care that includes medical treatment (organic and area support) and hospitalization,
     medical evacuation to include medical regulating, and medical logistics to include blood management
     (FM 4-02). Army health service support is a critical capability embedded within formations across all
     warfighting functions. For additional information, see FM 4-02.

     1-21. The Army Health System is a component of the Military Health System that is responsible for
     operational management of the health service support and force health protection missions for training,
     predeployment, deployment, and postdeployment operations. The Army Health System includes all mission
     support services performed, provided, or arranged by the Army Medicine to support health service support
     and force health protection mission requirements for the Army and as directed, for joint, intergovernmental
     agencies, coalition, and multinational forces (FM 4-02). The force health protection mission falls under the
     protection warfighting function and will not be covered in detail in this publication. For information on force
     health protection, see ADP 3-37 and ATP 4-02.8. For a complete description of the Army Health System
     (AHS), see FM 4-02.

PRINCIPLES OF SUSTAINMENT

     1-23. The principles of sustainment shown in figure 1-1 are essential to enabling freedom of action, extending

INTEGRATION

     1-24. Integration is combining all of the sustainment elements within operations assuring unity of
     command and effort. It requires deliberate coordination of sustainment with operations across units at all

     operations to maximize the complementary and reinforcing effects across the Army, Service components,
     and other unified action partner resources. One of the primary functions of the sustainment staff is to ensure
     the holistic integration of sustainment capabilities in plans and operations.

SYNCHRONIZATION

     1-25. Synchronization is the arrangement of military actions in time, space, and purpose to produce maximum
     relative combat power at a decisive place and time (JP 2-0). Plans and orders link sustainment actions across
     the warfighting functions and establish the appropriate sequencing of events, units, or actions in support of
     plans and operations. Nesting sustainment support along multiple lines of effort creates opportunities to
     synchronize warfighting.

ANTICIPATION

     1-26. Anticipation is the ability to foresee events and requirements and initiate necessary actions that
     most appropriately satisfy a response without waiting for operation orders or fragmentary orders. It
     is shaped by professional judgment based on experience, knowledge, education, intelligence, and intuition.
     Commanders and staff must understand and visualize future operations and identify appropriate or required
     support. They must then start the process of acquiring the resources and capabilities that best support the
     operation. Anticipation is facilitated by predictive sustainment and data-driven information tools that provide
     the common operational picture (COP) and enable decision dominance and agility in action. Commanders
     and staff use the principle of anticipation to integrate risk management into the operations process by
     identifying anticipated hazards, assessing those hazards, and implementing controls to mitigate identified
     risks. For more information on the risk management process, see ATP 5-19.

RESPONSIVENESS

     1-27. Responsiveness is the ability to react to changing requirements and respond to meet the needs to
     maintain support. It provides the right support in the right place at the right time. It includes the ability to
     anticipate operational requirements. Responsiveness is informed by the COP, including information from
     Army enterprise resource planning systems and other DOD information systems. That COP enables
     commanders to see all supported forces, anticipate requirements based on situational understanding, and
     provide support when and where needed. Responsiveness involves identifying, accumulating, and
     maintaining sufficient resources, capabilities, and information necessary to meet rapidly changing
     requirements. Responsiveness is increased when sustainment forces utilize local production of supplies,
     autonomous distribution, and prepositioned sustainment nodes. Through responsive sustainment,
     commanders maintain operational focus and pressure, set the tempo of friendly operations to prevent
     exhaustion, replace ineffective units, and extend operational reach.

SIMPLICITY

     1-28. Simplicity relates to processes and procedures to minimize the complexity of sustainment.
     Simplicity in sustainment refers to minimizing the complexity of processes and procedures. Unnecessary
     complexity leads to confusion, while clear tasks, standardized and interoperable procedures, and clearly
     defined command relationships promote efficiency. Simplicity enables economy and efficiency in the use of
     resources, while ensuring effective support of forces.

ECONOMY

     1-29. Economy is providing sustainment resources in an efficient manner that enables the commander
     to employ all assets to the greatest effect possible. Economy is achieved through efficient management,
     discipline, prioritization, and allocation of resources. Economy is further achieved by eliminating
     redundancies and capitalizing on joint interdependencies. Disciplined sustainment assures the greatest
     possible tactical endurance and constitutes an advantage to commanders. Economy may be achieved by
     contracting for support or using host-nation resources that reduce or eliminate the use of limited military
     resources. By efficiently and ethically managing Army resources, Army professionals are stewards who act
     in the best interest of the American people.

SURVIVABILITY

     1-30. Survivability is all aspects of protecting personnel, weapons, and supplies while simultaneously
     deceiving the enemy (JP 3-34). Survivability consists of a quality or capability of military forces to avoid or
     withstand hostile actions or environmental conditions while retaining the ability to fulfill their primary
     mission. Agility is integral to survivability during large-scale combat operations. This quality or capability

CONTINUITY

     1-31. Continuity is the uninterrupted provision of sustainment across all levels of war. Continuity is

     combat operations. This ensures continuity of sustainment in contested logistics environments. Continuity

     endurance.

IMPROVISATION

     1-32. Improvisation is the ability to adapt sustainment operations to unexpected situations or
     circumstances affecting a mission. It includes creating, arranging, or fabricating resources to meet
     requirements. It may also involve changing or creating methods that adapt to a changing operational
     environment (OE). Sustainment leaders must apply operational art to visualize complex operations and
     understand additional possibilities. These skills enable commanders to improvise operational and tactical
     actions when enemy actions or unexpected events disrupt sustainment operations.

PRINCIPLES OF FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT

     1-33. Financial management operations are guided by fundamental principles that apply in large-scale
     combat operations as well as any other operation along the conflict continuum. The six financial management
     principles are adherence to fiscal law, timeliness, audit readiness, consistency, accuracy, and synchronization.
     These principles provide broad and enduring guidelines for the execution of the Finance Corps’ role and are
     the foundation upon which the core competencies and functions are built.

     1-34. Although independent of one another, these principles must be integrated in the planning and execution
     of financial management operations and synchronized in time, space, and purpose. The following paragraphs
     briefly describe the principles that enable finance elements to meet the commander’s mission.

ADHERENCE TO FISCAL LAW

     1-35. Adherence to fiscal law is understanding and complying with relevant laws, regulations, and policies
     during the execution of fiscal resources. It is critical for financial managers to understand fiscal law for two

     mission if it does not possess the necessary funding authority or funds. Second, spending funds
     inappropriately can result in punitive or adverse administrative action against a commander. Therefore, it is
     imperative for organizations to have a foundational understanding of the legally permissible way to spend
     appropriated funds.

     solatium payments, non-combatant evacuation relocation assistance, and humanitarian relief efforts). Timely

                     Sustainment in Unified Action

    This chapter discusses unified action, the OE, the Army strategic contexts, and sustainment
    support to joint campaigns and operations. Sustainment requires full integration of the U.S.
    military with the efforts of interorganizational and multinational partners to achieve strategic,
    operational, and tactical success.

UNIFIED ACTION

     2-1. Unified action is the synchronization, coordination, or integration of the activities of governmental and
     nongovernmental entities with military operations to achieve unity of effort (JP 1, Volume 1). It starts with
     national strategic direction reflecting the whole-of-government approach that focuses on cooperation of the
     U.S. military and other interorganizational participants toward common objectives, even though the
     participants are not part of the same command or organization. Achieving unified action can be challenging
     in contested environments due to the complex interplay of multiple actors, diverse interests, and dynamic
     conditions. Successful unified action requires persistent interorganizational cooperation between unified
     action partners, such as the Department of State and intelligence agencies, to align efforts toward a common
     goal. During sustainment operations, the principle of integration enhances decision making efficiency by
     leveraging joint and multinational capabilities. Integration with unified action partners maximizes the
     complementary and reinforcing effects of each Service component’s and other unified action partners’
     resources. This cooperation is essential, as failure to achieve unity of effort can jeopardize mission success.
     For additional information on unified action, see JP 1, Volume 1, JP 3-0, FM 3-0, and FM 4-0.

SUSTAINMENT SUPPORT TO JOINT CAMPAIGNS AND
OPERATIONS

     2-2. Sustainment support to joint campaigns and operations is about enabling and maintaining the overall
     effort. The Army is a globally engaged, regionally responsive force that provides its full range of capabilities,
     which are essential for sustaining joint campaigns to combatant commanders in four primary roles. Army
     forces shape OEs, counter aggression during crisis, prevail during large-scale combat operations, and
     consolidate gains on land for the joint force. To support these roles, the Army relies on a robust network of
     joint and strategic capabilities. This network facilitates critical functions such as strategic airlift, sealift,
     intratheater air and sea lift, and supply support at both national strategic and theater strategic levels of warfare.
     Campaigning is the persistent conduct of related operations, activities, and investments that align military
     actions with the other instruments of national power, supporting global integration across the competition
     continuum in pursuit of strategic objectives (JP 3-0).

     2-3. The sustainment warfighting function primarily contributes to joint force campaigning with port
     opening, theater opening, theater distribution, and sustainment functions, and provides lead Service and
     executive agent activities for designated common-user logistics to interorganizational and unified action
     partners as directed. The theater sustainment command is a theater enabling command that connects strategic
     enablers to tactical formations. It integrates and synchronizes sustainment support to Army and mission
     partners during military operations and is a tailorable headquarters, task-organized to support joint operations
     and command subordinate sustainment organizations. It can be augmented as necessary with a combination
     of combat sustainment support units and functional logistics units based on the mission. For additional
     information see ATP 4-93.

SUSTAINMENT IN MULTINATIONAL OPERATIONS

     2-20. Unity of effort in multinational operations requires coordination of logistics operations not only
     between Services and United States Government departments and agencies, but also among relief and
     humanitarian organizations in theater. A major objective in the sustainment of multinational operations is to
     maximize operational effectiveness through interoperability. Interoperability is the ability to act together
     coherently, effectively, and efficiently to achieve tactical, operational, and strategic objectives (JP 3-0).
     Support provided and received in multinational operations must be in accordance with existing legal
     authorities. Multinational operations are usually undertaken with the structure of a coalition or alliance. For
     additional information, see FM 3-16.

     2-21. In multinational operations, sustainment is primarily a national responsibility. However, relations
     between the United States and its North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) allies have evolved to where
     sustainment is viewed as a collective responsibility (NATO Military Committee Decision 319/1). In
     multinational operations, the multinational commander must have sufficient authority and control
     mechanisms over assets, resources, and forces to effectively achieve mission objectives. Multinational
     commanders consider each MNF capabilities and the desired level of interoperability during planning.
     Nations that perform their own, separate sustainment functions are inefficient and expensive and may not be
     interoperable with other nations. This hinders the multinational commander's ability to influence and
     prioritize limited resources to support the operation and accomplish the mission.

     2-22. Lead nation is the nation with the will, capability, competence, and influence to provide the essential
     elements of political consultation and military leadership to coordinate the planning, mounting, and execution
     of a multinational operation (JP 3-16). The lead nation concept is one that sees one nation providing the
     framework for one or more logistics functions to support the MNF. For additional information, see JP 3-16

     agreement between the parties involved. For additional information see JP 3-16.

HOST-NATION SUPPORT

     2-24. Host-nation support is civil and military assistance rendered by a nation to foreign forces within its
     territory during peacetime, crises or emergencies, or war based on agreements mutually concluded between
     nations. Many host-nation support agreements exist between partner, coalition and NATO nations. Potential
     host-nation support agreements may address labor support arrangements for port and terminal operations,
     using available transportation assets in country, using bulk petroleum distribution and storage facilities,
     possible supply of class III (Bulk) and class IV items, and developing and using field services. The United
     States initiates and continually evaluates agreements with multinational partners for improvement. They
     should be specifically designed to enable planners to adjust to specified requirements. Additionally, the
     commander should assess the risk associated with using host-nation support, considering operational area
     security and operational requirements. For additional information see FM 3-16.

ACQUISITION AND CROSS-SERVICING AGREEMENT

     2-25. Under ACSA authority (Title 10 United States Code, sections 2341 and 2342), the Secretary of Defense
     can enter into ACSAs for logistical support, supplies, and services on a reimbursable, replacement-in-kind,
     or exchange-for-equal-value basis. These agreements can be with eligible nations and international
     organizations of which the United States is a member. Under specific circumstances, these agreements may
     be entered into with non-member nations (10 United States Code, section 2342). An ACSA is a broad overall
     agreement, which is generally supplemented with an implementing agreement. Implementing agreements
     contain points of contact and specific details of the transaction and payment procedures for orders for logistics
     support. Neither party is obligated until the order is accepted.

     2-26. Under these agreements, common support may include food, billeting, transportation (including airlift),

     operations, storage services, use of facilities, training services, spare parts and components, repair and
     maintenance services, calibration services, and port services. Items that may not be acquired or transferred

     additional information, see JP 3-16 and FM 3-16.

     2-33. Participating nations have the option to develop mutual support arrangements (bi- and multi-laterally)

COMMAND STRUCTURES FOR MULTINATIONAL OPERATIONS

     2-35. All multinational operations have two chains of command regardless of structure or authority. The first
     is the multinational chain of command constructed by the United Nations, alliance, or coalition. The second
     chain of command is a national chain of command extending back to the national capitals of the nations
     involved. The United Nations has three types of operations:

          • Operations under the command of the United Nations reported to the Secretary General. The
                Security Council sets the mandate for these operations in a resolution. The Secretary General
                manages and supervises the execution of the mandate.

          • Operations conducted by a coalition of willing states authorized by the United Nations. The coalition
                remains under the command of a lead state or regional organization and reports to its national or
                alliance chain of command. The Security Council that allows the coalition to achieve its specified
                mission authorizes these operations.

          • Hybrid operations. The United Nations force operates with a force from another country without
                any formal command and control.

     2-36. Alliances, United Nations forces, and coalitions create a command structure that meets the needs,
     diplomatic realities, constraints, and objectives of the participating nations. Since no single command
     structure fits the needs of all alliances and coalitions, several different command structures evolved:

          • Lead nation.
          • Parallel.
          • Integrated.
          • Combination.

MULTIDOMAIN OPERATIONS

     3-1. Multidomain operations are the combined arms employment of joint and Army capabilities to create and
     exploit relative advantages to achieve objectives, defeat enemy forces, and consolidate gains on behalf of
     joint force commanders (ADP 3-0). The sustainment warfighting function enables operations by providing
     the support required to keep the Army and its unified action partners engaged in operations across the Army’s
     strategic contexts. During competition, sustainment forces enable operations to demonstrate readiness for
     conflict, deterring adversaries while assuring allies and partners. In crisis situations, sustainment enables
     operations by providing Army forces the freedom of action, operational reach, and endurance required to
     disrupt the enemy’s decision making, stabilize situations, and demonstrate preparedness for any transition
     that may occur. During operations, sustainment forces support the employment of combat power to achieve
     national objectives while managing vast amounts of sustainment data, requiring a common data repository
     and automation for predictive analysis. For additional information, see ADP 3-0, FM 3-0, and FM 4-0.

     3-2. Sustainment operations are inherently joint. Joint and multinational capabilities enable sustainment
     forces to project combat power, and Army capabilities enable joint and MNFs to achieve objectives. The
     interrelationship of the air, land, maritime, space, and cyberspace domains requires a shared understanding
     of an OE. Sustainment commanders and staff must understand the friendly and enemy capabilities that reside
     in each domain and their potential impacts on sustainment operations. To generate the complementary and
     reinforcing effects necessary to create and exploit relative advantages, leaders must understand the
     interdependence between their own assigned capabilities and the capabilities provided by others.

     3-3. Distributed sustainment operations will be challenged across all domains during operations. In such an
     environment, sustainment decisions must be made quickly and precisely. Precision sustainment is the
     effective delivery of the right capabilities at the point of employment, enabling commander's freedom
     of action, extending operational reach, and prolonging endurance. Precision sustainment is enabled by
     predictive sustainment. Predictive sustainment is a system of sensors, communications, and applications
     (data support tools and data visualization) that enables quicker and more accurate sustainment
     decision making at echelon from tactical to strategic. Predictive sustainment enables decision dominance
     and greater precision and speed with running estimates and course of action development. It provides options
     for commanders and planners to know precisely where and when to prioritize support to ensure the right
     commodities go to the unit that most needs them for success. Precision sustainment, enabled by predictive
     sustainment, is key to delivering the complementary and reinforcing effects at the point of employment.
     Predictive sustainment provides commanders with data and information they can leverage for information
     advantages that contribute to achieving decision dominance. The Army Unified Network Plan is essential in
     enabling the precision sustainment and decision dominance required to support operations. Precision
     sustainment contributes to achieving decision dominance by supporting the commander’s ability to quickly
     and efficiently make decisions that enhance projection of combat power and command and control. Sustainers
     must become accustomed to making decisions at echelon using real-time data received via predictive
     sustainment sensors and systems.

     3-4. Precision sustainment is the goal for optimizing resources during operations. Precision sustainment
     integrates sustainment data and information that can be used at all echelons. It also provides anticipation and
     responsiveness for sustainment and operational planners to react to supply and maintenance trends. Precision
     sustainment also employs the principle of economy and ensures sustainment resources are provided in the
     most efficient manner to enable employment of assets to the greatest effect possible. For additional
     information, see FM 4-0.

OPERATIONAL ENVIRONMENT

     3-8. The operational environment is the aggregate of the conditions, circumstances, and influences that affect

     mission variables to understand and maintain military engagements and the ability to rapidly transition from
     one strategic context to another. Understanding the specific OE in each situation is essential to successful
     execution of sustainment operations conducted in support of combatant commander objectives. For
     additional information see JP 3-0, FM 3-0, and FM 4-0.

     sector planning and training to synchronize all aspects of sustainment in a U.S. response to humanitarian and
     complex crises. Interagency training and training with non-United States Government stakeholders should
     provide for individual military and civilian instruction, military unit and civilian agency instruction, and
     combined military and civilian agency training in a formal joint program. See FM 4-0 for additional
     information on training sustainment formations and leader development.

Glossary

campaigning
          The persistent conduct of related operations, activities, and investments that align military actions with
          the other instruments of national power, supporting global integration across the competition
          continuum in pursuit of strategic objectives. (JP 3-0)

consolidate gains
          Activities to make enduring any temporary operational success and to set the conditions for a

          (ADP 3-0)

*continuity
          The uninterrupted provision of sustainment across all levels of war.

*economy
          Providing sustainment resources in an efficient manner that enables the commander to employ all
          assets to the greatest effect possible.

endurance
          The ability to persevere over time throughout the depth of an operational environment. (FM 3-0)

*field services
          Includes aerial delivery, food service, shower and laundry, mortuary affairs, and water purification.
          These services enhance unit effectiveness and mission success by providing for Soldier basic needs.

financial management
          (Army) An element of the sustainment warfighting function that leverages fiscal policy and economic
          power across operations through the two core competencies of finance operations and resource
          management. (FM 4-80)

general engineering
          Those engineering capabilities and activities, other than combat engineering, that provide
          infrastructure and modify, maintain, or protect the physical environment. (JP 3-34)

health service support
          (Army) The support and services performed, provided, and arranged by Army Medicine to promote,
          improve, conserve, or restore the behavioral and physical well-being of personnel by providing direct
          patient care that includes medical treatment (organic and area support) and hospitalization, medical
          evacuation to include medical regulating, and medical logistics to include blood management.
          (FM 4-02)

*improvisation
          The ability to adapt sustainment operations to unexpected situations or circumstances affecting a
          mission.

information environment
          The aggregate of social, cultural, linguistic, psychological, technical, and physical factors that affect
          how humans and automated systems derive meaning from, act upon, and are impacted by information,
          including the individuals, organizations, and systems that collect, process, disseminate, or use
          information. (JP 3-04)

*integration
          Combining all of the sustainment elements within operations assuring unity of command and effort.

interagency coordination
          The planning and synchronization of efforts that occur between elements of Department of Defense
          and participating United States Government departments and agencies. (JP 3-0)

interoperability
          The ability to act together coherently, effectively, and efficiently to achieve tactical, operational, and
          strategic objectives. (JP 3-0)

                                                                                                                                          Glossary

interorganizational cooperation
          The interaction that occurs among elements of the Department of Defense; participating United States
          Government departments and agencies; state, territorial, local, and tribal agencies; foreign military
          forces and government agencies; international organizations; nongovernmental organizations; and the
          private sector. (JP 3-08)

large-scale combat operations
          Extensive joint combat operations in terms of scope and size of forces committed, conducted as a
          campaign aimed at achieving operational and strategic objectives. (ADP 3-0)

lead nation
          The nation with the will, capability, competence, and influence to provide the essential elements of
          political consultation and military leadership to coordinate the planning, mounting, and execution of a
          multinational operation. (JP 3-16)

*logistics
          (Army) Planning and executing the movement and support of forces. It includes those aspects of
          military operations that deal with: design and development, acquisition, storage, movement,
          distribution, maintenance, evacuation, and disposition of materiel; acquisition or construction,
          maintenance, operation, and disposition of facilities; and acquisition or furnishing of services.

mission command
          (Army) The Army’s approach to command and control that empowers subordinate decision making
          and decentralized execution appropriate to the situation. (ADP 6-0)

multidomain operations
          The combined arms employment of joint and Army capabilities to create and exploit relative
          advantages to achieve objectives, defeat enemy forces, and consolidate gains on behalf of joint force
          commanders. (ADP 3-0)

multinational integrated logistic unit
          An organization resulting when two or more nations agree to provide logistics assets to a multinational
          logistic force under the operational control of a multinational commander for the logistic support of a
          multinational force. (JP 3-16)

national support element
          Any national organization or activity that supports national forces that are a part of a multinational
          force. (JP 1, Volume 2)

nongovernmental organization
          A private, self-governing, not-for-profit organization dedicated to alleviating human suffering; and/or
          promoting education, health care, economic development, environmental protection, human rights, and
          conflict resolution; and/or encouraging the establishment of democratic institutions and civil society.
          (JP 3-08)

operational contract support
          The process of planning for and obtaining supplies, services, and construction from commercial
          sources in support of combatant commander-directed operations. (JP 4-10)

operational environment
          The aggregate of the conditions, circumstances, and influences that affect the employment of
          capabilities and bear on the decisions of the commander. (JP 3-0)

operational reach
          The distance and duration across which a force can successfully employ military capabilities. (JP 3-0)

*personnel services
          Sustainment functions that man and fund the force, maintain Soldier and family readiness, promote the
          moral and ethical values of the nation, and enable the fighting qualities of the Army.

Glossary

*precision sustainment
          The effective delivery of the right capabilities at the point of employment, enabling commander’s
          freedom of action, extending operational reach, and prolonging endurance.

*predictive sustainment
          A system of sensors, communications, and applications (data support tools and data visualization) that
          enables quicker and more accurate sustainment decision making at echelon from tactical to strategic.

*responsiveness
          The ability to react to changing requirements and respond to meet the needs to maintain support.

setting the theater
          The broad range of activities continuously conducted to establish conditions for the successful
          execution of operations in a theater. (ADP 3-0)

*simplicity
          Relates to processes and procedures to minimize the complexity of sustainment.

survivability
          (joint) All aspects of protecting personnel, weapons, and supplies while simultaneously deceiving the
          enemy. (JP 3-34)

*sustainment preparation of the operational environment
          The analysis to determine infrastructure, physical environment, and resources in the operational
          environment that will optimize or adversely impact friendly forces means for supporting and sustaining
          the commander’s operations plan.

sustainment warfighting function
          The related tasks and systems that provide support and services to enable freedom of action, extend
          operational reach, and prolong endurance. (ADP 3-0)

synchronization
          The arrangement of military actions in time, space, and purpose to produce maximum relative combat
          power at a decisive place and time. (JP 2-0)

transportation
          A logistics function that includes movement control and associated activities to incorporate military,
          commercial, and multinational motor, rail, air, and water mode assets in the movement of units,
          personnel, equipment, and supplies in support of the concept of operations. (FM 1-02.1)

unified action
          The synchronization, coordination, or integration of the activities of governmental and
          nongovernmental entities with military operations to achieve unity of effort. (JP 1, Volume 1).

unified action partners
          Those military forces, governmental and nongovernmental organizations, and elements of the private
          sector with whom Army forces plan, coordinate, synchronize, and integrate during the conduct of
          operations. (ADP 3-0)
