                                 Preface

TC 7-0.3, Plan an Event, provides Army leaders the fundamental processes and procedures to plan and
conduct training events. Users of this training circular (TC) must familiarize themselves with Army training
doctrine as described in ADP 7-0 and FM 7-0.

The principal audience for TC 7-0.3 is all members of the profession of arms. Commanders and staffs of
Army headquarters serving as joint task force or multinational headquarters should also refer to applicable
joint or multinational doctrine concerning the range of military operations and joint or multinational forces.
Trainers and educators throughout the Army will also use this publication.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure that their decisions and actions comply with applicable United
States, international, and in some cases host-nation laws and regulations. Commanders at all levels ensure
that their Soldiers operate in accordance with the law of war and the rules of engagement. (See FM 6-27).

TC 7-0.3 uses joint terms where applicable. Selected joint and Army terms and definitions appear in both the
glossary and the text. For definitions shown in the text, the term is italicized, and the number of the proponent
publication follows the definition.

TC 7-0.3 applies to the Active Army, Army National Guard/Army National Guard of the United States and
United States Army Reserve unless otherwise stated.

The proponent of TC 7-0.3 is the United States Army Combined Arms Center. The preparing agency is the
Training Management Directorate, United States Army Combined Arms Center. Send comments and

                              Introduction

Army training forms the foundation of unit readiness, emphasizing mission-essential tasks and operational
effectiveness. TC 7-0.3, Plan an Event, outlines key frameworks for planning training activities, including
lane training, the 8-step training model, and Troop Leading Procedures (TLP). It provides leaders with
practical strategies to efficiently plan, execute, and evaluate training events at various levels. This TC further
elaborates on the training concepts introduced in the Army’s training doctrine, ADP 7-0 and FM 7-0.

TC 7-0.3 contains two chapters:

Chapter 1 offers a comprehensive and detailed methodology for planning training events, aimed at
enhancing coordination, skill development, and overall effectiveness. From individual tasks to collective
operations, every activity is aligned with the commander’s training guidance, leveraging structured models
and resource-efficient methods to achieve optimal outcomes. This chapter explores the processes and
principles of planning training events, emphasizing platoon-level planning and execution strategies.

Chapter 2 provides detailed planning for lane training, a structured approach to improving unit proficiency
in essential tasks within a controlled environment. Lane training emphasizes repeated practice, feedback, and
retraining to efficiently meet tactical and technical standards. This chapter highlights its principles,
advantages, and practical applications, with a focus on platoon-level planning to optimize training outcome.

TC 7-0.3 contains two appendices:

Appendix A outlines the 8-step training model, a flexible framework for planning and executing small-unit
training events efficiently while ensuring key activities are completed and adaptable to unit needs.

Appendix B outlines Army troop leading procedures, a structured detailed 8-step process used by small-unit
leaders to efficiently plan, prepare, and execute training while translating the commander’s intent into
actionable tasks.

                                                 Chapter 1

                         Training Event Planning

    Army training events serve as the cornerstone for developing unit readiness, ensuring that
    soldiers are proficient in mission-critical tasks and equipped to succeed in diverse operational
    scenarios. These events are meticulously planned and executed, following established
    frameworks and methodologies to enhance coordination, skills, and overall effectiveness. From
    individual tasks to collective operations, each activity is designed to align with the
    commander’s training guidance, utilizing structured models and resource-efficient methods to
    achieve optimal results. This chapter explores the processes and principles guiding training
    events, with an emphasis on platoon-level planning and execution strategies.

OVERVIEW

     1-1. Army training events are structured activities aimed at enhancing the skills, coordination, and overall
     readiness of a unit. These events focus on collective tasks critical to the unit's mission, including mission
     essential tasks (MET), tactical tasks, battle tasks, weapons training, battle drills, and communication
     exercises. The primary objective is to ensure that the unit functions as a cohesive and effective entity in
     scenarios ranging from combat operations to other mission requirements.

     1-2. Training events are typically guided by the Army's operations process, which encompasses planning,
     preparation, execution, and assessment phases. Leaders prioritize tasks, allocate necessary resources, and
     conduct after action reviews (AAR) to evaluate performance and identify areas for improvement.

     1-3. Leaders begin the planning process several months before execution to ensure that the resources required
     for effective training are secured ahead of time. This process involves coordination and support from the
     company, battalion, and higher echelons. Training events are integrated into the organization’s unit training
     plan (UTP) and are aligned with the commander's training guidance.

     1-4. Training events at the platoon and lower echelons typically demand fewer resources compared to those
     conducted at the company level or higher. A highly recommended method for platoon training is Lane
     Training, which is resource-efficient and facility-light. This technique centers on limited tasks performed
     within a controlled environment, enabling soldiers to practice repeatedly until they achieve proficiency.
     Chapter 2 details the process of planning and conducting lane training.

     1-5. While the 8-Step Training Model serves as a valuable checklist to ensure that key planning actions for
     major training events are completed, it lacks the necessary detail to fully plan and prepare small unit training
     events. However, squads and teams may still find the 8-Step Training Model beneficial for their needs. (Refer
     to Appendix A for a detailed description of the model).

PLATOON TRAINING EVENT PLANNING

     1-6. This chapter emphasizes a comprehensive sequence of actions and activities that leaders can follow at
     the platoon level to plan and execute effective training events:

          1. Dialogue with the Commander: Communicate with the commander to ensure alignment with
                training goals and directives.

          2. Determine Event Requirements: Identify the objectives, resources, and personnel needed for the
                event.

          3. Iterate and Develop an Event Concept: Create a detailed outline of the event, including tasks,
                timeline, and logistical considerations.

          4. Obtain Commander Approval: Present the concept for review and secure the commander’s
                authorization.

          5. Issue Orders: Distribute operational orders to ensure all participants are informed and prepared.
          6. Monitor Pre-Execution Checks: Verify that all necessary preparations are completed before the

                event begins.
          7. Execute the Event: Conduct training, evaluate performance, and implement recovery activities.

1. DIALOGUE WITH THE COMMANDER

     1-7. The platoon leader consults with the company commander to seek additional guidance and gain approval
     to proceed with planning. During this step, both leaders review training requirements and discuss the
     execution plan. Communication continues throughout the event, incorporating a series of touch points as
     necessary.

     1-8. At a minimum, the discussion should cover the following topics:
          • MET to battle task nesting: Alignment of tasks to METs, initial training objectives, and any
                additional tasks to be trained.
          • Proficiency End State: The commander defines the desired proficiency level to be achieved by the
                end of the event. This includes attaining a "T" rating (trained) for the primary tasks being practiced.
          • Training Environment: Determine whether the event is conducted live, virtually, constructively,
                or through a blended approach (combination of these approaches).
          • Feasibility of Objectives: An assessment of whether the training objectives are achievable, based
                on a review of Training and Evaluation Outlines (T&EOs). For a detailed analysis of the task,
                refer to TC 7-0.4.
          • Resource Requirements: Identification of necessary resources and discussion of any known
                constraints related to time or availability requiring attention by the chain of command.

2. DETERMINE EVENT REQUIREMENTS

     1-9. Platoon leaders gather comprehensive information about the training event to ensure the most accurate
     and complete details are considered. In this step, platoon leadership performs the following actions:

          • Reviews commander’s training guidance to align the event in time and within the broader unit
                objectives.

          • Examine initial training objectives to clarify priorities and focus areas and specific tasks and
                required proficiency to train.

          • Assesses records of previous similar events to identify lessons learned and best practices.
          • Determines major resource requirements, including personnel, equipment, and facilities.
          • Evaluates standards and proficiency requirements as outlined in the T&EO, to ensure training

                goals meet operational benchmarks and standards.
          • Identifies additional tasks to train, addressing any gaps in skills or readiness required to execute

                the primary tasks trained.

Review Commanders’ Training Guidance

     1-10. The company commander's training guidance (CTG) provides direction for the entire training period.
     Platoon leaders review the CTG to identify critical details for planning events, such as:

          • Event types (e.g., Tactical Exercise Without Troops (TEWT), Lane Training, Situational Training
                Exercise (STX)).

          • Event dates.
          • Initial training objectives.
          • Additional information needed for planning.

Review of Initial Training Objectives

     1-11. Platoon leadership reviews the initial training objectives outlined in the CTG. These tactical goals focus
     on specific results that need to be achieved during training. During this stage, leadership refines initial
     training events to align with these goals. Platoons and lower echelons typically concentrate on:

          • Company METs: objectives critical for company success

Review Records of Previous Like-Events

     1-12. Platoon leadership reviews previous unit training records, focusing on the last instance when a similar
     event was conducted. This review provides valuable planning insights such as whether training objectives
     were met, resourcing requirements, necessary points of contact, coordination challenges, after action review
     (AAR) comments, and other critical details. Key sources of information on past training events include:

          • Unit training records (e.g., the Army’s digital system of record) and unit files.
          • AAR reports, documenting lessons learned.
          • Orders and concept of operation (CONOP) issued for previous events.
          • Resource requests related to the training.
          • Range facility contracts.
          • Unit lessons learned files, accessible through computer common drives, SharePoint or similar

                platforms.

Identify Major Resources

     1-13. Leaders identify and coordinate major training resources that require long lead times. Coordination
     may involve battalion-level and higher headquarters to ensure availability and support. After identifying
     resources, leaders must determine the best approach to synchronize resource requests, procurement, and
     expenditure with the training event requirements. For instance, if ammunition requires a 120-day reservation
     period while ranges only need 90 days, leaders should prioritize ranges that align with the requested
     ammunition. Examples of these resources include:

          • Ranges for live-fire and tactical exercises.
          • Range Regulations for compliance with local requirements and prerequisite conditions.
          • Training Areas for maneuver and scenario-based drills.
          • Multiple Integrated Laser Engagement Systems (MILES) for personnel and vehicles.
          • Class V (ammunition) blank, for weapons and live-fire training.
          • Simulators to support virtual and constructive training environments.
          • Unique Training Facility.
          • Unique or scarce resources critical for specialized training.
          • Off-installation training resources, such as facilities or areas located outside the installation.

Review Standards & Proficiency Requirements

     1-14. Leaders review task and weapon system standards alongside proficiency requirements. For tasks,
     leaders examine performance measures, and the task criteria matrix outlined in the T&EOs. These resources
     define the criteria necessary to achieve the desired training objectives. (For detailed information on using
     T&EOs, refer to TC 7-0.4. Training and Evaluation Outlines. 26 August 2025).

     1-15. For weapons training, leaders consult the appropriate training circular specific to each weapon type to
     ensure compliance with standards and requirements.

     1-16. Additionally, T&EO’s assist planners in event design.

Determine Additional Tasks to Train

     1-17. Platoon leadership evaluates additional collective tasks to include in training. These tasks may consist
     of activities that the platoon chooses to prioritize or mission-specific requirements that need attention. Special
     emphasis is placed on training high-payoff tasks, which yield significant operational benefits. Leaders can
     refer to T&EOs to identify supporting collective and individual tasks that align with the parent task.

3. DEVELOP AN EVENT CONCEPT

     1-18. With the guidance and approval of the company commander, the platoon leader proceeds with the
     further development and refinement of the plan. This includes:

          • Conducting reconnaissance of training sites.
          • Developing training course of action (COA).
          • Determining and requesting the necessary resources.

Recon Training Sites

     1-19. Training sites are identified during the development of the company UTP. In this step, leaders conduct
     physical verification of training locations to ensure they can support the proposed training and enable the unit
     to achieve its training objectives. Leaders also review training support plans to confirm that necessary
     resources are properly coordinated and prepared for execution

     1-20. Leaders visualize the progression of event actions based on the proposed COAs. They create a concept
     for execution that optimizes training and retraining opportunities within the constraints of available time and
     space. It’s important to have all key participants involved with the training execution to attend the recon.

Develop a Training Course of Action

     1-21. Leaders visually work backward from the end state (the desired outcome) of the training event. Once a
     desired end state is determined, the backward planning approach starts by determining what to train and
     subsequently deciding how to train. Figure 1-1 on page 5 demonstrates the flow and backwards planning
     concept.

Determine/Request Required Resources

     1-24. At this stage, the primary resources have been identified and coordinated. Platoons proceed by
     identifying and requesting any additional resources that may be required. It is essential to submit all requests
     for resources and support early in the planning process. Leaders should explore additional sources to ensure
     all resource needs are addressed:

          • Assessing resource requirements based on the type and scope of the event, including tasks outlined
                in T&EOs and weapons training circulars.

          • Reviewing records from similar past events, as these often provide the most accurate insights into
                resource needs. Unit historical records serve as a valuable tool for estimating requirements for like-
                type events previously conducted.

          • Utilizing the locally available installation training resources. The Battalion S3/S4 staff are a reliable
                source of this information.

          • Determine if the unit has any equipment or devices that can be used in the training.

     1-25. Resource Monitoring and Coordination: After submitting supporting resource requisitions, platoons
     must consistently track their status to ensure timely coordination. A T-Week calendar serves as an effective
     tool to remind planners to follow up on resource coordination efforts. Additionally, using a "training resource
     tracker," such as the example provided in FM 7-0, helps maintain progress and ensures all resources are
     prepared for execution.

          • Training Mines and Training Improvised Explosive Devices (IED) for recognition exercises.
          • Functional Triggers designed for use with Training IEDs.

4. OBTAIN COMMANDER APPROVAL

     1-28. Platoon leadership provides a detailed briefing to the company commander on the progress of planning.
     This includes all aspects of the training event, from the status of resource coordination to the planned
     execution of the event. The briefing enables the commander to assess whether the training event aligns with
     the company and platoon training objectives, or whether refinements are needed.

     1-29. Based on the briefing, the commander issues guidance and approves the training event plan, pending
     any necessary modifications. Once approval is granted, platoons proceed with further development and
     preparation for the event.

5. ISSUE ORDERS

     1-30. Leaders create and distribute orders to provide essential information about training events to all
     participants and support personnel. These orders address the "who, what, when, where, and why," ensuring
     everyone involved is informed and can complete necessary preparations. Known as the five paragraph
     OPORD, this format is a structured way to organize and communicate military operations, ensuring clarity
     and consistency in conveying plans. See FM 5-0 for details on all types of orders.

     1-31. All orders generally follow the standard five paragraph format, with content tailored to the specific
     type of order. Refer to figure 1-3, page 9, for the structure of a five paragraph OPORD. The format is
     remembered using the acronym SMEAC, which stands for Situation, Mission, Execution,
     Administration/Logistics, and Command/Signal:

          • Situation: Describes the current environment, including details about enemy forces, friendly forces,
                attachments, and terrain considerations.

          • Mission: Outlines the "who, what, when, where, and why" of the operation.
          • Execution: Details the concept of operations, tasks for subunits, and coordinating instructions.
          • Sustainment (or Administration/Logistics): Covers logistical and administrative support, such as

                supplies, medical evacuation, and transportation.
          • Command and Signal: Specifies command relationships, communication methods, and signal

                instructions.

6. MONITOR PRE-EXECUTION CHECKS

     1-34. Using a T-Week calendar and unit developed pre-execution checklists is recommended to track
     specific preparation actions and activities for each event planned. They are constantly reviewed to ensure
     pre-execution checks are identified and accomplished on time prior to the event. For example:

    T-Week       Example T-Week considerations
    T-16
    T-12              Activity
    T-11    Identify major training facilities (sixteen weeks before the event)
            Conduct training event planning (twelve weeks before the event)

     1-35. Leaders create and adjust T-Week calendars based on the planning requirements and the resourcing
     timelines of the installation and command. For instance, submitting a request for MILES equipment may
     require 18 weeks on one installation, while it could take longer than others. For a comprehensive
     explanation of T-Week planning, refer to TC 7-0.2.

Select and Certify Primary Trainers

     1-36. Initial Selection and Identification: Potential trainers are chosen based on their expertise, leadership
     potential, and operational experience to ensure that only the most qualified individuals serve as trainers within
     the unit. The commander defines the training and certification requirements for these trainers, as outlined in
     the unit's SOP. Candidates selected to lead training events, conduct simulation-based exercises, or teach
     specific classes must undergo additional certification processes. This systematic approach ensures that those
     designated as primary trainers possess the requisite qualifications, instructional capabilities, and operational
     knowledge to effectively fulfill their roles.

     1-37. Practical Experience and Certification: After gaining hands-on experience, trainers must
     demonstrate their proficiency by leading sessions under observation. Training event rehearsals provide an
     opportunity for trainers to gain experience and showcase their expertise before being certified by the

Conduct Rehearsals

     1-38. Leaders conduct rehearsals before training to ensure plans are well understood, synchronized, and that
     subordinates are aware of their roles and actions. Refer to the Army Training Network’s (ATN) “How to
     Conduct Rehearsals” video for additional guidance. Rehearsals help leaders to:

          • Confirm the micro timeline estimates.
          • Identify weaknesses or gaps in the plan.
          • Teach effective training techniques.
          • Coach trainers until they are confident in their roles.
          • Ensure safety and environmental considerations are addressed and updated.
          • Determine if subordinate leaders are tactically and technically proficient.
          • Evaluate subordinate leader competencies and provide constructive feedback.
          • Build subordinates' confidence in their ability to train or operate effectively.

     1-39. A few examples of rehearsals include:
          • Rehearsal of Concept (ROC) Drills: A visual and verbal walkthrough of the operation or training
                event to align understanding and coordination.
          • Backbriefs: Subordinate leaders brief back their understanding of the plan to confirm intent and
                alignment.
          • Sand Table Exercise: A physical or visual representation of the terrain and plan, used to rehearse
                operations in a hands-on manner.
          • TEWT: A low-cost exercise conducted in field conditions on actual terrain with minimum troop
                and support personnel.

             Note: OPFOR personnel (if used) also conduct rehearsals prior to training

Additional Planning Considerations

     1-40. Plan to include multiple sets and repetitions during training. Allocate sufficient time to perform as
     many iterations of tasks as possible. Increased practice, combined with timely feedback, enhances
     performance. Once proficiency is achieved, leaders should adjust the conditions under which tasks are
     trained, making the training environment more difficult, unpredictable, and challenging. When the unit
     consistently demonstrates task proficiency under any conditions, it achieves the concept of task mastery.
     Examples of altering task conditions include:

          • Conducting execution during day and night.
          • Adapting to changes in terrain or weather conditions.
          • Introducing changes in leadership roles.
          • Simulating equipment malfunctions or substitutions.
          • Experiencing failures in communication and command/control systems.
          • Reacting to actions or activities by opposing forces.

             Note: Task mastery extends beyond merely performing a task to standard through repeated
             practice (sets and repetitions). It involves successfully completing multiple iterations of a task
             under the most challenging and dynamic conditions. Task mastery requires continuously adapting
             to changes in the operational environment while optimizing the use of available resources.

     1-41. Plan to evaluate training during the event. This involves platoon leadership developing a
     comprehensive evaluation plan. Key considerations include:

          • Who will evaluate? Identifying personnel responsible for conducting evaluations.
          • Evaluator certification: Evaluators must be knowledgeable, experienced, and competent in the

                tasks being trained.
          • Observation positioning: Determining where evaluators should be located to best observe task

                execution.
          • Evaluation standards: Confirming evaluators have access to task T&EOs or weapon TCs to assess

          • AAR equipment: Ensuring evaluators have the necessary equipment to conduct AARs.
          • Feedback reporting: Establishing how evaluators will report feedback and identifying recipients

     1-42. Plan to retrain. Leaders allocate time for retraining tasks and weapons to ensure training objectives
     are fully achieved before concluding any event. Retraining is typically scheduled at the mid-point or the end
     of the event. Lane training offers an additional benefit: if task standards are not met, the lane can be
     reattempted repeatedly until proficiency is attained.

     1-43. Plan opportunity training. Opportunity training (or "hip-pocket training") focuses on individual tasks
     or crew-based skills during inactive periods. Before the event, platoon NCOs select tasks or drills that support
     battle tasks, require minimal preparation, and can be completed in 10 to 20 minutes.

     1-44. Pre-combat checks and inspections. Prior to execution, leaders perform pre-combat checks and
     inspections (PCC/PCI). These operational checks ensure that all personnel and equipment are fully prepared
     for operations. Unlike pre-execution checks, PCC/PCI focuses directly on readiness for the mission.

7. EXECUTE (TRAIN, EVALUATE, RECOVER)

     1-45. Platoons and squads execute training while leaders simultaneously carry out other critical activities,
     such as conducting evaluations, informal AARs, and retraining whenever proficiency is not achieved.

     1-46. During training, trainers and evaluators monitor the process, evaluate performance, and provide
     feedback using tools like T&EOs, weapon training TCs, written observations, or electronic means.

     1-47. Training is not considered complete until the objectives are fully met. Given the scarcity of training
     time, postponing proficiency goals necessitates scheduling future training events to address deficiencies. This
     also requires additional planning and coordination to secure unprogrammed resources for regaining
     proficiency.

                                Lane Training

    Lane training is a systematic and effective approach to improving unit proficiency in individual
    and collective tasks, battle tasks, and battle drills within a controlled environment. This method
    focuses on repeated practice, immediate feedback, and retraining, enabling units to efficiently
    meet tactical and technical standards. From rehearsing tasks to executing them in realistic
    scenarios, lane training enhances readiness and sharpens performance under evaluative
    oversight. Through its structured framework, this chapter examines the principles, planning
    processes, advantages, and practical applications of lane training at the platoon level and
    beyond, providing leaders with strategies to optimize training outcomes and operational
    efficiency.

OVERVIEW

     2-1. Lane training is a technique to train and evaluate small units and Soldiers on individual tasks, collective
     tasks, or battle drills in a designated and controlled area, or facility. In lane training, a small unit (company
     size and smaller) operates from a stationary position, or tactically moves mounted, or dismounted through a
     prescribed land course (a lane), and performs selected tasks which are observed by an evaluator. The tasks
     performed are observed and evaluated based on standards outlined in the tasks’ T&EO. The focus of lane
     training is on small unit task proficiency.

     2-2. In planning the lane, the unit commander determines the training objectives for the exercise. Typically,
     the unit moves through each stage of the lane executing tasks as it goes. During execution, certified evaluators
     observe and evaluate tasks focused on the training objectives. Following task execution, an evaluator
     provides feedback to the unit based on observed performance during an on-site AAR. Based on unit
     performance, the unit then moves to the next lane or retrains the task before re-entering the lane for another
     attempt. An evaluator applies this general technique for either a lane training exercise or a situational training
     exercise.

     2-3. In a lane, the unit enters and conducts a rehearsal of the tasks it will perform. Movement through the
     lane is performed through tactical movement. For example, a dismounted infantry squad moves forward in
     patrol formation, or a mounted logistics convoy moves from one location to another. Following a rehearsal,
     the unit enters the lane area in which it will react to a tactical situation. Unit reaction to the programmed
     scenario requires it to execute the tasks selected for observation and evaluation (training objectives). After
     task execution, the evaluator conducts a facilitated AAR and provides the unit with a GO or NO-GO rating.
     In the lane training context, a GO rating indicates that the unit met T&EO standards for the task as specified
     by the training objectives for the lane. A NO-GO rating indicates the unit did not.

     2-4. If the unit achieves a GO rating, the unit is allowed to proceed to the next lane to perform other tasks. If
     the unit is rated a NO-GO and fails to achieve task standards and training objectives, then it retrains and then
     re-enters the same lane at the start to perform the task again. This process is repeated multiple times until the
     unit achieves task standards.

     2-5. The primary advantages of lane training are—
          • It requires limited space to execute tasks.
          • The training environment is unit controlled.
          • Tasks are based on unit proficiency requirements and training objectives.
          • Feedback of performance is immediate.
          • It provides the opportunity to retrain and execute tasks until performed to standard.

     2-6. Lane training is a systematic, performance-oriented process. The rigor of the lane training process
     enables units to attain proficiency quickly and efficiently in tactical and technical tasks. It enables training to
     be effectively structured, administered, supported, and evaluated by limiting the number of tasks, time,

TYPES OF LANE TRAINING

     2-9. There are two types of exercises that employ basic lane training techniques:
          • Lane training exercise (known as LTX).
          • Situational training exercise (known as STX).

     2-10. A lane training exercise is a company and below exercise conducted in a live or simulated training
     environment used to train individual and/or collective tasks and battle drills that include a designated area,
     terrain, or facility. A LTX consists of an assembly area, rehearsal, lane execution, AAR, and retraining
     activities. It is a limited scope exercise, does not rely on a tactical scenario and allows leaders to focus training
     on multiple iterations of a task to proficiency. Trainers consider the following when developing LTXs:

• An LTX does not use free play.

2-11. An LTX will include one or more lanes, with each lane focusing on executing one primary task or drill.
When an LTX includes more than one lane, each lane can be used to train the same primary task over in
multiple iterations or to train different tasks. Ideally, units use different lanes for rehearsals, lane execution,
and retraining on the same primary task.

2-12. A situational training exercise is a company and below exercise conducted in a live or simulated
training environment to train mission-based tactical situations focused on a group of related collective tasks
or battle drills. A STX usually contains multiple collective tasks linked to form a realistic scenario of a
military operation, sometimes incorporating free play. STXs are developed by the Army branch proponent
to train the doctrinally preferred way to perform a specific series of tasks. The STX is a walk-level event.

2-13. Commanders employ STXs to—
     • Train multiple tasks in a small-scale, limited operational environment.
     • Conduct multiple iterations of tasks until standards are met.
     • Provide immediate feedback through AARs in a controlled environment.
     • Develop and refine unit tactical SOPs.

2-14. STXs facilitate training through the application of standardized tactical formations and employment.
They are considered when training small units whenever appropriate. They provide immediate feedback to
leaders and Soldiers through integrated AARs. STXs require fewer resources and support personnel than
large-scale command post exercises (CPX) or field training exercises (FTX) and can be set up in smaller
training areas close to unit garrison locations as necessary.

LANE TRAINING CHARACTERISTICS

     2-15. The lane training process follows the same steps—plan, prepare, execute, and assess—as the operations
     process. Historically, lane training has been associated with tasks requiring movement over terrain (for
     example, conduct a movement to contact, conduct an attack or conduct convoy operations); however,
     movement is not required. Lane training is appropriate for most small-unit maneuver, maneuver support, and
     sustainment collective tasks requiring teamwork and practice, whether conducted in fixed facilities or in a
     field environment.

2-16. To ensure standardization, units conduct lane training to teach doctrinally preferred ways to perform
specific missions or tasks. Initially, lane training focuses on a task or a few tasks one at a time and may
exclude related tasks that may distract Soldiers from learning. However, once a unit is proficient in the tasks
trained, more tasks may be progressively included to increase realism.

2-17. A lane can consist of multiple lanes training the same task but with different and varying conditions.
Lanes are more flexible than drills, and units can tailor a lane to meet a unit’s METs, battle tasks, or assigned
mission requirements.

2-18. Lane training has characteristics that make it significantly different from other forms of training
exercises. Unit leaders consider these characteristics before planning and executing lane training to ensure
that conducting lane training is the most efficient manner of developing the task proficiency desired. These
characteristics are listed in table 2-1, pages 17-18.

END STATE PROFICIENCY

     2-38. Planning and Evaluation Process: As part of the planning process, the commander defines the
     training objective for the lane, culminating in the desired end state proficiency. Following the lane AAR, the
     evaluator determines the unit’s performance against the task’s T&EO and assigns a proficiency rating: T
     (Trained), P (Practiced), or U (Untrained).

     2-39. For example, the commander may require first and second platoons to achieve a ‘T’ rating for a specific
     collective task. If the first platoon achieves a ‘P,’ the result is a NO-GO for the lane. Conversely, if the second
     platoon achieves a ‘T,’ the result is a GO.

             Note: The commander’s stated training proficiency end state should align with the unit's
             capabilities, such as ensuring the presence of the required number of leaders and Soldiers.

     2-40. In the event of a NO-GO, the unit retrains and re-enters the lane until it achieves a GO. Once a GO is
     achieved, the unit may advance to the next lane (if applicable) or re-enter the same lane to improve its
     proficiency rating or train under more demanding conditions. Ideally, event planners schedule multiple
     iterations for each unit to maximize training effectiveness.

     2-58. A unit conducting lane training should not provide its own support, as this limits the full participation

     internally supported or externally supported. Long-range planning and coordination are critical to ensuring
     sufficient support for lane training. When external support is unavailable, leaders organize and conduct
     internally supported lanes.

Internally Supported

     2-59. Internally Supported Training: Internally supported training relies on resources provided within the
     unit responsible for managing the training. It is typically used in the following scenarios—

          • Single-Unit Lane Training: When only one unit undergoes lane training, support functions are
                often managed adequately by the next higher unit.

          • Multi-Unit Lane Training: When several units undergo training on one or more lanes, the
                supporting headquarters (two echelons above the training unit) identifies and acquires resources
                from units within the command.

Externally Supported

     2-60. Externally supported training involves resources sourced from outside the unit responsible for
     achieving training proficiency. This approach is typically necessary when multiple units undergo training
     across one or more lanes, and internal resource support cannot meet the demand. In such cases, the supporting
     or managing headquarters identifies the required resources and secures them externally.

     2-61. Multi-Lane Training Implementation: Senior commanders often utilize multi-lane training, which
     demands significantly more planning, scheduling, coordination, and resource allocation compared to single-

   Using Troop Leading Procedures for Planning Training
                                     Events

    Army troop leading procedures are a systematic, 8-step process that small-unit leaders use to
    plan, prepare, and execute missions (training) efficiently—even under time constraints. TLPs
    transform a higher commander's intent into clear, actionable steps for training or combat
    operations.

OVERVIEW

     B-1. When developing training events and plans at company level and below, leaders follow troop leading
     TLP as the framework for planning (see FM 5-0). Although some of the planning activities performed for
     operations differ for training, they are similar enough that TLP provide a sound and reliable basis for planning
     training events. Additionally, using TLP reinforces the principle of train as you fight. Leaders familiar with
     Army operations and planning processes in training can more seamlessly transition to operations and
     planning processes when deployed.
     B-2. Platoon and below leaders begin TLP when they receive a WARNORD from company leadership to
     plan a training event. The process typically includes the following steps—

                                            TROOP LEADING PROCEDURES

          • STEP 1 Receive the Mission: The leader gathers the initial mission briefing, gaining
              an understanding of the objective and operational environment.

          • STEP 2 Issue a Warning Order: A preliminary order is distributed to alert
              subordinates and allow them to begin preparations.

          • STEP 3 Make a Tentative Plan: The leader quickly develops an initial plan,
              considering available information and resources.

          • STEP 4 Initiate Movement: Orders are given to begin positioning or movement as
              needed while planning continues (May be skipped when conducting training events).

          • STEP 5 Conduct Reconnaissance: The leader, often accompanied by subordinate
              leaders, gathers more detailed information on the terrain, enemy positions, or other
              relevant factors.

          • STEP 6 Complete the Plan: The tentative plan is refined and finalized, incorporating
              updated intelligence and resource assessments.

          • STEP 7 Issue the OPORD: A detailed order is delivered to all unit elements that
              outline the mission, tasks, and coordination activities.

          • STEP 8 Supervise and Refine: Continuous oversight ensures that the execution
              stays on track, with adjustments made as necessary based on evolving conditions.

     B-24. Once the tentative plan is prepared, the planner conducts a dialogue (backbrief) with the company
     commander and first sergeant. This review covers the list of training objectives, the overall concept of
     operations, and the support requirements for the event. The backbrief should be sufficiently detailed to allow
     the command team to ask critical questions and confirm that all exercise coordination is progressing as
     planned. Upon completion, the company commander approves the selected COA for the training event.

STEP 7 - ISSUE THE ORDER

     B-25. In step 7, the commander issues the administrative OPORD to subordinate leaders to support further
     planning. Leaders below the company level typically deliver small unit orders verbally. Planners make
     updates, changes, and clarifications to the base administrative OPORD through WARNORD and FRAGORD
     as necessary. All orders follow the standard five-paragraph order format. Refer to chapter one for more
     information on the types of orders.

STEP 8 - SUPERVISE AND REFINE

     B-26. In the final step, all company leaders oversee and monitor pre-execution checks, providing updates on
     progress and status during weekly training meetings. These meetings are essential for maintaining the
     timeline of T-Week actions and facilitating face-to-face discussions between planners and leaders on
     completed actions and upcoming tasks. This process ensures that accountability for specific actions is
     assigned to designated individuals and remains enforced until the company successfully completes the
     training event.

after action review
          A guided analysis of an organization’s performance, conducted at appropriate times during and at the
