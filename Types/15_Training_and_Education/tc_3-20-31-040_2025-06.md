                                 Introduction

     The purpose of the direct fire kill chain is to provide a standard description of all the tasks and actions of the
     firers, teams, squads, crews, and platoons employing direct fire weapon systems. This allows Soldiers and
     leaders to maximize the effects of lethal fires against any threat while simultaneously reducing or eliminating
     fratricide and collateral damage. Introductory figure-1 presents the direct fire kill chain.

     The direct fire kill chain (hereinafter the kill chain) is a deliberate, ongoing series of
     interconnected tasks, actions, and functions that enable the rapid and effective application of
     the appropriate combat power on a confirmed threat to achieve the desired tactical effects on
     the target.

                                      Introductory figure-1. Direct fire kill chain

PURPOSE

     This training circular describes the kill chain for direct fire weapons as it applies to Soldiers, leaders, crews,
     squads, and platoons. It provides an overview of the kill chain, including each interconnected task and the
     specific outcomes they must accomplish regardless of the steps taken to achieve those outcomes.
     The kill chain described within this publication represents the standard process for engaging threats with
     direct fire weapons. It relies upon the firer’s knowledge of the unit’s combat identification equipment, basic
     Soldier skills, rules of engagement, doctrine (including tactics, techniques, and procedures), target
     identification systems, and the available situational understanding systems. This training circular discusses
     those systems in detail only when they directly impact the kill chain. It assists leaders in making the decision
     to eliminate threats with lethal direct fire. When appropriate, it identifies publications with additional
     information about a specific, complex function within the kill chain.

Introduction

OVERVIEW

     The kill chain provides an iterative, standard, and systematic approach to targeting engagement activities.
     The following chapters describe the basic functions (or links) of the kill chain in detail.

CHAPTER 1—WEAPON POSTURE

     This chapter reviews the points at which leaders inform subordinate elements of the weapon posture and rules
     of engagement for the tactical mission. The weapon posture is a combination of weapon safety status and the
     appropriate weapons control status based on the likelihood of enemy contact. The unit commander provides
     the rules of engagement. Together, the weapon posture and rules of engagement enable each maneuver
     element to establish within the organization the appropriate level of readiness for rapid delivery of judicious
     and appropriate combat power against hostile contact. The weapon posture reinforces the commander’s
     intent, supports fratricide prevention, and facilitates reduction in collateral damage.

          • Source—Operation orders, warning orders, fragmentary orders, rules of engagement, tactical
                standard operating procedures, threat templates, situation templates, and other information and
                intelligence.

          • Outcome—The small unit knows and reinforces the appropriate weapon posture and rules of
                engagement, including establishing the appropriate battlecarry for systems with multiple
                ammunition types (for example, Abrams and Bradley).

CHAPTER 2—OBSERVE

     This chapter discusses the continuous process of the Soldier’s or collective element’s scan or search of
     specific overlapping sectors of responsibility to achieve visual contact of a potential tactical threat before
     other forms of contact are known. The observer selects one or more areas for more detailed scrutiny. These
     areas contain indications of anomalous surroundings and potential threat positions, avenues of approach, and
     useful terrain to a hostile force attempting to interrupt friendly operations.

          • Source—Established sectors of observation within the organization or system, based on capabilities,
                standard operating procedures, and function.

          • Outcome—An area of interest warrants additional scrutiny.

CHAPTER 3—DETECT

     This chapter describes the initiation of the target acquisition process (detect, identify, and locate) and the
     process observers employ to find evidence (target signatures) of a potential threat in the sector of
     responsibility. The ability of Soldiers to achieve visual contact before a threat gains visual contact enables
     the unit to deliver appropriate combat power (reciprocating contact) first.

          • Source—A target signature or an anomaly discovered by an observer.
          • Outcome—An observer found evidence (target signatures) of a potential threat in the sector of

                responsibility.

CHAPTER 4—IDENTIFY

     This chapter reviews the responsibilities of identification—the process of determining the friendly or hostile
     character of an unknown detected contact (JP 3-01). The work of identification includes the interrogation and
     discrimination of recognizable contacts as friendly, foe, neutral, noncombatant, or unknown. During the
     identification process, by using intelligence, the known situation, friendly and enemy force dispositions, and
     other information, the observer also determines the neutral or noncombatant status of a detected contact. The
     process to identify is a key component of target acquisition (as defined in JP 3-60).

          • Source—Analysis of a detected contact’s components by both following the combat identification
                steps to recognize, assess, identify, and discriminate and interrogating the frame, armament,
                suspension, turret, equipment, and reactive protection.

          • Outcome—
                 Recognize the contact’s military description.
                 Assess the description’s threat level.
                 Identify the model (or common name) and variant.
                 Discriminate the hostile intent as friend, foe, neutral, noncombatant, and unknown.

CHAPTER 5—LOCATE

     This chapter covers various methods for determining the location of an identified target description to direct,
     guide, or orient the appropriate weapons, systems, or combat power. It details how an observer of an
     identified threat provides that tactical information to the leader in the form of a contact report or, when from
     a firer, an acquisition report. The determination of a location instigates the element’s orientation of optics,
     sensors, and makeready weapons. The process to locate is a key component of target acquisition (as defined
     in JP 3-60).

          • Source—Discriminated target description information shared with the collective element; the use of
                digital or analog systems or methods (to determine the location).

          • Outcome—The appropriate leader receives information regarding the target description’s location,
                and the appropriate weapon is or systems are oriented on the threat.

CHAPTER 6—DECIDE

     This chapter outlines the decisions and process of a leader with the authority to issue a command of execution
     while that leader determines the appropriate use of combat power, the classification of the threat level, the
     prioritization of the threats (sequence of engagement), and the fire pattern to apply during the conduct of fire.
     The command of execution authority bases all decisions on the application of the kill chain principles.

          • Source—Contact or acquisition report provided to the leader.
          • Outcome—

                 The leader has selected the appropriate combat power, classified the presented threats,
                     prioritized the threats for elimination, and selected the fire pattern that will achieve the desired
                     target effects.

                 The leader and firer completed target confirmation.

CHAPTER 7—ENGAGE

     This chapter provides a general overview of the conduct of fire for direct fire engagements. It describes the
     elements of the fire command for squad, crew, and collective conduct of fire that serve as the structure for
     applying direct fire against a confirmed threat. TC 3-20.31-043, a subordinate publication to this training
     circular, provides further information on fire commands and their appropriate use. The fire command cycle
     continues until the successful accomplishment of the desired effects on the known threats to the satisfaction
     of the tactical leader’s intent, meaning the firing element effectively alters, disrupts, or halts the confirmed
     target’s kill chain and reduces or eliminates the threat to friendly forces.

          • Source—The leader’s decision to apply combat power against the confirmed target.
          • Outcome—The desired target effect is achieved against all known threats.

                           Weapon Posture

The weapon posture combines the senior commander’s intent, current and potential threat
assessments, and the likelihood of enemy contact. It executes clear use of the rules of firearms
safety, weapons control status (WCS), weapon safety status (WSS), and rules of
engagement (ROE) (see figure 1-1). The WSS coupled with the WCS provide small-unit
leaders with force protection measures that allow for the appropriate level of readiness and
clear articulation of the ROE throughout a mission, potential contact with threat forces, and the
most likely forms of contact with hostile forces.
Prior to the unit’s entering a hostile environment, commanders and leaders review the potential
for enemy contact, ROE, and mission objectives. Commanders describe how each unit will
posture their weapons and systems to provide the highest degree of force protection that
addresses those items. During the orders process for any ongoing or future mission,
commanders direct their units’ weapon postures.

Chapter 1

WEAPONS AND WEAPON SYSTEMS HANDLING

     1-1. Safe weapons handling procedures offer a standard by which Soldiers are to handle, operate, and employ
     weapons or weapon systems safely and effectively. Weapons handling comprises three components: the
     Soldier, the weapon, and the environment.

     1-2. The Soldier maintains situational understanding of friendly forces, the weapon’s status, and the
     environment to handle the weapon properly. Smart, adaptive, and disciplined Soldiers are the primary safety
     mechanism for all weapons under their control.

     1-3. The weapon is a Soldier’s primary tool to defeat threats in combat. Soldiers maintain knowledge on
     operating the mechanical and electrical safeties of the weapons or weapon systems they employ, as well as
     the principles of operation for those weapons to understand how the cycle of function directly impacts the
     weapon posture.

     1-4. The environment is a Soldier’s surroundings and mindset. Soldiers remain aware of muzzle discipline,
     the nature of the target, and what is behind the target. Threats often mix with noncombatants, so Soldiers
     remain ready to perform rapid discrimination.

WEAPONS HANDLING MEASURES

     1-5. To handle weapons safely and effectively, Soldiers maintain cognitive awareness of the four distinct
     weapons handling measures that build the weapon posture. The weapons handling measures within the
     weapon posture are—

          • Rules of firearms safety.
          • WSS.
          • WCS.
          • ROE.

     1-6. The rules of firearms safety are common practices for training and combat. Each weapon’s training
     publication, technical manual, or training strategy lists its applicate rules.

     1-7. The leader or commander typically delivers the WSS and WCS initially in the tactical standard operating
     procedures (SOPs) and then updates them appropriately in the operation order (OPORD), warning order, or
     fragmentary order (FRAGORD). Transmissions of weapon posture updates occur across digital, analog,
     face-to-face, or radio methods.

Chapter 1

WEAPON SAFETY STATUS

     1-9. The WSS code of colors—green, amber, red, and black—indicates the safety levels of a weapon (see
     figure 1-3). To place a weapon in each safety level requires a specific series of actions. In training and combat,
     the colors assign safety levels to the current tasks or actions of Soldiers, small units, or groups.
     1-10. WSS ratings depend on the level of safety measures for the weapon itself. Figure 1-3 links general
     weapon safety conditions to WSS ratings based on the Army-standardized color scheme in FM 1-02.2.

                       Figure 1-3. Weapon safety status and general descriptions

   1-11. All firers and leaders remain fluent in WSS ratings and their pertinence to the employed weapons. They
   maintain awareness of the firer’s responsibility to own each shot or burst. Basic definitions for each WSS
   rating follow, but the proper reference for a specific weapon’s process is its training circular:

        • Green—fully safe:
              The weapon is clear, no ammunition is present (nor magazine or belt), the chamber is empty,
                   and the weapon is on safe.
              All weapons have an individual task to CLEAR WEAPON.

        • Amber—substantially safe:
              The weapon is clear and verified by a leader, the bolt or slide (depending on the weapon) is
                   forward, and ammunition is introduced to the weapon.
              This is an administrative and preparatory WSS.
              Its primary use is for mounted weapons during combat operations under the direction to
                   maintain a substantially safe weapon’s abilities to transition and escalate rapidly to red or black
                   status as the situation dictates.

        • Red—marginally safe:
              The weapon is on safe, and depending on the weapon type, the magazine is locked into the well,
                   or the belted ammunition is on the feed tray with the cover closed.
              For pistols, rifles, carbines, and sniper weapon systems, a round is in the chamber, and the bolt
                   is forward in the locked position.
              For the M249 and M240 series, the bolt is locked to the rear, and the ammunition is on the feed
                   tray with the cover closed.

        • Black—not safe:
              The weapon is fully prepared to fire, the firer has positively identified the target, the weapon is
                   on fire, the firer’s finger is on the trigger, and the firer is in the process of engaging the threat.
              The color black indicates the actions of the firer when in red status but entering an engagement
                   sequence as part of the engage function of the direct fire kill chain (that is, its use clearly
                   differentiates red WSS from active, deliberate threat engagement).

RULES OF ENGAGEMENT

     1-14. ROE are Department of Defense directives issued by competent military authority that delineate the
     circumstances and limitations under which Soldiers initiate and continue combat engagement of encountered
     forces. They reflect the law of land warfare and the operational considerations, and their primary concern
     remains the restraints on the use of force. They are the primary means by which leaders convey legal,
     political, diplomatic, and military guidance to Soldiers. Leaders at every level train Soldiers thoroughly,
     before their deployments, in the ROE and the laws governing armed conflict. During the conduct of
     operations, leaders ensure Soldiers properly apply the ROE to preclude inappropriate engagements.
     1-15. ROE and tactics, techniques, and procedures depend on the theater. Before a force deploys, the Judge
     Advocate General’s Corps of the Army reviews and approves through the chain of command the rules by
     which a target may be engaged. These rules address the appropriate escalation of force.
     1-16. The rules of interaction (ROI) provide guidance for public interaction in the operational environment.
     ROI, when applied with effective interpersonal communication skills, improve a Soldier’s ability to
     accomplish the mission while reducing hostile confrontations. ROI founded on firm ROE provide Soldiers
     with tools to address nontraditional threats such conflict from political, ideological, cultural, and religious
     tensions. ROI remain regionally and culturally specific. Leaders integrate ROE and ROI into all training
     appropriately.

                                 Observe

Within the kill chain, certain principles govern the overall outcome. Soldiers lacking the skills
to observe their areas or sectors of responsibility effectively cannot deliver lethal fires on target
before the enemy does. The ability to observe correctly with all available sensors and optics
increases the firer’s and unit’s lethality. No other function (or link) of the kill chain can occur
without appropriate observation for and detection of threats to the friendly force (see
figure 2-1). In relation to the observation of potential threats in the modern battlespace, this
chapter describes the roles and responsibilities of all Soldiers as tactical observers, individual
firers, members of a cohesive crew or team, and members of a small unit. It discusses a logical
process to assist observers in focusing on the most probable threat locations, the various
signatures left or emitted by common threats, and general observation challenges and best
practices.

Chapter 2

PROCESS

     2-1. At the onset of any mission, each Soldier carries the responsibility to search continuously for potential
     threats to the friendly force, regardless of military occupational specialty. During large-scale combat
     operations, Soldiers use various search techniques. Whether on patrol, conducting a tactical mission task,
     performing base defense, force protection, or guard duty, traveling with a unit, or stationary while conducting
     routine functions, all Soldiers work actively or passively to detect potential threats.

     2-2. During combat operations, observation for threats is a continuous function. The three methods of
     observing assigned sectors of responsibility for potential threats to the friendly force follow:

          • Rapid scan:
                 A fast, continuous observation of the sector of responsibility, typically with the use of only the
                     primary optic or naked eye, to discover conspicuous areas, anomalies, positions, avenues of
                     approach, and key terrain.
                 Useful vertically, horizontally, or in depth across the observed area.

          • Slow scan:
                 A more methodical observation at a reduced pace, with additional optics or sensors, to develop
                     understanding of the terrain for the ultimate selection of locations for inspection.
                 Useful vertically, horizontally, or in depth, depending on the nature of the observed area.

          • Detailed search:
                 A deliberate investigation of the observed area that prompts additional scrutiny for potential
                     target signatures (also known as a search or deliberate search).
                 Provides the most probable locations of potential threats and specific areas of interest to focus
                     target detection efforts.

     2-3. The observation process continues until any conspicuous activity, anomalies, potential threat positions,
     avenues of approach, and key terrain that could serve to interrupt friendly operations undergo correct scrutiny
     or until the unit observes a potential threat. Upon perceiving any of those conspicuous items, an observer
     escalates to detect a threat. Figure 2-2 portrays the observation process.

SCAN

     2-4. A scan is the act of observing designated areas in the assigned sector. A scan or scanning allows an
     observer to detect anomalies, potential threat positions, avenues of approach, and key terrain that could serve
     to interrupt friendly operations. Anomalies include irregular shapes, colors, exhaust, heat sources, movement,
     and actions the element views as incongruous with the surrounding area. Scanning can delimit an area of
     likelihood for the element to focus its search for potential threat positions. While conducting their scans,
     observers ask themselves the question, Where would I be if I were the threat?

     2-5. In terrain scans, the visibility conditions, element’s positioning, fire distribution planning, and optic or
     sensor capabilities dictate the coverable distance (depth and width). Each element’s observation area overlaps
     adjacent elements’ sectors. These factors require the element to coordinate how they will cover their assigned
     sector for offensive and defensive operations.

     2-6. Elements apply various scanning methods in an overlapping manner. Doing so ensures the least amount
     of dead space and maximizes the use of Soldiers and optics to increase the overall ability of the unit to acquire
     potential threat positions as rapidly as possible.

     2-7. Elements use thermal optics, infrared (IR) night-vision devices (NVDs), machine gun optics, binoculars,
     and the naked eye to scan during good and limited visibility conditions. Soldiers train to use all available
     optics and sensors proficiently, so they may switch between types of optics or sensors, particularly during
     operations with limited visibility.

SCANNING BEST PRACTICES

     2-8. Scanning requires patience and correct practice. Some best practices for scanning during combat follow:
          • Conduct initial scanning with and without optics or sensors.
          • Scan continuously.
          • Integrate active listening with visual observation.
          • Use scanning methods appropriate for the environment and terrain.
          • Practice sector discipline; stay in assigned sector.
          • Periodically check the airspace above assigned sector since sectors of responsibility are
                three-dimensional.
          • Vary sensor adjustments for brightness, contrast, focus, polarity, and magnification and between the
                search and stare modes.

     2-9. Collective small units, crews, and teams simultaneously employ multiple scan methods to maximize the
     results of their observation efforts. Two scanning methods include three patterns for determining potential
     threat locations:

          • Rapid scan:
                 This method is useful for quickly detecting obvious signs of enemy activity.
                 Whether moving or stationary, observers usually employ this method first.

          • Slow scan:
                 This method is useful when observers detected no targets with the rapid scan.
                 It is most useful to observers in a defensive position, during a short halt, or with the ability to
                     stabilize the optic or sensor firmly and thereby to reduce jittering or bouncing in the field of
                     view.

     2-10. Elements ensure ground reference points are always within their fields of view to maintain directional
     control and situational awareness (SA) while scanning. The terrain surrounding the potential threat and the
     threat’s domain (ground or air) directly influences which scanning method and pattern will best enable visual
     contact.

SCAN LIMITS

     2-11. Each small unit, crew platform, system, and element covers various sectors of observation to provide
     360-degree security and to focus observation efforts in the most probable directions of enemy contact. The
     unit’s tactical SOPs typically list sectors of observation, limits, and areas. Alterations to those procedures
     occur automatically during operations, depending on the terrain, tactical situation, intelligence reports, and
     related activities.
     2-12. This section describes some best practices for observers to review their assigned areas of observation
     and to detect threats as quickly as possible. These best practices serve to educate observers on the techniques,
     patterns, and coordination efforts for the efficient and effective completion of this critical force protection
     task.

Estimation of Scan Limits

     2-13. Scan limits are defined in the assigned sector of observation. They typically overlap with those of other
     observers to ensure redundancy of effort. Environmental conditions, the unit’s posture (offense or defense),
     and other factors often inform the limits. When scanning, observers periodically change their employed
     techniques as the terrain or situation dictates.

Horizontal Pattern

     2-15. Observers use the horizontal pattern for large distances in the sector of observation. This pattern is
     applicable to both the rapid and slow scanning methods. It involves a left-to-right, smooth traverse of the
     sector of observation for the detection of anomalies.

     2-16. The horizontal pattern limits in open terrain begin at the visible horizon, from the left-most edge to the
     right most edge of the assigned sector of observation. The horizontal pattern calls for smooth, lateral left and
     right eye movements. Periodically, observers scan above the horizon for potential air target signatures across
     the entire sector.

     2-17. Observers scanning with the horizontal pattern follow these procedural steps:
          • Oscillate the gaze from left to right, then right back to left, and so on.
          • Progressively shift attention from the far horizon inward (that is, closer to one’s location) with each
                pass.
          • Once one’s focus lies within 100 meters of one’s location, increase the distance incrementally
                outward again to the horizon.
          • When one’s focus again meets the far horizon, scan approximately 20 degrees above the horizon for
                possible air threats.
          • Estimate 20 degrees by fully spreading the fingers and gauging the tip of the thumb as the upper
                scan limit (see figure 2-3).

     2-18. In the limits of the assigned sector of observation, observers completely understand their own limits,
     as well as where their limits overlap with those of others and with whom their limits overlap. Horizontal
     pattern limits are much larger than those in the vertical plane or those apparent while the observer is scanning
     in depth. When a sector is broad, observers make concerted efforts to conduct their scans across the entire
     breadth of the sector.

Depth Pattern

     2-19. Observers use the depth pattern with the rapid or slow scanning method to review terrain from near to
     far or from far to near along clearly defined boundaries. These boundaries are where avenues of approach,
     channels, corridors, gullies, creek or stream beds, low-lying areas, arroyos, or wadis are present.

     2-20. The depth pattern has observers begin scanning close to their locations and then follow the low area’s
     structure outward to the far horizon. This pattern is useful for detecting potential threats from reverse slope
     defense positions that use the natural state of the terrain to advantage.

Vertical Pattern

     2-21. Elements may miss aircraft during their scans of the sky when they limit their threat acquisition efforts
     to areas near the horizon. Conversely, elements will likely miss low-flying aircraft when they expand the
     upper limits of their acquisition efforts beyond 20 degrees above the horizon.

     2-22. An alternate pattern for conducting such a scan is for Soldiers to scan the horizon by shifting their gazes
     from object to object. They can see more detail this way than with a continuous scan of the horizon. The
     vertical pattern is preferable in hilly terrain.

RAPID SCAN

     2-24. Elements use rapid scans to recognize conspicuous threats, signatures, and positions (see figure 2-5).
     A rapid scan is a swift visual sweep of the sector with the naked eye or platform-mounted and hand-held
     optics in the wide field of view (WFOV).

Individual Observer

     2-25. Rapid scans are useful for quickly detecting obvious signs of enemy activity. Whether moving or
     stationary, observers usually use this scanning method first.
     2-26. Rapid scanning involves methodical steps. Soldiers start scanning the center of the sector and rapidly
     scan from the point nearest to their locations to the point farthest from their locations. They then orient left
     or right and conduct a rapid scan from near to far. This sweep overlaps the center of the previously scanned
     sector. Once they complete their scans of one side from the center, they scan the opposite side from the center
     in the same manner.

                                  Detect

Maneuver warfare, either in the offense or defense, requires Soldiers’ active detection of
contacts with hostile intent or capability. Soldiers maintain proficiency in the techniques and
procedures of rapid detection of potential threats. They provide the information to the
appropriate leader for making the appropriate engagement decision. The Soldier’s ability to
detect contacts rapidly directly impacts the mission, force protection, and engagement
decisions against hostile targets to orchestrate the engagement process. The ability of Soldiers
and leaders to process the target information accurately and quickly supports friendly force
capabilities to fire faster than the threat, engage the threat at the maximum possible range, and
hit the threat first.
Within the kill chain, the detect function (or link) begins when a friendly force can see a contact
in the sector of observation with the naked eye, optics, or sensors. The detect function ends
when the observer finds evidence of a target signature (see figure 3-1).

PROCESS

     3-1. Target acquisition is the detection, identification, and location of a target in sufficient detail to permit
     the effective employment of capabilities that create the required effects (JP 3-60). It is the discovery of any
     contact in the operational environment such as personnel, vehicles, equipment, or objects of potential military
     significance. Three functions (or links) of the kill chain are specific to target acquisition (TA): detect,
     identify, and locate (see figure 3-2). The detection of contacts that may present threats to the friendly force
     initiates the TA process. This is a force protection measure, and it leads to lethal delivery of combat power.
     The faster a unit detects a target, the faster it can positively identify, locate, and appropriately engage the
     threat. During the application of the kill chain to a tactical situation, many actions (functions) overlap, occur
     simultaneously, complete sequentially, or integrate with other processes.

                         Figure 3-2. Target acquisition process within the kill chain

    3-2. The TA process begins after the successful scan and search of the sector of observation, during which
    the observer selects an area of interest to investigate. The observer’s ability to detect potential threats rapidly
    under any condition is critical to survivability and mission success.

           Note. Threat detection is the discovery of a target signature in the sector of observation. The
           significance and relevance of the signature is unknown and cues the observer to gather more
           information. The detect function (or link) of the kill chain is the threat detection link.

    3-3. In accordance with the kill chain, once the observer selects an area of interest to scrutinize for potential
    threats, the observer begins to acquire targets with threat detection. Effective threat detection requires specific
    skills that observers master through proper, repetitive application. Threat detection is an active process within
    the kill chain during combat operations, regardless of whether a threat presence is clear or known. The
    observer’s target detection skills enable all direct fire engagements.

    3-4. Threat detection encompasses the observer’s ability to employ various optics and sensors to discover
    subtle target signatures indicative of a potential threat. The observer maintains the capabilities to determine
    possible threat locations rapidly and to apply detection techniques effectively.

    3-5. Soldiers employ all available assets and options to facilitate rapid TA. Leaders often incorporate systems
    such as unattended ground sensors or unmanned aircraft systems to assist in threat detection in their sectors
    of responsibility. Soldiers build confidence in TA by successfully recognizing various target signatures and
    adapting to the detection challenges of the environment, equipment, and other influential factors.

TARGET SIGNATURES

     3-6. Target signatures are indicators or clues that aid observers in detecting targets. Most threats (personnel,
     weapons, and vehicles) have identifiable signatures, which usually result from their design or the
     environment in which they operate (for example, noise, smoke, dust, a blast, or a flash can all result from the
     firing of a vehicle’s main weapon system). Vehicle movement through built-up areas produces more noise
     than vehicle movement in open spaces. Different types of aircraft have different signatures (for example, a
     hovering helicopter’s signature differs from that of a fixed-wing [FW] aircraft). Other factors affecting target
     signatures are visibility, temperature, and weather conditions.

     3-7. Observers categorize target signatures by threat type and determine their key characteristics by using
     their senses of sight, sound, and smell. Figure 3-3 on page 24 groups common target signatures that Soldiers
     identify by sense and correlates these to each threat type. When composing within the format of the POVA3
     mnemonic, the target signature categories are—

          • Personnel.
          • Obstacles or mines.
          • Vehicles.
          • Antitank.
          • Artillery.
          • Aircraft.

PERSONNEL

     3-8. Areas with potential threat personnel are identifiable by several target signatures. Threat personnel may
     operate in groups with other soldiers or may intermingle with noncombatants. When threat personnel operate
     as a small unit, the resulting personnel target signatures include—

          • Fighting positions with disturbed earth.
          • Small arms spent brass.
          • Ripped or torn clothing.
          • Trash from food items.
          • Footprints in soft or muddy soil.

     3-9. Threat personnel who intermingle with noncombatants are often more difficult to detect. In groups of
     civilians, they are identifiable by the following:

          • Extensive interest in friendly forces or action.
          • Digital recording equipment.
          • Writing or sketching during casual observation of friendly forces.
          • Bulky or bulging clothing (as attempts to conceal weapons or explosives).
          • Inappropriate quantity or quality of clothing items for the given environment.
          • Dark-colored clothing.

OBSTACLES OR MINES

     3-10. Obstacles, mines, and improvised explosive devices (IEDs) are difficult to detect by Soldiers on the
     move. Their target signatures include—

          • Disturbed earth.
          • Discolored ground in small shapes.
          • Small dirt piles.
          • Stacked or piled garbage.
          • Carcasses of larger animals common in the region.

     3-11. Detonated mines or IEDs are identifiable by single or twin large craters, craters with nearby broken
     track blocks or detached wheels, and craters with remaining electrical wires or explosive residue. Soldiers
     maintain awareness of the potential for additional mines or IEDs around apparent passageways around
     previously detonated munitions and craters and related passageways.

VEHICLES

     3-12. Wheeled and tracked vehicles have various target signatures, which are most detectible in open areas
     and on rolling terrain. Their key target signatures include—

          • Wheel or track imprints in soft or muddy soil or loose terrain.
          • Exhaust smoke plumes.
          • Diesel engine noise.
          • Sharp angles in their profiles.

     3-13. Thermal optics can use the heat from the wheels or tracks, engine compartment, and any vehicle
     occupants to define the shape and size of the vehicle for the observer.

ANTITANK

     3-14. Threat antitank positions afford visual coverage of primary avenues of approach—those that tanks and
     personnel carriers (PCs) are most likely to use. The surrounding environment typically serves to mask or
     conceal the positions. During daytime operations, sunlight may reflect off the optics for the guidance systems.
     Soldiers maintain awareness of any thin wires with destroyed vehicles, which can indicate a wire guided
     system. Some target signatures of threat antitank positions are—

          • Glares or reflections from optics or spotter binoculars.
          • Open terrain with few trees on one side of an axis of advance.
          • Higher ground with little or no foliage or trees along the route of movement.
          • Keyhole positions at which vehicles remain stationary for longer than 30 seconds before continuing

                movement.

             Note. Tying obstacles to the fronts of stationary vehicles creates narrow passages that enable
             long-range antitank guided missile engagements of fixed small units or vehicles.

ARTILLERY

     3-15. Target signatures of threat artillery are specific to prior indirect fires in a specific area. (Self-propelled
     and towed artillery remain covered while moving in the vehicle group.) Recent target signatures of indirect
     fires include—

          • Craters, especially linear-type craters.
          • White lingering smoke.
          • Acidic smells.
          • Burning vehicles or artificial objects.
          • Fragmented missile bodies or component assemblies.

     3-16. Rotary-wing (RW) (helicopter) and FW (airplane) aircraft have different target signatures and share

DETECTION CHALLENGES

     3-20. Some threats are more difficult to detect than others. Soldiers remain trained to detect and locate targets,
     and they maintain comprehensive understanding of the detection challenges they face and how to overcome
     those. Characteristics or types of more difficult targets to detect follow:

          • Peripheral (on the edge of the field of view).
          • Camouflaged, masked, innocuous in appearance, or blended into the surrounding environment.
          • Heard but not seen.
          • Small and single (for example, lone dismounted threats, antitank guided missile or rocket-propelled

                grenade firing positions).
          • Small in complex detection environments such as urban or jungle.
          • Under less-than-ideal indirect fire illumination.
          • Mirage effects from high temperatures and heat waves near the ground (heat shimmers present in

                the line of sight).
          • Natural and artificial obscurants or surroundings.
          • Behavioral or physical deficiencies of the observer (for example, fatigue, eye reaction to gun

                flashes).

             Note. As regards indirect fire illumination, when the illumination is in front of the target, the
             resulting shadow is darker than the target. When the illumination is behind the target (and not in
             a position to wash out the crew’s optics), the target tends to remain distinct from the background.
             Soldiers keep one eye closed during their illumination searches and never look directly into
             illumination sources to retain their own night vision.

     3-21. These threat detection challenges are surmountable, but they slow the threat detection process,
     regardless of the Soldier’s level of training. Of equal importance to a Soldier’s understanding is both why
     the challenges occur and how to overcome them.

LIMITED VISIBILITY

     3-22. Current military-issued optics, thermals, sensors, and illuminators may compound threat detection
     challenges by how they function. Leaders and Soldiers prioritize developing their familiarity with assigned
     optics and with which stimulants they are capable of detecting. They develop the abilities to manipulate optic
     controls, switch between WFOV and NFOV, and transition between thermal and image-intensifying optics.
     Soldiers increase their proficiency in employing multiple sensors or optics to detect threats within their
     sectors efficiently, rapidly, and accurately.

ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM

     3-23. The electromagnetic spectrum contains various frequencies and wavelengths of energy (radiation),
     including radio waves, microwaves, IR, visible light, ultraviolet light, x-rays, and gamma rays. To address
     threat detection challenges, understanding how best to employ various types of optics relies on knowledge
     of IR and visible light within the electromagnetic spectrum.

             Note. For this section, the term optics applies to all magnifying glass lenses, thermals, illuminators,

     3-25. Placement of each wavelength of energy in the spectrum occurs from lowest to highest frequency. As
     the frequency changes, the characteristics change. The bundling of energy types into groups of frequencies,
     or bands, with similar characteristics best serves comprehension of those changes.

     3-26. Major concerns for the planning and use of thermal and other optics to aid in threat detection lie in how
     they function and what they allow the user to see. Each device produces a digital representation of the scene
     or view in focus, which it bases on detectable wavelength frequencies from the electromagnetic spectrum.

     3-27. Thermal optic equipment operates in the far to middle bands of the IR range, which is the farthest of
     the IR wavelength frequencies from visible light. Thermal optics cannot translate (or render viewable) visible
     light. They cannot allow the user to see IR equipment such as IR strobes or chemical lights, illuminators, or
     laser pointers. They identify emitted radiation in the form of heat energy only.

     3-28. Image-intensifying optic equipment such as NVDs uses the near band of the IR range, which is closest
     to the visible light wavelength frequency, and visible light to create a digital picture of the view. These
     systems cannot detect heat or heat sources or render them viewable. For example, they cannot detect heat
     from fire. However, they can detect the flames (or light).

Thermal Optics

     3-29. Some camouflaged targets are difficult to acquire with day optics but remain detectable with thermal
     optics. Thermal optics operate within the 3- to 30-micrometer frequency of the IR range. They translate
     emitted radiation (heat) into a digital image, thereby replicating the view with gradient colors that designate
     specific temperatures. Thermal optics cannot discern IR beacons, reverse polarity paper, or other equipment
     radiating outside a 3- to 30-micrometer frequency.

Image-intensifying Optics

     3-30. Image intensifiers are common to AN/PVS-7 or AN/PVS-14 night-vision goggles (NVGs). These
     sensor systems operate within the 700- to 920-nanometer frequency of the IR range. Image-intensifying
     optics are limited to shorter ranges, require ambient light to amplify or intensify the view, and cannot discern
     thermal signatures.

     3-31. Soldiers keep their image-intensifying optic usage to a minimum due to the negative effect on a user’s
     night vision. Proper adjustment, brightness, and donning for the user’s nondominant eye serve as guidelines
     for Soldier use.

             Note. When equipped with thermal optics, Soldiers should use them to acquire targets during all
             visibility conditions. While actively detecting threats, Soldiers switch between other optics and
             sensors (day, thermal, and image-intensifying) to ensure they observe most of the visible light and
             IR ranges.

Hybrid and Enhanced Optics

     3-32. These optics combine the capabilities of thermal (IR) and image-intensifying optics into a single sensor.
     These systems such as the AN/PSQ-20 enhanced NVG enable viewing across a wider spectrum of light. They
     produce a digitally enhanced replication of the view that includes thermal and intensified signatures within
     the 30-micrometer to 400-nanometer range.

             Note. Depending on the equipment, the maximum observable distance may be significantly shorter
             than the maximum effective range (MER) of the employed weapon or weapon system. The
             appropriate use of multiple optics and sensors can manage or mitigate this capability gap between
             the optic and weapon systems.

DETECTION BEST PRACTICES

     3-33. Whether a Soldier is dismounted or mounted, threat detection is a critical skill that requires thoughtful
     application of the sensors, optics, and systems at the Soldier’s disposal. Finding potential threats quickly and
     effectively affords the maximum amount of time to friendly forces to defeat the threat. Leaders and Soldiers
     maintain familiarity with the following best practices to increase threat detection:

          • Scan with the naked eye first and then with magnified optics.
          • Practice using image-intensifying and thermal optics in tandem during limited visibility.
          • Know the difference in what image-intensifying and thermal optics allow the user to see.
          • Practice preference for using thermal optics for TA (detection, identification, and location) and

                threat engagement, day or night.
          • Maintain awareness of the capabilities and limitations of the optics and sensors.
          • Overlap search areas with those of other crew members or small units, but do not focus on the same

                sector.
          • Protect and shield the eyes from the blinding effects of the sun, particularly in light-colored

                environments such as sandy and snowy terrain.
          • Practice extreme light discipline during limited visibility.
          • Think as the threat and search in the most advantageous areas from their perspective.
          • Even though threat detection is much more difficult in a chemical environment, practice detection

                skills with chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear (CBRN) gear and maintain awareness of
                the increased constraints and limitations, day and night.
          • Use map reconnaissance to assist in predetermining potential threat locations.
          • When conducting air searches, focus on a ground feature to regain focus periodically and to prevent
                blurred vision.
          • Know the assigned area of operations (for example, remain capable of recognizing when a road or
                market that is usually crowded suddenly seems empty of that usual traffic or activity).

             Note. In general, straight lines or right angles do not appear in nature, so the size and shape of a
             vehicle can stand out from natural surroundings. Sharp angles and distinct straight lines are
             indicative of an artificial object, even when camouflaged, masked, or hidden.

                                 Identify

Identification is the process of accurately characterizing detected contacts in the operational
environment to support an engagement decision. Following a particular sequence of actions for
identification, observers of a detected contact recognize its target description, assess the level
of threat it presents to the friendly force, identify its model or common name and its variant,
and discriminate its hostile intent as friend, foe, neutral, noncombatant, and unknown. This
systematic approach to achieving combat identification (CID) is a continuous routine and
inherent responsibility of all Soldiers. The first letters of the names of each of the steps—
recognize, assess, identify, and discriminate—form the mnemonic RAID.

Observers, firers, gunners, and leaders at all levels complete the RAID process to determine
the nature and potential hostile intent of detected contacts in the battlespace for the direct
support of an engagement decision. Figure 4-1 highlights the identify function of the kill chain
and relates the RAID steps to the identification process.

PROCESS

     4-1. The identification process has four experience-based steps, RAID, that provide desired tactical
     outcomes. The outcomes are tactically significant intelligence requirements that develop the tactical situation
     and enable the effective use of combat power. They provide multiple checks and balances, which serve to
     reduce or eliminate fratricide and collateral damage during combat operations.

     4-2. The outcomes directly relate to the analyzed and interrogated attributes of a detected contact. Figure 4-2
     and the following outline the outcomes for each of the RAID procedural steps:

          • Recognize—description: The observer reviews the characteristics of the detected contact to
                determine a military description.

          • Assess—threat classification:
                 The observer assesses the threat or danger level the contact’s military description presents to
                     the friendly force.
                 Threat level assessments may recur multiple times during the kill chain.
                 The first time an observer assesses the threat levels of multiple detected contacts’ military
                     descriptions happens immediately after the observer recognizes them.

          • Identify—model and variant:
                 The observer views or perceives the characteristics and capabilities of the components and then
                     uses that information to define the purpose, function, system, and details of the detected contact
                     and to determine its model.
                 Leaders scrutinize the components, characteristics, and capabilities for the specific variant of
                     the model.
                 The model and variant inform the situation template for the commander.

          • Discriminate—hostile intent:
                 The firer or leader confirms the contact’s hostile intent as friend, foe, neutral, noncombatant, or
                     unknown.
                 The successful completion of target confirmation must occur prior to threat engagement with
                     any form of combat power.

     4-3. The observer’s experience and assigned position directly relate to the minimum outputs required. For
     example, a driver who has less experience and authority than the vehicle commander (VC) would also have
     less of a say in the actions taken against a perceived or confirmed threat.

METHOD

     4-6. The RAID procedural steps have observers review components of a contact for characteristics such as
     shape, capabilities such as function and purpose, and other features. Such observations address the technical
     question, What is this contact? The observer’s conclusions serve in the development of sufficient, accurate
     tactical knowledge and understanding of a detected contact to support engagement decisions.

     4-7. The RAID procedural steps have observers interrogate air and land domain contacts for rapid
     determination of the requisite information to support engagement decisions. Interrogation of the frame,
     armament, suspension, turret, equipment, and reactive protection/rotor/wings (known by the
     mnemonic FASTER) components serves the identification of both air and land domain contacts (see
     figure 4-4). Interrogation based on FASTER components is an evolution from previous interrogations of the
     hull, armament, turret, and suspension (known by the mnemonic HATS) and the wings, engine, fuselage, and
     tail (known by the mnemonic WEFT) components. The interrogation of FASTER components integrates all
     equipment, vehicles, and aircraft in one process for efficiency and accuracy.

INTERROGATION

     4-8. When observers detect target signatures while scanning their sectors of responsibility, they immediately
     begin the work to recognize the tactical military significance of the detected contacts. This requires their use
     of certain skills to develop specific understanding of the view or scene.

     4-9. In accordance with the kill chain, observers analyze detected contacts for specific information to develop
     their understanding of what the contacts are. An observer poses a series of questions about the nature of a
     detected contact while engaging active and passive means of discovery and leveraging various, available
     optics, sensors, and digital systems.

             Note. Observers commonly employ the FASTER interrogation method on a detected contact to
             develop and refine intelligence, to determine the contact’s military description, model and variant,
             and hostile intent, and to yield sufficient tactical knowledge to support an engagement decision.

     4-10. Observers employ the FASTER interrogation method on all equipment, vehicles, and FW, RW, and
     uncrewed aircraft. As part of the process, observers scrutinize the viewable or observable components of
     detected contacts to develop tactical understanding and acquire information. Information about each
     component of a detected contact serves in the development of tactical understanding, determination of
     military significance, purpose, and use, and discrimination of hostile intent.

             Note. Components of a detected contact are common segments, workings, mechanisms, or
             assemblies of a detected contact that observers use to interrogate, recognize, and identify the
             contact accurately. They render the determination of the contact’s operational domain easy.

     4-11. The components an observer analyzes with the FASTER interrogation method follow:
          • F—frame—The hull for land domain systems and the fuselage for aircraft.
          • A—armament—Weapons for all equipment, vehicles, and aircraft.
          • S—suspension—Mobility capability of land domain systems and landing gear, skids, or related
                assemblies for aircraft when on the ground.
          • T—turret/tail—Turret for most tracked and wheeled vehicles and tail for FW and RW aircraft.
          • E—equipment—Outfitting for all land and air domain systems.
          • R—reactive protection/rotors/wings—Reactive protection for land domain systems (vehicles) and
                rotors and wings for aircraft.

     4-12. Attributes are useful in developing technical understanding that incrementally refines an observer’s
     answer to the tactical question, What is this? When analyzed, each component may include up to three distinct
     attributes:

          • Characteristics—The shape, size, number, spacing, or basic configuration of a component, the
                analysis of which informs the determination of the contact’s military description.

          • Capabilities—The purpose, function, system, or details of the visible characteristics, the
                interpretation of which informs the identification of the object’s model.

          • Features—Specific physical elements seemingly related to the tactical, engagement, protection,
                maneuver, or communication capability of a component, the changes, additions, alterations, or
                modifications of which increase the component’s capabilities and inform the identification of the
                object’s variant.

ASSESS THE THREAT LEVEL

     4-33. The assess phase of RAID occurs when multiple contacts are detected and recognized. Once observers
     recognize the detected contacts’ military descriptions, they assess the threat or danger levels those present.
     Observers use available information to classify the threat according to the threat or danger level the contacts’
     military descriptions present from the perspective of the friendly force.

             Note. Threat classification is an observer’s determination of the threat or danger level a detected
             contact’s military description presents to the observer’s element. Observers initially consider each
             contact hostile until they discriminate otherwise. They use threat classifications when multiple
             potential threats are present to determine which require immediate attention.

     4-34. Assessing the threat or danger level of the detected contact’s military description yields one of four
     distinct threat classifications. Initially, observers consider contacts hostile (that is, a potential threat) until
     they discriminate otherwise. The four threat classifications follow:

          • Most dangerous:
                 A threat capable of defeating the friendly force and preparing to do so.
                 Units immediately defeat these targets.

          • Dangerous:
                 A threat capable of defeating the friendly force but not preparing to do so.
                 Units defeat these targets after they have eliminated all the most dangerous targets.

          • Least dangerous:
                 A threat currently incapable of defeating the friendly force but coordinating with other threats
                     of a higher classification.
                 Units defeat these targets after they have defeated all the targets with a higher threat
                     classification.

          • High-payoff:
                 A threat of tactical importance to the successful completion of the mission, whose destruction
                     would significantly contribute to the success of the friendly course of action and which
                     commanders typically provide in OPORDs or FRAGORDs as part of the commander’s intent
                     or concept of operations.
                 Units defeat these targets of opportunity after they have eliminated all the most dangerous and
                     dangerous targets.

     element. Not all threats present the same threat or danger level to all friendly force elements.

             Note. For example, the crew of a friendly mine-resistant, ambush-protected vehicle might perceive
             as most dangerous a rocket-propelled grenade team with shoulder-launched munitions oriented at
             them, whereas an Abrams tank commander might classify the same threat as dangerous only.

IDENTIFY THE MODEL AND VARIANT

     4-39. The identification phase of RAID has observers analyze the characteristics, capabilities, and features
     of a recognized object’s components to determine the object’s model or common name and, when possible,
     variant. For example, all aircraft share several identifying features. These features enable observers to
     recognize and accurately identify the aircraft. This section reviews the key elements of a detected contact an
     observer visually interrogates to determine the object’s type, model, and variant.

     4-40. By isolating the components of a detected contact and their characteristics, capabilities, and features,
     the observer accurately and rapidly identifies it, which is critical to the unit’s success in the battlespace. This
     identifying information enables the leader or firer to achieve rounds on the target first, the unit to overmatch
     the threat, and the unit to defeat threats before they deliver combat power against the friendly force.

     4-41. Due to the complexity and usefulness of identifying a model to its specific variant, the identification
     requirement from model to variant is based on the observer’s assigned position within the organization, which
     ties directly to the position’s inherent experience demands. The identification of the model is the first
     experience-based outcome for the identification phase of RAID and the minimum tactical information
     requirement for a Soldier, observer, firer, gunner, or team leader. The difference between the model and
     variant follows:

          • Model—The designator given by the manufacturer or the country of origin (COO) to a system,
                vehicle, aircraft, or piece of military equipment.

          • Variant—The designator supplemental to the base model that further indicates the possible
                modifications in technology, combat power, active or passive defense mechanisms, capabilities, and
                features without instigating a new base model.

     4-42. Most military equipment, regardless of the COO, receives a model designator once it enters service.
     Changes to a model occasion a variant suffix. In the event of a dramatic change, the manufacturer or COO
     may create a new model.

     4-43. In some instances, variants undergo modifications with minor changes that cause them to receive an
     additional suffix. When that occurs, a version identifier may be assigned. Typically, observers cannot discern
     changes from version to version for the base model and variant.

             Note. For example, few visual cues categorically differentiate an M1A2 SEPv2 Abrams main
             battle tank from an M1A2 SEPv3. Although the changes between the versions are significant, at
             a distance, day and night, little visual evidence exists to allow for distinguishing one version from
             the other.

Reactive Protection

     4-61. Recent inclusions in wheeled vehicle designs are passive and active protection systems, additional
     armor plating, modular armor packages, and other protection systems that increase crew survivability and
     vehicle preservation. Figure 4-27 provides examples of common protection systems serving as reactive
     protection components of wheeled vehicles. It includes protection systems that remain viewable at a distance
     (with and without optics and sensors) for the purpose of identifying models or common names and variants
     as follows:

          • Passive systems alert the crew to combat hazards (for example, laser warning, projectile warning)
                and warn the crew of potential direct or indirect fire hazards for their actions or defensive measures.

          • Soft kill systems alert the crew to combat hazards and engage, discharge, or physically move part
                of or the entire vehicle in a defensive manner to reduce or eliminate risk to personnel and equipment.

             Note. In this publication, active protection systems remain specific to soft kill capabilities (for
             example, aerial countermeasures, IR decoys, flares, chaff, radar decoys).

          • Hard kill systems physically counterattack an incoming threat, thereby destroying or altering its
                payload or warhead to the point of severely impeding the intended effect on the target (for example,
                an additional armor package whose function or detonation interrupts, disrupts, or defeats a chemical
                energy round’s jet stream or a long-rod kinetic energy penetrator round, thereby preventing the
                threat from catastrophically damaging the vehicle), and they react to a kinetic strike of sufficient
                force to activate the reactive armor composition.

          • Add-on, augmenting systems for vehicles’ points of vulnerability increase survivability (for
                example, modular armor, field expedient armor, locally manufactured devices).

     4-95. After recognizing the potential threat, assessing the threat or danger level it presents to the friendly
     force, and identifying any vehicle and aircraft models and variants, the firer or leader continues interrogating
     the potential threat to discriminate its hostile intent. Discrimination is the determination of a target as
     friend, foe, neutral, noncombatant, or unknown.

     4-96. The observer does not generally complete the discrimination phase of RAID. Discrimination is a critical
     leader task for sergeants and above in gunner, team leader, and senior positions. It has the leader interrogate
     the detected contact for positive identification. The process ends when the leader can clearly discriminate the
     contact as friend, foe, neutral, noncombatant, or unknown. This determination directly supports any pending
     engagement decision.

JOINT CAPABILITIES RELEASE

     4-99. Joint Capabilities Release (JCR) is the Army’s next generation friendly force tracking system. This
     upgrade builds on the SA tool Force XXI Battle Command, brigade and below (FBCB2), which is fielded to
     every brigade combat team in the U.S. Army. FBCB2 consists of computer hardware and software integrated
     into various platforms at brigade and below. It interconnects platforms through a communications
     infrastructure called the tactical internet, which includes the enhanced position location and reporting
     system (also called EPLRS) and the single-channel ground and airborne radio system (SINCGARS). These
     nets pass SA data and conduct C2. The primary function of FBCB2 is to send and receive automatic position
     location reports, C2 message traffic, and graphics. The collection, integration, and display of a common
     operational picture of the battlespace, which is the same at each user’s display, provide SA.

     4-100. FBCB2 houses a common database with automated friendly positional information. It displays
     graphics for friendly forces, as well as for known or suspected enemy forces. Collectively, the FBCB2 system
     generates the friendly operating picture. It displays relevant information, showing the user’s location, other
     friendly forces’ locations, observed or templated enemy locations, and all known obstacles. It also provides
     standardized reports, allowing leaders to disseminate graphic overlays and written OPORDs and
     FRAGORDs rapidly. The warfighter receives these data pushes from all other battlespace systems to maintain
     real-time battle information. These battlespace systems draw from the reports and positional data passed from
     the lower tactical internet to provide information at higher command levels. They push information such as
     locations of adjacent units, known and templated enemy positions, graphics, and OPORDs to FBCB2 users.

     4-101. Though the system works automatically on vehicles equipped to operate on the tactical internet, it
     does not provide locations to every friendly element in the battlespace. For example, the system will not
     automatically track a dismounted rifle squad operating at extended ranges from an M2A3. Also, it does not
     cover any digitally unequipped units or allied or coalition forces operating next to the platoon. FBCB2 allows
     for the manual insertion of icons for those elements, based on information received from frequency
     modulation radio reports, but the system will not update the icons in real time. For this reason, the leader
     cannot rely solely on FBCB2 to clear fires.

             Note. Observed enemy force locations, neutral organizations, civilians, FBCB2-unequipped
             friendly and coalition units, and other battlespace hazards are not automatically updated in
             FBCB2; changes to these elements’ positions required manual updates by an FBCB2 operator.

                                  Locate

Target location is the determination of the position in relation to the shooter, small unit,
and adjacent units of a discriminated threat in the operational environment. Locating a
target or series of targets is the last step of TA, which includes the detect, identify, and locate
functions (or links) of the kill chain. The overall outcome of the locate function is to know and
relay the location of the discriminated threat(s) and to orient the available weapons or weapon
systems appropriately on those targets (see figure 5-1). Weapons orientation may include
tactical movement, so the maximum armor protection is also oriented toward the hostile threat’s
location.
Upon the successful locating of a target, the rest of the unit receives rapid and efficient
communication of the target’s location. Announcement methods of the target’s location depend
on the individual’s specific position or platform, operation’s graphic control measures, unit’s
SOPs, and available time. This chapter discusses three methods for locating a target,

AREAS

     5-18. Areas provide a general zone or vicinity in which the target is located. They undergo further division
     by cardinal direction, near or far, and left or right modifications to define the location better.

Engagement Area

     5-19. The degree of relatively unobstructed intervisibility lines available to the unit’s weapon systems in their
     firing positions and the maximum range of those weapons both determine the size and shape of the EA.
     Typically, commanders delineate areas of responsibility in the EA by assigning each platoon a sector of fire
     or direction of fire. Paragraphs 5-20 through 5-26 cover these fire control measures. Fire control measures
     are the means by which the commander or subordinate leaders control fires.

Objective

     5-20. The size and shape of an objective is mission specific. This graphic control measure is useful for
     orienting fires and in locating. The objective remains divisible into quadrants, sectors, or other partitions as
     necessary.

Sector

     5-21. A sector of fire is a defined area covered by direct fire. Leaders assign sectors of fire to subordinate
     elements, crew-served weapons, and individual Soldiers to ensure coverage of an area of responsibility.
     Leaders may limit the sector of fire of an element or weapon to prevent accidental engagement of an adjacent
     unit. When assigning sectors of fire, commanders and subordinate leaders consider the number and types of
     available weapons. Also, they consider the acquisition system type and field of view when determining the
     width of a sector of fire. For example, while unaided vision (or the naked eye) has a WFOV, the ability to
     detect and identify targets at certain ranges and in limited visibility conditions is restricted. Conversely, most
     fire control acquisition systems have greater detection and identification ranges than the unaided eye, but
     they have an NFOV. Means of designating sectors of fire include—

          • TRPs.
          • Clock directions.
          • Terrain-based quadrants.
          • Friendly-forces-based quadrants.

Quadrants

     5-22. Quadrants subdivide an area into four separate tracts by a pair of perpendicular axes over the terrain,
     effectively creating a coordinate plane on which to name locations. Quadrants may be based on the terrain,
     friendly forces, or enemy formations.

     5-23. The unit’s SOPs establish the method of quadrant numbering. However, units take extra care to avoid
     confusion during simultaneous use of quadrants based on terrain, friendly forces, and enemy formations.

     5-24. Terrain-based quadrants have a TRP, either existing or constructed, as the origin (intersection) of the
     axes that are dividing the area into four tracts. Both offensive and defensive operations employ this technique.
     In the offense, the commander defines the origin, or the center of the coordinate plane, with an existing
     feature or by creating a reference point (for example, ground-burst illumination round, marking smoke round,
     fire ignited by incendiary or tracer rounds). The axes creating the quadrants run parallel and perpendicular to
     the direction of movement. In the defense, the commander defines the origin with a TRP.

POINTS

     5-27. Soldiers can locate a target by the nearest known point in several ways. Soldiers may use TRPs,
     artificial objects, intersections, terrain features, and other notable identifiers. The most common choices are
     TRPs and artificial structures. Units select their own types of points to use in accordance with their SOPs and
     tactical SOPs.

Target Reference Point

     5-28. Task force units and below use TRPs. TRPs can delineate sectors of fire in an EA. Their designators
     follow the standard target symbols or numbers issued by the fire support officer. TRPs can also constitute
     indirect fire targets.

     5-29. A TRP is a recognizable point on the ground leaders use to orient friendly forces and to focus and
     control direct fires. Leaders designate TRPs at probable enemy locations and along likely avenues of
     approach. These can be natural or artificial points. Friendly units sometimes construct markers to serve as
     TRPs. A TRP may be an established site such as a hill or a building, or it may be a feature of a specific point
     that receives an impromptu designation as the TRP (for example, burning enemy vehicle, smoke generated
     by an artillery round).

     5-30. TRPs should be visible in three observation modes (naked eye, image-intensifying, and thermal optics),
     so they all friendly forces can see them during day and limited visibility conditions. Examples of TRPs
     include—

          • Prominent hill masses.
          • Distinctive buildings.
          • Observable enemy positions.
          • Destroyed vehicles.

Artificial Structure

     5-31. Soldiers can locate a target’s general vicinity by nearby artificial objects. These objects are permanent
     in nature and are not to have been specified as TRPs. Examples include destroyed vehicles, easily identifiable
     structures, intersections of artificial structures with natural terrain features such as a bridge spanning a river,
     and intersections of multiple artificial structures such as roads intersecting.

     5-32. These items are specific to the firer’s perspective, and graphic control measures from the higher element
     typically do not include them. Generally, the range or sketch cards or the sector sketches for internal use by
     a small unit or crew identify these.

LINES

     5-33. Lines for graphic control measures identify specific lateral locations of tactical importance in the
     battlespace. In general, lines are not useful for providing pinpoint locations but for focusing fires on a range
     band in an area of interest, observation, or engagement.

     5-34. Graphic overlays and sketches may incorporate numerous types of lines. For the sake of simplicity, this
     section lists only the most common. Units use other line types or functions as needed to best articulate
     location information to the friendly force.

Maximum Engagement Line

     5-35. The maximum engagement line (MEL) is the depth of the sector and is normally limited to the MERs
     of the weapon systems. However, the target description and the effects of terrain also influence it. Slope,
     vegetation, structures, and other terrain features provide cover and concealment that may prevent a weapon
     from reaching its MER. The use of a map assists in determining the distance to each MEL and ensures the
     accurate depiction of the MELs on DA Form 5517.

     5-36. An MEL serves several purposes. Commanders use one to prevent Soldiers from engaging targets
     beyond the MERs of their weapon systems, to define criteria for triggers, and to delineate the maximum
     extent of the battlespace on the sector sketch. It also decreases needless and ineffective ammunition
     expenditure during an engagement.

Restrictive Fire Line

     5-37. A restrictive fire line is a linear fire control measure that prohibits engagement beyond it without
     coordination. In the offense, the commander may establish one to prevent a base of fire element from firing
     into the area in which an assaulting element is maneuvering.

     5-38. This technique is particularly important when armored vehicles support the maneuver of Infantry
     squads. In the defense, the commander may establish a restrictive fire line to prevent the unit from engaging
     a friendly rifle squad positioned in restricted terrain on the flank of an avenue of approach.

Phase Line

     5-39. A PL indicates a lateral separation of an area by terrain or by a change in operational function, in
     maneuver, or in control. Leaders associate PLs to terrain features, mission requirements, and maneuver tactics
     as necessary.

     5-40. PL names from an operational overlay provide a standard method for articulating target locations. PL
     naming conventions from the unit’s tactical SOPs provide rapid situational understanding without map or
     overlay references and assist in the development of an image of the developing situation in relation to the
     larger operation.

Final Protective Fire

     5-41. Final protective fire serves as a clear barrier on the operational overlay. This prearranged barrier of fire
     impedes enemy movement across a defensive line or area. Typically, the use of this barrier allows for the
     retrograde or tactical maneuver of a force while other elements mass fires to deny the enemy the freedom of
     maneuver. Crew-served weapons, small units, and crews may have a specific area or a portion of the final
     protective line to orient all available direct fire assets.

Final Protective Line

     5-42. The final protective line is a selected line of fire where an enemy assault is to be checked by interlocking
     fire from all available weapons and obstacles (FM 3-90). The unit reinforces this line with protective
     obstacles and with final protective fire whenever possible. The initiation of final protective fire is the signal
     for elements, crews, and individual Soldiers to shift fires to their assigned portions of the final protective line.
     They spare no ammunition in repelling the enemy assault, a particular concern for machine guns and other
     automatic weapons.

Once elements detect and discriminate threats, leaders develop the situation in their sectors of
responsibility (see figure 6-1). Leaders make decisions based on the composition, disposition,
and availability of combat power. With these variables, leaders prioritize the threats and
confirm all the given information is correct. Without situational development, the likelihood of
target overkill, fratricide, and collateral damage increases while that of survivability decreases.
The situational development process is a series of progressive and interdependent steps (or
actions) toward making ROE decisions. Leaders are ultimately responsible for the actions of
their elements. However, all Soldiers have the ethical responsibility to identify and discriminate
the threats they intend to engage.

COMBAT POWER

     6-4. Combat power is the total means of destructive and disruptive force that a military unit/formation can
     apply against an enemy at a given time (JP 3-0). It is the selection of the appropriate measures to defeat or
     eliminate a given threat or series of threats. When selecting the appropriate combat power to employ against
     threats, leaders or Soldiers consider the tactical situation, available weapons and ammunition, type of threat,
     range to the target, survivability, any applicable engagement criteria, commander’s intent, and ROE.

     6-5. When selecting the appropriate combat power (see figure 6-3), leaders and Soldiers choose the weapon,

          • Eliminate fratricide, given the proximity of friendly forces, noncombatants, and neutral forces.
          • Minimize friendly exposure to the threat.

METHOD OF ENGAGEMENT

     6-13. Friendly forces deliver their combat power to the known threats in the EA by way of the method of
     engagement. It identifies the way in which the team, crew, squad, or collective small unit is to engage the
     targets from a collective standpoint, as well as any specific techniques the leader believes will solve the
     presented threat problem most efficiently.

     6-14. The selected combat power may be a combination of direct, indirect, and external warfighting
     functions. An established method of engagement defines which weapon systems to employ to engage what
     targets. This enables the crew to destroy multiple targets, thereby increasing the likelihood of their
     overmatching the threat, affording swift engagements from target to target, and ultimately increasing the
     unit’s lethality.

     6-15. An understanding of the tactical situation and presented threats assists the leader in determining the
     method of engagement against those threats. (See figure 6-6.) The leader mentally determines the—

          • Fire pattern that maximizes the effects on the target, based on force capabilities and the presented
                tactical array, while minimizing the exposure of the friendly force to the threat.

          • Engagement techniques the direct fire weapon systems employ to maximize the effects on the target
                while minimizing the exposure of the friendly force to the threat.

                      Figure 6-6. Determination of the method of engagement

6-16. Fire patterns and engagement techniques are useful for the effective and efficient distribution of fires
within teams, crews, squads, sections, and platoons. A small unit’s tactical SOPs describe the standard
defaults it is to use. In the defense, those defaults may undergo revision in the orders process or upon the
leader’s demand as the tactical situation dictates.

6-17. Selecting the appropriate fire pattern and engagement techniques for the tactical situation allows the
unit to mass its fires to achieve decisive results. Massing entails the focus of fires on critical points and the
distribution of effects. A random application of fires is unlikely to have a decisive effect, making for a
piecemeal and disjoined engagement. For example, concentrating the unit’s fires at a single target may ensure
the target’s destruction or suppression, but that engagement technique will fail to achieve a decisive effect
on the overall enemy formation or position.

6-18. Conversely, selecting the wrong fire pattern or engagement techniques may result in target overkill.
Target overkill wastes ammunition and ties up weapons better employed in other target acquisitions and
threat engagements. The requirement to destroy the greatest threats first tempers the idea of having every
weapon engage a different target.

FIRE PATTERN

     6-19. Leaders of collective small units select a fire pattern for the element to follow with the prescribed or
     common engagement techniques. The most common fire patterns are—

          • Frontal fire:
                 Typical use is against multiple targets arrayed in front of the unit in a lateral configuration.
                 Weapon systems engage targets to their respective fronts.

          • Cross fire—Use is against targets arrayed laterally across the unit’s front in a manner permitting
                diagonal fires at the enemy’s flank or against obstructions in the battlespace that prevent the unit’s
                use of frontal fire.

          • Depth fire:
                 Use is against targets dispersed in-depth and perpendicular to the unit.
                 The friendly unit’s weapons in the center of the formation engage the closest targets; the flank
                     weapons engage deeper targets.
                 As the unit destroys targets, weapons shift fire toward the center of the enemy formation.

ENGAGEMENT TECHNIQUE

     6-20. Engagement techniques are effects-oriented fire distribution measures. In addition to employing a
     fire pattern, the leader may employ one or a combination of multiple engagement technique(s) to distribute
     fires. The most common engagement techniques are—

          • Simultaneous fire.
          • Alternating fire.
          • Observed fire.
          • Point fire.
          • Area fire.
          • Sequential fire.
          • Time of suppression.
          • Reconnaissance by fire.

Simultaneous Fire

     6-21. Simultaneous (or volley) fire rapidly masses the effects of fires and can gain fire superiority. For
     example, a unit may initiate a support by fire operation with simultaneous fire and then revert to alternating
     or sequential fire to maintain suppression. Simultaneous fire also negates the low probability of the hit and
     kill of certain antiarmor weapons. For example, a rifle squad may employ simultaneous fire with its
     AT4s (antitank) to ensure rapid destruction of a BMP engaging a friendly position.

Alternating Fire

     6-22. For alternating fire, a pair of elements continuously engage the same point or area target, one at a time.
     All size elements, from buddy team through company team, may accomplish alternating fire. Alternating fire
     permits a unit to maintain suppression for a longer duration than does simultaneous fire. It also forces the
     enemy to acquire and engage alternating points of fire.

Observed Fire

     6-23. Observed fire allows for mutual observation and assistance while protecting the location of the
     observing elements. Typically, units in a protected defensive position use observed fire when engagement
     ranges are greater than 2,500 meters. This allows the least amount of friendly exposure to the threat while
     surgically eliminating targets at the farthest tactically relevant range. Ground and air elements alike employ
     observed fire. Examples include a tank element lasing and observing while a Bradley fighting vehicle element
     engages, or a ground element lasing and observing for an attack air element.

Point Fire

     6-24. Point fire concentrates the effects of fires against a specific, identified target such as a vehicle, machine
     gun bunker, or antitank guided missile position. When leaders direct point fire, all the unit’s weapons engage
     the target, firing until either the target is destroyed or the required time of suppression expires. Employing
     converging fires from dispersed positions makes point fire more effective because the target undergoes
     engagement from multiple directions. The unit may initiate an engagement with point fire against the most
     dangerous threat and then revert to area fire against less threatening point targets.

Area Fire

     6-25. Area fire distributes the effects of fires over an area with numerous or obscure enemy positions. For a
     large area, leaders assign sectors of fire to subordinate elements using a terrain-based distribution method
     such as quadrants. Typically, the primary purpose of area fire is suppression. However, sustaining effective
     suppression requires judicious control of the rate of fire.

Sequential Fire

     6-26. For sequential fire, the subordinate elements of a unit engage the same point or area target, one after
     another, in an arranged sequence. For example, a mechanized Infantry platoon may sequence the fires of its
     four Bradley fighting vehicles to gain maximum time of suppression. Sequential fire can also help to prevent
     the waste of ammunition, as when an Infantry rifle platoon waits to see the effects of the first Javelin before
     firing another. Additionally, sequential fire permits elements who have already fired to pass on the
     information they learned from the engagement. For example, a Soldier who missed a BMP with an AT4
     forwards the range and lead information for the next Soldier to make appropriate range and lead adjustments
     when engaging the target with another AT4.

Time of Suppression

     6-27. Time of suppression is the commander-specified, requisite period to suppress an enemy position or
     force. Suppression time typically depends on the time it will take a supported element to maneuver. Normally,
     a unit suppresses an enemy position by the sustained rate of fire of its automatic weapons. In planning for
     sustained suppression, leaders consider several factors: the estimated time of suppression, size of the area
     undergoing suppression, type of target for suppression, range to the target, rates of fire, and available
     ammunition quantities.

Reconnaissance by Fire

     6-28. Reconnaissance by fire is a technique in which a unit fires on a suspected enemy position (FM 3-90),
     the process of engaging possible enemy locations to elicit a tactical response such as return fire or movement.
     Such a response supports the commander and subordinate leaders in accurate TA and in their ability to mass
     fires against the enemy element. Typically, a commander directs a subordinate element to conduct
     reconnaissance by fire. For example, a commander may direct an overwatching platoon to conduct
     reconnaissance by fire against a probable enemy position before initiating the movement of a bounding
     element.

                                                  DANGER

                    To prevent fratricide, a crew never engages a target without its
                    discrimination as foe.

                    Instances may arise in which friendly, neutral, or noncombatant
                    entities appear to have or are actively participating with hostile
                    intent. In such cases, the leader is responsible for making effective
                    decisions that prevent fratricide, reduce or eliminate harm to
                    noncombatants and neutral actors, and reduce or eliminate
                    collateral damage. Leaders maintain clear understanding of the
                    tactical situation and of the nature of the battlespace in which they
                    operate. They maintain the capability of employing nonlethal
                    methods, tactical movement, or actions to eliminate potential or
                    prevent imminent fratricide.

TARGET CONFIRMATION

     6-29. All personnel are proactive in reducing the risks of fratricide and noncombatant casualties. Many tools
     assist in preventing fratricide (for example, training for identifying combat vehicles and aircraft; a unit’s
     weapon safety posture [an ammunition handling instruction that allows the commander to control the
     safety of the unit’s weapons precisely]; the WCS, recognition markings, and common operational picture
     employing solid tactics, techniques, and procedures that the unit’s SOPs reinforce). Yet, the primary means
     of preventing fratricide and noncombatant casualties are applying the kill chain, understanding friendly force
     markings and symbols, and properly employing and applying the ROE.

     6-30. As part of target confirmation during the decide function (or link) of the kill chain, the leader making

          • Appropriate selection of combat power.

APPROPRIATE COMBAT POWER

     6-35. The leader selects the appropriate combat power to use against the threat and verifies the combat power
     is indeed appropriate. The goal of all leaders is the judicious use of available combat power to manage
     munitions throughout the longer mission. The appropriate combat power also reduces target overkill, which
     wastes ammunition resources vital to mission success.

     6-36. Using the appropriate weapon for the target increases the probability of rapid enemy destruction or
     suppression and saves ammunition. The unit has many weapons for engaging the enemy and an equal variety
     of targeting sensors and optics. The key factors for determining the appropriate weapon and ammunition are
     the type of target, range to the target, amount of friendly exposure to the threat, available weapons and
     ammunition, and desired target effects.

     6-37. Leaders consider individual crew and small unit capabilities when deciding on the employment of
     weapons and munition types. The commander task-organizes and arrays forces based on the terrain, enemy,
     and desired effects of fires. For example, when the commander expects an enemy infantry assault in restricted
     terrain, the commander employs the Infantry squads, taking advantage of their ability to engage numerous,
     fast-moving targets most effectively. Subordinate units can then concentrate on their target sets and use
     adjacent or available warfighting functions more effectively.

DISCRIMINATION OF FOES

     6-38. Target discrimination is a leader’s legal and ethical responsibility. Therefore, when a leader is unsure,
     based on the weapon posture, WCS, and ROE, the leader also has a legal and an ethical obligation to fire at
     known threats only.

     6-39. The leader maintains awareness of the current battlespace’s situational understanding systems that are
     available to the friendly and allied forces who are operating in the leader’s sector of responsibility. Other
     technological elements such as JCR may enhance these systems, and the Soldiers’ procedures within the
     small unit may support them. (See chapter 4 for more detailed information on the discrimination process.)

TARGET IDENTIFICATION

     6-40. Target identification is the accurate and timely characterization of a detected contact in the battlespace
     as friend, foe, neutral, noncombatant, or unknown. This aspect of CID is time sensitive and serves as the
     basis for a Soldier’s decision to engage or not. The two types of target identification are cooperative and
     non-cooperative. In direct fire situations, regardless of the system the Soldier is using to identify a target, the
     Soldier makes visual positive identification before engaging the target.

Cooperative Target Identification

     6-41. Cooperative target identification technologies send a query and receive the response, I am a friend.
     Cooperative target identification systems may be high technology such as query systems that electronically
     identify friendly elements, or they may be as low of technology as a sentry issuing a challenge and awaiting
     a password. Cooperative target identification directly addresses fratricide avoidance by positively identifying
     friendly elements.

     6-42. Cooperative target identification provides an affirmative identification of friendly units equipped as
     well with cooperative target identification technology. However, the absence of a friendly response does not
     substantiate a target discrimination of foe. The Soldier would still need to use the direct fire engagement
     process to identify targets that the cooperative target identification technology has not discriminated as friend.
     Cooperative target identification technologies are never to serve as the sole criterion for engaging a target.
     Mechanical issues or enemy action can render the cooperative target identification technology inoperative,
     or friendly units or allies in the operational environment may not possess compatible cooperative target
     identification technology. With or without cooperative target identification technology, Soldiers are never to
     engage unknown contacts in the battlespace before they make visual positive identification.

     6-43. Current cooperative target identification technologies include identification, friend or foe systems and
     battlefield target identification device and RBCI systems. None of the cooperative target identification
     technologies currently in use or under development truly discriminate friend or foe; they can discriminate
     friend or unknown only.

Non-cooperative Target Identification

     6-44. Non-cooperative target identification devices support target classification by exploiting the physical
     characteristics of the detected contact. They require no cooperative action or response on the part of the
     detected contact. They may be active or passive and employ an array of technologies, including those that
     support the detection and analysis of signal, noise, and radiation emissions or the determination and analysis
     of physical dimensions and characteristics.

     6-45. Non-cooperative target identification systems include—
          • Optics (forward-looking infrared, NVGs, and binoculars).
          • JCIMSs for vehicles and personnel:
                 CIP.
                 TIP.
                 IR beacon.
                 DCIMS.
                 Automated target recognition device.
          • JCIMSs for use with forward-looking infrared optics and NVGs, which assist in friendly
                identification at the point of engagement.

     6-46. Both cooperative and non-cooperative target identification methods are tools for enhancing the direct
     fire engagement process. However, they are subject to error and require Soldiers to make visual positive
     identification before engaging the target.

POSITIVE IDENTIFICATION

     6-47. Positive identification is an identification derived from observation and analysis of target
     characteristics, including visual recognition; electronic support systems; non-cooperative target recognition
     techniques; identification, friend or foe systems; or other physics-based identification techniques (JP 3-01).
     It is the culminating result of target discrimination and identification that enables the leader to make an
     engagement decision.

6-49. Effective fire control requires a unit to acquire the target and mass the effects of fires rapidly to achieve
decisive results in the close fight. When planning and executing direct fires, the commander and subordinate
leaders know how to apply several fundamental principles.

6-50. The principles of direct fire do not exist to restrict subordinates’ actions. Applied correctly, these
principles help the firing element accomplish its primary goal in direct fire engagement: to acquire first and
shoot first. In this way, subordinates retain the freedom to act quickly upon target acquisition. This section
emphasizes the following principles:

     • Plan for extreme limited visibility conditions.
     • Develop contingencies for diminished capabilities.

6-51. Units increase their survivability by allowing their own exposure to the threat, but only so far as is
necessary to engage the enemy effectively. Defilade, whether natural or artificial, provides the best cover
from kinetic energy direct fire munitions. Crews and squads minimize their exposure by constantly seeking
effective, available cover, attempting to engage the enemy from the flank, remaining dispersed, firing from
multiple positions, and limiting engagement times.

6-52. At night, limited visibility fire control equipment enables the unit to engage enemy forces at similar
ranges as those achieved during daylight operations. Obscurants such as dense fog, heavy smoke, and
blowing sand can reduce the capabilities of thermal and image-intensifying optics and sensors. Units develop
contingency plans for such extreme limited visibility conditions.

6-53. Although decreased acquisition capabilities have minimal effect on area fire, point target engagements
occur at decreased ranges. Typically, firing positions, whether offensive or defensive, adjust closer to the
area or point on which the commander intends to emphasize fires. Another alternative is to use visual or IR
illumination when insufficient ambient light exists for image-intensifying optics and sensors to function to
their maximum capabilities.

6-54. Leaders initially develop plans based on their unit’s maximum capabilities. They make alternate plans
for implementation in the event of casualties and weapon damage or failure. While leaders cannot anticipate
or plan for every situation, they can develop plans for what they view as the most probable occurrences.

FIRE COMMANDS

     7-6. A fire command is a structured order from an authorized leader to deliver combat power against a
     confirmed target or a series of confirmed targets. A fire command serves to control and synchronize the
     conduct of the firing of weapons (known as conduct of fire).

     7-7. There are three types of fire commands: initial, subsequent, and supplemental. They establish a common
     language and framework (that is, a conduct of fire) for application during engagements of single or multiple
     threats. The common language and framework assists coordinated efforts to adjust direct fires and thereby to
     achieve the desired effects on the targets.

INITIAL FIRE COMMAND

     7-8. An initial fire command initiates a direct fire engagement on a confirmed target. In general terms, the
     initial fire command contains all the requisite elements to answer the following questions:

          • Who is firing (alert)?
          • What weapons and ammunition are in use (weapon/ammunition)?
          • What target (target description) is being engaged?
          • How is the target being engaged (method or technique)?
          • Where is the target located (direction or elevation)?
          • How far is the target from the firer (range)?
          • What controls are in place to manage the fire (controls)?
          • When are the weapons to be fired, or when are the firers to engage (command of execution)?

SUBSEQUENT FIRE COMMAND

     7-9. A subsequent fire command directs the firing element to continue engaging the same target by delivering
     subsequent rounds. After receiving the evaluation results of the effects of fire, a leader may give subsequent
     fire commands after any fire command. Leaders give them under the following conditions:

          • The previous round or burst does not achieve the desired effect on the target.
          • Despite the successful achievement of an effect on a target, additional rounds are necessary to

                suppress other less dangerous threats in the same vicinity.

SUPPLEMENTAL FIRE COMMAND

     7-10. A supplemental fire command transitions a firer to another target (that is, one different than the target
     just engaged) within an array during an engagement with multiple targets. The transition to another target
     may involve changes in the employed weapon and ammunition. When leaders decide to transition to another
     target, they give supplemental fire commands after the execution of any other fire command. Supplemental
     commands contain all the elements necessary to deliver the following:

          • Direct the gunner or firer onto a new target (target description).
          • Direct any change of weapon or ammunition type (weapon/ammunition).
          • Issue the order to commence firing (command of execution).

FIRE COMMAND USE IN CONDUCT OF FIRE

     7-11. The small unit leader or VC is responsible overall for the conduct of fire during all engagements. Firers
     are responsible for all the rounds fired from their weapons or weapon systems.

     7-12. A fire command (initial, subsequent, and supplemental) with an authorized command of execution must
     be given for each threat engaged. Leaders and firers must confirm the hostile character of each target prior
     to engaging it. Small unit leaders must use fire commands to authorize subordinates to engage.

EFFECTS

     7-34. The ninth element of a fire command concerns the effects on the target. Evaluating the recent direct
     fire engagement is useful for determining the effects on the target and whether additional rounds are
     necessary to achieve the desired outcome. Further, it alerts the Soldier, team, crew, or small unit of changes
     to the current engagement and to prepare for additional commands from the leader.

             Note. The effects element depends upon an evaluation of the direct fire already delivered against
             the threat. The evaluation assesses the strike of the round, the kill indicators present at the time of
             impact, and the resulting level of destruction. When able, the leader provides this element to the
             crew. However, when a leader is unable to make the proper assessment, the gunner may provide
             this element. In that case, the element supports the leader’s tactical understanding and subsequent
             engagement decision.

     7-35. Leaders and firers visually confirm the effects of fire to build sufficient tactical information to enable
     subsequent engagement decisions. This evaluation includes observation of the strike of the round or its
     perceived point of impact in relation to the target, the actions of the threat once struck with those rounds, and
     a determination of the threat’s remaining military capability. Generally, the sensing is a short description that
     supports the leader’s determination of the tactical engagement decision with the highest probability of
     protecting the force and reducing or eliminating the threat.

     7-36. Regardless of the effects on the target, the minimum acceptable result is hitting the target. Against
     dismounted targets, the desired outcome may be suppression since it affords the friendly force time to
     maneuver to a position of advantage from which it can defeat the threat. In general, suppression of
     dismounted targets and hits on all other types of targets create significant tactical events to alter the threat’s
     engagement decision process. This, in turn, can provide the friendly force with more time to reduce or
     eliminate the threat.

     7-37. The threat’s engagement decision process or kill chain is comparable to that of the U.S. Army. In a
     continuous cycle, threat elements perform the same actions to detect, identify, locate, decide to engage,
     engage, and assess the effects of their fires on their targets.

     7-38. Interrupting the enemy’s kill chain equates with suppressing the target. Yet, upon target suppression,
     the enemy may return to the beginning of their engagement decision process. Nevertheless, this still allows
     the friendly force time to reposition and to deliver more effective fires on the target. Suppressive fires may
     continue, depending on how rapidly the threat returns to the engage function (or link) of their kill chain, or
     they may continue until the friendly force succeeds in neutralizing or destroying the threat.

Sensing

     7-41. A sensing is the oral response to a round or series of rounds (such as machine gun burst) from the firer,
     VC, or leader regarding where rounds struck or passed targets in relation to the target aiming points. When
     the first round or burst fails to destroy the target, a sensing enables the small unit or crew to adjust fire for
     subsequent rounds when directed. When part of the collective kill chain, this assessment provides a
     standardized reporting function to the next higher leader. This, in turn, supports the development of the
     situation report to the higher headquarters on the actions taken, status of the friendly forces, and estimated
     effects of the engagement.

     7-42. Sensing effectively describes the impact of the rounds in relation to the target. The following are
     standard descriptions of the impact:

          • Doubtful (left or right):
                 The observed impacts are to the flanks of the target.
                 This assessment generally leads to a subsequent fire command for the firing element.

          • Lost:
                 The firer or leader either could not observe or determine the impact.
                 Lost rounds typically pass over the intended target.
                 This assessment generally requires a more drastic adjustment in a subsequent fire command to
                     bring the round either on target or on a more observable point (so the firer can assess its impact,
                     and the leader or firer can then make more accurate adjustments).

          • Over—The observed impacts (typically above the target or of tracers) indicate a need to drop
                subsequent rounds (lower the elevation) a known amount to achieve impacts on the target.

          • Short:
                 The observed strikes are in front of the target.
                 This assessment generally requires a subsequent fire command to correct an error in the range
                     to the target or made by the firer or crew.

          • Target:
                 The observed strikes are on the intended target.
                 After this assessment, the leader ascertains the effects of fire by observing the threat’s actions
                     immediately after the strike.

     7-43. Sensings are not the only information leaders use to make their next engagement decisions. The type
     of target, desired outcome, sensing, and threat’s actions provide actionable intelligence for the leader. Leaders
     use the kill indicators to determine whether the direct fires succeeded in suppressing or killing the threat.

Kill Indicators

     7-44. When assessing the effects of combat power against a threat, the firer and leader look for signs at the
     target’s location that indicate a change to the threat’s military significance based on the threat type by domain.

     7-45. Kill indicators for tracked and wheeled vehicle targets can indicate a change in their hostile intent.
     Examples of these kill indicators follow:

          • Vehicle stops moving.
          • Vehicle stops firing.
          • Vehicle is smoking from what appears to be internal damage.
          • Vehicle explodes.
          • Crew abandons the vehicle.

     7-46. The clear observance of kill indicators does not necessarily validate assumptions or confirm
     conclusions. For example, a change in the movement of the target does not indicate the successful
     achievement of a kill but may indicate the successful suppression of the threat. When a vehicle stops moving,
     a leader may decide to reengage in the event the vehicle has maintained the capability to engage with direct
     fires, even if in a degraded capacity. For a second example, when a round or missile hits an armored vehicle,
     an observable explosion with flash occurs. This is the effect of the round impacting the target, not necessarily
     an indication of damage to the target.

Level of Destruction

     7-47. Different characteristics for an adequately engaged target apply when assessing the effects on the target.
     The leader uses specific indicators to evaluate the battlespace. Levels of destruction aid the leader in
     determining any subsequent engagement decision. These are suppression and mobility, firepower, combined,
     and catastrophic kills.

Suppression
     7-48. The confirmation of achieved suppression occurs when the firer cannot observe any strikes on the
     target. Yet, the target has reacted to the contact, and consequently, the threat has altered their engagement
     decision process.
     7-49. The primary objective of suppressive fire is to get the enemy to keep their heads down, which reduces
     their abilities to shoot, move, or observe within their surroundings. Suppressive fire can be aimed directly at
     targets (for example, an enemy vehicle) or at an area target (for example, a tree line where enemy troops are
     hiding).

                                                 WARNING

                    Suppression is applicable to dismounted targets only.

     7-50. For the suppression of an area target, the duration of conduct is based on the situation’s mission variable
     requirements, METT-TC (I). The unit maintains its suppression of the target until either it gains a tactical
     advantage, or another element closes with the target and destroys it. Other elements close with the enemy
     only after the conduct of coordination and the lifting and shifting of fires.
     7-51. Suppressive fire has three purposes:

          • To kill or destroy targets as quickly as possible.
          • To stop or alter the threat’s engagement decision process.
          • To allow the maneuver force both to locate and deliver fire on the threat position more accurately.

Mobility Kill
     7-52. Mobility kills have been successful when the effects of fire degrade a threat’s ability to move under its
     own power, but the threat is still able to use weapon systems. This does not apply to dismounted targets (see
     paragraphs 7-48 through 7-51).

Firepower Kill
     7-53. Firepower kills prevent a threat’s use of weapon systems. This does not apply to dismounted targets
     (see paragraphs 7-48 through 7-51).

Combined Kill
     7-54. Combined kills have been successful when both a mobility and firepower kill render an enemy vehicle
     ineffective for combat. This does not apply to dismounted targets (see paragraphs 7-48 through 7-51).

Catastrophic Kill
     7-55. Catastrophic kills effect the total loss of enemy weapon systems, mobility, and all onboard equipment.
     This applies to all target types. The sensing of a catastrophic kill announces TARGET, TARGET. The
     duplicated sensing announcement declares a catastrophic kill.

RESPONSES OF A FIRE COMMAND

     7-59. In addition to the nine elements of a fire command, the latter includes responses for responding to,
     confirming an action for, adding to, repeating, and correcting a fire command. Each Soldier in the small unit
     or crew has specific duties to perform in response to certain elements of a fire command. The responses and
     actions of each vary by situation but are essential components to the overall efficiency and effectiveness of
     the small unit or crew. The three response classification groups are modifier, clarification, and movement.

RESPONSE

     7-60. Responses are confirmations of the Soldier’s or crewmember’s understanding of the fire command or
     completion of an implied or directed task from the fire command. They can also provide information to the
     small unit or crew. These responses vary by small unit, weapon system, and echelon. They are essential
     components of the fire commands and increase the tempo of the engagement by providing rapid confirmation
     of action to the leader.

     7-61. Responses may be oral declarations, actions, or a combination of both. The involvement of ballistics
     or safety requires an action and an oral response. (See TC 3-20.31-043 for more information.)

MODIFIER

     7-62. Modifiers define which target of multiple targets to engage first. They become mandatory for the
     method element of the fire command when multiple targets are present.

     7-63. When there are multiple targets, the requirement for clarity in target identification and accuracy in
     target description for first engagement, regardless of the firer, increases for leaders. When leaders stipulate
     modifiers to firers, the firers repeat the given modifier when they identify the target. For example, if the VC
     announces GUNNER, SABOT, TWO TANKS, LEFT TANK, the gunner then identifies the target,
     determines the correct range to the target, and responds to the VC’s fire command. In this case, the gunner
     would respond with IDENTIFIED, LEFT TANK, ONE SIX HUNDRED. This response serves as a
     confirmation of the modifier, especially when the VC is not able to view the firer’s actions through an
     associated optic.

     7-64. Some other possible modifiers are—
          • NEAR, FAR.
          • LEFT, RIGHT, CENTER.
          • STATIONARY, MOVING, DEFILADE.
          • LEFT TO RIGHT, RIGHT TO LEFT:
                 It directs the gunner to engage the identified targets in a certain order that may differ from the
                     standard of most dangerous to least dangerous.
                 Its most common use is during collective fire with a section or platoon element.
                 Its typical use is in a section or platoon fire command to adhere to that higher element’s
                     instruction or the platoon’s SOPs.
          • NEAR TO FAR, FAR TO NEAR:
                 It directs the gunner to engage the identified targets in a certain order that may differ from the
                     standard of most dangerous to least dangerous.
                 Its most common use is during collective fire with a section or platoon element.
                 Its typical use is in a section or platoon fire command to adhere to that higher element’s
                     instruction or the platoon’s SOPs.

CLARIFICATION

     7-65. Clarification responses allow Soldiers to announce when they have not understood an element (or any
     portion therein) of a fire command. The Soldier requiring clarification announces the name of the element
     type that is unclear. For example, a Soldier who has not heard the ammunition element announces AMMO.
     The leader responds SABOT to this request for clarification.

MOVEMENT

     7-66. Movement responses provide guidance or direction to the small unit or crew on required movements
     for the engagement. Dismounted, mounted, or collective elements use movement responses within their
     tactical situations. The actual response terminology the elements use is typically common to the organization,
     basic tactics or movement techniques, or their SOPs.

TERMINATION

     7-67. Although terminations of direct fire engagements are not an element of a fire command, every direct
     fire engagement is terminated with the announcement of CEASEFIRE. This announcement requires all firers
     who hear it to stop firing immediately. Normally, leaders issue the order to terminate firing once they
     determine their sectors are clear of threats.

     7-68. The termination to any fire command will come from one of three sources: a Soldier, the leader, or a
     higher authority. A delivered termination cannot be overridden, regardless of the situation.

DELIVERY BY SOLDIER

     7-69. A Soldier announces CEASEFIRE after the observation of any unsafe or hazardous conduct that must
     cease. Any Soldier may announce CEASEFIRE for any act that is injuring or killing friendly, noncombatant,
     or neutral persons or that is damaging equipment. After the cessation of all fire, Soldiers then announce their
     position or identity and state the reason for their CEASEFIRE announcement.

DELIVERY BY LEADER

     7-70. The leader issues a termination at the reduction or elimination of the threat to avoid the expenditure of
     additional combat power. The leader may also announce CEASEFIRE to prevent actions or unsafe conduct
     from injuring friendly, noncombatant, or neutral persons or from damaging equipment.

DELIVERY BY HIGHER AUTHORITY

     7-71. The tactical situation, commander’s intent, available munitions, and threat activity drive a higher
     authority’s announcement of CEASEFIRE. When a subordinate leader receives the command to cease fire,
     the leader notifies all subordinates who are actively engaging targets to cease all direct fires. Once all
     subordinates are in confirmed red (hold) status, the leader reports their weapon posture to the higher authority.

     7-72. In the event of an administrative cease-fire, typically during live fire training events, the higher
     authority may announce CEASEFIRE FREEZE. Immediately, any firing subordinate units are to stop all
     movement, cease all firing, and leave all firing controls, ballistic solutions, ammunition, and weapon
     azimuths and superelevations in their immediate state. Units may place weapons in mechanical and electrical
     safe modes, provided the procedures they use do not move the weapons from their firing positions or change
     the ballistic solutions or fire control information. The announcement of CEASEFIRE FREEZE is generally
     for a significant live fire event that requires immediate attention and possible investigation.

TERMS OF REFERENCE

     7-73. An understanding of specific terms will contextualize the concepts presented within this chapter.
     TC 3-20.31-043 further discusses the following specific terms:

          • Conduct of fire—The specific orders, directions, phrases, terms, modifiers, actions, reactions,

          • Fire command—A standardized format containing up to nine elements used by a team or crew to
                deliver direct fire against a threat.

          • Engaged target—A threat that has been struck or received direct fires, but the round(s) did not

          • Serviced target—A target that has been struck by direct fire and achieved the desired effects.
          • Confirmed target—A detected contact that has been interrogated sufficiently to identify its model

          • Previously engaged target—A known threat that has received direct fires from the same firer or

          • Engagement:

                 A series of one or more targets that receive direct fire from a firer, team, crew, or small unit to
                     alter the threat’s engagement decision process against a friendly force.

                     and ends with a termination command.

          • Engagement sequence:
                 The order in which multiple targets are engaged with direct fire.
                 May be directed during the conduct of fire or follow standard target prioritization rules of thumb
                     (see chapter 6).

BATTLE DAMAGE ASSESSMENT REPORT

     8-20. A battle damage assessment report provides a timely and accurate estimate of damage that has resulted
     from the application of military force, either lethal or nonlethal, against a predetermined objective. It allows
     the higher headquarters to track the number of destroyed enemy vehicles and eliminated squads, which assists
     them in their planning for future operations. An accurate assessment updates the common operational picture
     for the organization and allows the commander to make well-informed decisions.

                                                                                                                                          Glossary

date-time group
          (NATO) A group of six digits with a zone time suffix and the standardized abbreviation for the month.
          The first pair of digits represents the day; the second pair the hour; the third pair the minutes. After the
          month may be added the last two digits of the year. (AAP-06)

direction of fire
          The direction on which a fire unit is laid to the most significant threat in the target area, to the chart
          direction to the center of the zone of fire, or to the target. (ATP 3-09.50)

*discrimination
          The determination of a target as friend, foe, neutral, noncombatant, or unknown.

engagement area
          An area where the commander intends to contain and destroy an enemy force with the massed effects
          of all available weapons and supporting systems. (ADP 3-90)

*engagement techniques
          Effects-oriented fire distribution measures.

final protective fire
          An immediately available, prearranged barrier of fire designed to impede enemy movement across
          defensive lines or areas. (JP 3-09.3)

final protective line
          A selected line of fire where an enemy assault is to be checked by interlocking fire from all available
          weapons and obstacles. (FM 3-90)

*fire control measures
          The means by which the commander or subordinate leaders control fires.

graphic control measure
          A symbol used on maps and displays to regulate forces and warfighting functions. (ADP 6-0)

identification
          The process of determining the friendly or hostile character of an unknown detected contact. (JP 3-01)

*maximum engagement line
          The linear depiction of the farthest limit of effective fire for a weapon or unit.

objective
          (Army) A location used to orient operations, phase operations, facilitate changes of direction, and
          provide for unity of effort. (ADP 3-90)

phase line
          An easily identified feature in the operational area utilized for control and coordination of military
          operations. (JP 3-09)

positive identification
          An identification derived from observation and analysis of target characteristics, including visual
          recognition; electronic support systems; non-cooperative target recognition techniques; identification,
          friend or foe systems; or other physics-based identification techniques. (JP 3-01)

*quadrants
          Four separate sectors, or subdivisions of an area, created by superimposing a pair of perpendicular axes
          over the terrain.

reconnaissance by fire
          A technique in which a unit fires on a suspected enemy position. (FM 3-90)

restrictive fire line
          A specific boundary established between converging, friendly surface forces that prohibits fires or their
          effects from crossing. (JP 3-09)
