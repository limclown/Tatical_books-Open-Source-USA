specialist as a reference for on-the-job training and as a text for any established pharmacy specialist assistant course
of formal instruction.

Described herein are the technical procedures practiced in the pharmacy department. This training circular
emphasizes the pharmacy specialist’s duties as they pertain to the many and varied activities of the pharmacy
section. Publications cited in the text are listed as references.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure that their decisions and actions comply with the applicable United

                                   Introduction

SECTION I – PHARMACY SPECIALIST ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES

     1-1. This training circular is designed to serve as a reference for the pharmacy specialist working in an
     operations environment outside of a Defense Health Agency (DHA) medical treatment facility (MTF). The
     68Q, Pharmacy Specialist, is a vital member of the healthcare delivery team whose actions contribute to the
     overall health, welfare, and readiness of the United States (U.S.) military forces.

ROLES

     1-2. Pharmacy specialists perform various roles within the pharmacy. Some roles include, but are not limited
     to—

             • Ordering medications.
             • Assisting license pharmacists with preparing medications for dispensing.
             • Serving as the custodian of controlled substances.
             • Assisting with patient triage.
             • Fulfilling medic roles in austere environments.

RESPONSIBILITIES

     1-3. Pharmacy specialists must possess a knowledge and understanding of drugs and their actions,
     interactions, and contraindications to their use. They are often called upon to furnish information to other
     members of the healthcare team and patients concerning the proper use of drugs.

     1-4. Pharmacy specialists are responsible for the following:
             • Ordering, receiving, and storing supplies and equipment.
             • Processing, filling, preparing, and issuing pharmaceutical products.
             • Operating and maintaining common pharmaceutical manufacturing and packaging equipment.
             • Maintaining logs, records, and reports of controlled, and non-controlled medications.
             • Performing other technical procedures that require pharmacy specific job skills and knowledge.

     1-5. Pharmacy specialists work under the direct supervision of a licensed pharmacist or dispensing physician
     and will provide support services to the unit commander and the officer in charge of the pharmacy.

             a. The unit commander is responsible for the operation of the pharmacy. He or she exercises careful
             supervision over all phases of the pharmacy's operations, including employment of recognized
             professional procedures, and the establishment and aggressive pursuit of policies that ensure conformity
             with the highest standards of the pharmaceutical profession. The unit commander is assisted in their
             pharmacy operation responsibilities by the pharmacy staff.
             b. The officer in charge (OIC) of the pharmacy must be a commissioned officer who is a graduate of a
             recognized school or college of pharmacy in the U.S., Puerto Rico, or the District of Columbia, and
             licensed in the U.S., Puerto Rico, or the District of Columbia.

                   (1) The OIC is charged with recognizing, identifying, selecting, preparing, safeguarding, testing,
                   evaluating, and dispensing all substances that are used in preventive or curative medicine.
                   (2) The OIC is responsible for keeping abreast of new developments in the field of pharmacy, and
                   for passing on information about these developments to the professional personnel they serve.

SECTION II – PHARMACY LAWS

     1-6. Laws and regulations pertaining to the dispensing of pharmaceutical preparations have been established
     by Federal and military authorities. Strict adherence to these laws and regulations protects both the pharmacy
     and patients.

THE FOOD, DRUG, AND COSMETIC ACT OF 1938

     1-7. The Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 established standards for quality and labeling of drug
     products. A major section of the act pertains to adulterations of drug products. Adulterating a product refers to
     changing the quality of a product by adding or taking away ingredients. A product is adulterated if it is
     packaged under unsanitary conditions or has ingredients inaccurately reflected on its labeling.

     1-8. Another section of the act concerns misbranding or errors in labeling. Examples of misbranding include
     labels—

             a. That contain false or misleading information.
             b. That lack required information, such as the manufacturer’s name and address, the name and strength
             of the drug, and the amount of the drug in the container.
             c. On habit-forming drugs that do not clearly indicate that the drug is habit-forming.

             Note. The Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 also requires that all drugs must be tested,
             proven safe, and approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) before they are
             marketed.

THE DURHAM-HUMPHREY AMENDMENT

     1-9. The Durham-Humphrey Amendment was added to the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act in 1951, and
     mandates that medications be divided into two basic categories: legend drugs (or prescription) and over the
     counter (OTC) drugs.

             a. Legend drugs can only be obtained legally by prescription. It is against the law to give legend drugs
             to persons who do not have a valid prescription. These drugs must have the following statement printed
             on their labels: "Caution: Federal law prohibits dispensing without a prescription."
             b. OTC drugs can be legally obtained without a prescription. Generally, OTC drugs are considered safe
             for use without direct medical supervision.

THE KEFAUVER-HARRIS AMENDMENT

     1-10. The Kefauver-Harris Amendment was added to the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act in 1962 and includes
     provisions that govern the reporting of adverse drug reactions and the testing of investigational drugs. The
     Kefauver-Harris Amendment—

             a. Established good manufacturing practices by the drug industry.
             b. Granted the FDA greater powers to access company production and control records to verify those
             practices.(Part III: Drugs and Foods Under the 1938 Act and Its Amendments, FDA 2018)
             c. Instituted stricter agency control over drug trials (including a requirement that patients involved
             must give their informed consent).
             d. Mandated efficacy as well as safety before a drug could be marketed.
             e. Required the FDA to assess the efficacy of all drugs introduced since 1938.

THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT

     1-12. The Controlled Substances Act regulates the acquisition, distribution, classification, and record keeping
     for certain drugs that have been identified to have abuse or dependence potential. Controlled substances are
     classified into five schedules according to their abuse potential.

             a. Schedule I: Drugs in this schedule are illegal and have no accepted medical use in the U.S. Examples
             of drugs in this schedule include heroin, marijuana, and lysergic acid diethylamide (commonly known as
             LSD).
             b. Schedule II: Drugs in this schedule have a high abuse potential with severe psychic or physical
             dependence liability. This includes both narcotic and non-narcotic substances. Examples of drugs in this
             schedule include cocaine, dextroamphetamine, morphine, and oxycodone.
             c. Schedule III: Drugs in this schedule have an abuse potential less than those in Schedules I and II.
             This schedule includes both narcotic and non-narcotic substances. Examples of drugs in this schedule
             include buprenorphine and ketamine.
             d. Schedule IV: Drugs in this schedule have an abuse potential less than those in Schedule III.
             Examples of drugs in this schedule include alprazolam, tramadol, and zolpidem.
             e. Schedule V: Drugs in this schedule have the lowest potential for abuse according to the Drug
             Enforcement Administration (known as DEA). An example of a drug in this schedule is a cough
             preparation containing less than or equal to 200 milligrams of codeine per 100 milliliters or per 100
             grams.

     1-13. The U.S. further classifies controlled substances as Note Q or Note R. These categories are assigned to
     all controlled substances to identify specific storage and handling requirements as outlined in the Federal
     Supply Catalog.

             • Note Q substances are those items classified as either Schedule III, IV, or V in the Controlled
             Substances Act.

             • Note R substances are alcohol, alcoholic beverages, precious metals, and drugs classified as
             Schedule II substances in the Controlled Substances Act.

             Note. Detailed information regarding the management of controlled substances can be found
             in AR 40-3.

THE POISON PREVENTION PACKAGING ACT OF 1970

     1-14. The Poison Prevention Act mandates certain products that are found to be hazardous or potentially
     hazardous must be sold or dispensed in safety packages, and requires that prescriptions filled in the pharmacy,
     except for prescriptions issued to hospitalized patients, be dispensed in child-resistant containers.

     1-15. The prescribing physician or patient may request prescription medicines to be dispensed in non-child
     resistant packaging. Local pharmacy standard operating procedure may dictate whether a written statement or
     other documentation is required from the physician or patient before providing non-child-resistant packaging.

THE OMNIBUS BUDGET RECONCILIATION ACT OF 1990

     1-16. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1990 (known as OBRA 90) originally mandated patient
     counseling, prospective drug utilization review, and record keeping only for Medicaid patients; however, the
     requirements have been extended to apply to both Medicaid and non-Medicaid beneficiaries.

     1-17. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1990 requires pharmacies to perform the following
     functions:

             • Maintain patient medication profiles.
             • Offer medication counseling for all new prescriptions.
             • Screen prescriptions for potential interactions, contraindications, duplication, and dosages.

HEALTH INSURANCE PORTABILITY AND ACCOUNTABILITY ACT

     1-18. The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) establishes national standards to
     safeguard an individual’s medical records and other personal health information. The HIPAA requires
     healthcare entities, including individuals who work for healthcare organizations, to follow rules that protect the
     privacy and security of sensitive patient health information.

     1-19. Employees of healthcare organizations may be disciplined and fined for violating HIPAA rules. Some
     examples of HIPAA violations include:

             • Accessing protected health information without a need to know.
             • Sharing protected health information with unauthorized individuals outside the organization.
             • Talking about protected health information (known as PHI) in public spaces.

THE RESOURCE CONSERVATIONS AND RECOVERY ACT

     1-20. The Resource Conservations and Recovery Act (known as RCRA) is an amendment to the Solid Waste
     Disposal Act that addresses the huge volumes of municipal and industrial solid waste. It consists of multiple
     subtitles that address the following:

             • Conserve energy and natural resources.
             • Ensure wastes are managed in an environmentally sound manner.
             • Protect human health and the environment from the potential hazards of waste disposal.
             • Reduce the amount of waste generated.

     1-21. Subtitle C of the Resource Conservations and Recovery Act establishes a federal program to manage
     hazardous waste from beginning to end and ensures it is handled in a manner that protects human health and the
     environment. To meet federal program goals, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (known as EPA)
     developed regulations for the generation, transportation, treatment, storage, and disposal of hazardous waste.

     1-22. The regulatory criteria for hazardous waste are based on toxicity, corrosivity, ignitability, and reactivity.
     Certain pharmaceuticals meet the same regulatory criteria as hazardous waste and are regulated by the Resource
     Conservations and Recovery Act when discarded.

FOOD AND DRUG ADMINISTRATION

     1-23. In accordance with 21 CFR 310.501 and 21 CFR 310.515, the FDA requires Patient Package Inserts to be
     dispensed with all prescriptions for oral contraceptives and estrogen-containing products. Patient Package
     Inserts are voluntary for other prescription drug products.

     1-24. The FDA requires a Medication Guide (a type of labeling for patients and caregivers) to be dispensed
     with certain prescription medications to ensure the patient’s safe and effective use of the medications. The
     Medication Guides provide specific information on medications and are meant to help patients avoid potentially
     serious adverse effects of the medications.

SECTION III – PHARMACEUTICAL CALCULATIONS

     1-25. Performing accurate math calculations is an essential skill and tool for pharmacy specialists. The most
     important and basic study for pharmacy specialists is the arithmetic pertinent to prescriptions. Without a
     complete understanding of pharmacy mathematics, pharmacy specialists will be unable to manufacture many of
     the prescriptions and compounds they may be called upon to dispense.

GENERAL

     1-26. Pharmacy specialists can work in many different locations, from Army hospitals and health clinics to
     improvised settings in a deployed environment. Different situations can change the pharmacy specialist’s level
     of responsibility. Deployment and troop movement are two such situations. In these instances, the pharmacist
     will rely more on the pharmacy specialist’s pharmaceutical preparation expertise.

     1-27. There will be situations when the pharmacist will rely on the pharmacy specialist to perform tasks like
     calculating conversions of drug strengths, compounding sterile and non-sterile preparations, and performing
     weight conversions and alligations (medial and alternate). Knowing how to quickly respond to pharmacy
     calculation questions is a mission essential skill the pharmacy specialist must possess.

CALCULATION METHODS

     1-28. Pharmacy personnel will frequently be required to calculate medication doses. They must use
     mathematical problem-solving skills to accurately calculate medication doses. The common methods used by
     pharmacy personnel to calculate medication doses include:

             • Alligations: Using a stronger strength and a weaker strength of a medication to create a new
             strength.
             • Percent volume in volume (%V/V): Mixing two liquids.
             • Percent weight in volume (%W/V): Mixing a solid and a liquid.
             • Percent weight in weight (%W/W): Mixing two solids.
             • Stock solution dilution: Using a stock solution to create smaller doses.
             • Weight-based dosing: Calculating a dose based on a patient’s weight.

ALLIGATIONS

     1-29. The alligations method is used to determine the quantities of ingredients of differing strengths needed to
     make a mixture of a desired strength. This method involves finding the average concentration of a mixture,
     given the concentration and quantity of its components.

PERCENT VOLUME IN VOLUME

     1-30. The percent volume in volume (expressed as % V/V) method expresses the concentration of a substance
     in a solution. The percent volume in volume is calculated by dividing the volume of solute by the total volume
     of the solution, then multiplying the result by 100.

PERCENT WEIGHT IN VOLUME

     1-31. The percent weight in volume (expressed as % W/V) method expresses the number of grams of an
     ingredient in 100 milliliters (mL) of liquid. The percent weight in volume is calculated by dividing the mass of
     the solute by the mass of the solution, then multiplying the result by 100.

PERCENT WEIGHT IN WEIGHT

     1-32. The percent weight in weight (expressed as % W/W) method expresses the concentration of a solute in a
     solution. The percent weight in weight is calculated by dividing the mass of solute by the solution, then
     multiplying the result by 100.

STOCK SOLUTION DILUTION

     1-33. Stock solution dilution is the process of reducing a stock concentrate (a solution of known, accurate
     concentration) to a lower concentration. Pharmacy personnel can prepare concentrated solutions and properly
     store them or prepare them at the time of use.

WEIGHT-BASED DOSING

     1-34. Weight-based dosing utilizes the patient’s body weight (in kilograms [kg]) to determine a specific
     medication dose for the patient. The weight-based dose is calculated by multiplying the medication dose by the
     patient’s weight (in kg), then dividing the result by the number of times the patient will take the medication in
     the day.

SECTION I – PHARMACY STRUCTURE AND PERSONNEL

     2-1. The organizational structure for pharmacy services, and the roles and responsibilities of pharmacy
     personnel during field operations differ from those in garrison. The brigade commander is responsible for the
     operation of pharmacy services and will achieve operational oversight through subordinate commanders and
     directly by use of staff officers (Chief of Pharmacy Services). Pharmacy services personnel include, the Chief of
     Pharmacy Services, the Noncommissioned Officer in Charge (known as NCOIC) of Pharmacy, and pharmacy
     specialists and staff.

PHARMACY SERVICES

     2-2. Pharmacists and pharmacy specialists assigned to modified table of organization and equipment (MTOE)
     units are responsible for pharmacy services. Pharmacy services include—

             • Bulk drug order filling.
             • Clinic inspections.
             • Drug information and therapeutic consultations.
             • Emergency medication cart (known as crash cart) sustainment.
             • Inpatient medication dispensing.
             • Outpatient medication dispensing.
             • Sterile product service.
             • Temperature sensitive medical product (TSMP) management and oversight for medications.

     2-3. According to AR 40-3, the following personnel are authorized to write prescriptions:
             a. Uniformed and civilian personnel engaged in professional practice at uniformed Services MTFs to
             include—
                   • Dentists.
                   • Physicians.
                   • Podiatrists.
                   • Veterinarians.
             b. Civilian personnel not assigned to a uniformed Services MTF but licensed in the jurisdiction of their
             practice and treating personnel eligible for care in the Military Health System to include—
                   • Dentists.
                   • Physicians.
                   • Podiatrists.

     2-4. According to AR 40-68, the following personnel are authorized to write prescriptions:
             a. Uniformed and civilian personnel engaged in professional practice at uniformed Services MTFs and
             privileged to prescribe medications to include—
                   • Certified Nurse Midwives.
                   • Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetists.
                   • Clinical Pharmacists.
                   • Nurse Practitioners.
                   • Occupational Therapists.
                   • Optometrists.
                   • Physician Assistants.

     2-11. A drug is defined by the Federal Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act as any item that is recognized by the

             Note. The terms drug and medication are used interchangeably in this publication.

             a. Prescription drugs, as defined by the Durham-Humphrey Amendment to the Federal Food, Drug and

             Without a Prescription.”
             b. Non-prescription or OTC drugs, as defined by the Durham-Humphrey Amendment to the Federal

             label, “Caution: Federal Law Prohibits Dispensing Without a Prescription.”

CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES

     2-12. Controlled substances are defined by the Controlled Substances Act which is the common name of Title
     II of the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970.The Controlled Substances Act
     classifies all substances regulated under existing federal law into five schedules, Schedules I, II, III, IV, and V,
     according to their abuse potential and psychological effects.

             • Schedule I drugs have no accepted medical use in the U.S. Examples of drugs under these schedules
             are heroin, marijuana, lysergic acid diethylamide, phencyclidine (commonly known as PCP).

             • Schedule II drugs, also known within the Army as NOTE R items, have a high abuse potential with
             severe physical and or psychological dependence liability.

             • Schedule III, IV, and V drugs, also known within the Army as NOTE Q items, have an abuse
             potential less than those in Schedule I or II.

             Note. According to AR 40-3, the hospital commander has the authority to classify non-
             controlled substances as controlled substances. All controlled substances must be documented
             on Department of the Army (DA) Form 3862 (Controlled Substance Stock Record) or in an
             approved and validated automation system.

     3-6. Hazardous drugs are defined by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (known as
     NIOSH) as having one or more of the following characteristics:

             • Carcinogenicity (causes cancer).
             • Reproductive toxicity, organ toxicity at low doses, genotoxicity (causes genetic mutations).
             • Structure mimicking existing hazardous drugs (similar in chemical structure).
             • Teratogenicity or other developmental toxicity (causes birth defects).

HAZARDOUS DRUG MANAGEMENT

     3-7. Many studies have shown that healthcare workers are potentially exposed to medications, to include
     hazardous drugs, through inhalation (vapors, dusts, and aerosols), skin absorption, ingestion, or injection. No
     minimum acceptable levels of exposure to hazardous drugs have been established. The exposure to hazardous
     drugs may result in acute effects or reproductive risks.

             a. Acute effects to healthcare workers include headaches, lightheadedness, hair loss, nausea, vomiting,
             dermatitis, and eczema.
             b. Reproductive risks include abnormalities, infertility, miscarriages, premature labor, and low-birth
             weight. The effects are not always immediate but may also be chronic, long-term.

     3-8. The identification of hazardous drugs and the implications to healthcare workers has led to the
     development of U.S. Pharmacopeia General Chapter <800> (known as USP <800>). U.S. Pharmacopeia
     General Chapter <800> was originally published in 2016 as a standard to protect personnel and provide
     guidance to handling hazardous drugs. This section (USP <800>) complements the requirements for U.S.
     Pharmacopeia General Chapter <795> and U.S. Pharmacopeia General Chapter <797> (known as USP <795>
     and USP <797>) in which pharmacy standards are based to ensure reduced risks for contamination, infection, or
     incorrect dosing.

     3-9. Whether hazardous drug handling occurs within the continental United States (known as CONUS) or
     outside the continental United States (OCONUS), healthcare facilities handling hazardous drugs must identify a
     Hazardous Drug Officer, and develop standard operating procedures, assessments of risks, spill procedures, and
     training plans.

     3-10. Safe transportation of hazardous drugs must take place to prevent spillage or product breakage. Personal
     protective clothing and equipment must be defined and available for healthcare workers.

             Note. The most current hazardous drug list can be found on the Centers for Disease Control
             and Prevention website.

HAZARDOUS WASTE MANAGEMENT

     3-11. Hazardous waste is defined by listed or characteristic properties according to the U.S. Environmental
     Protection Agency. This information can be found on the United States Environmental Protection Agency
     website.

             a. Listed waste is specific items found on the F, K, P, and U lists found in Title 40 of the Code of
             Federal Regulations, Part 261. Typically, F and K listed waste is not found in the medical setting.
             However, P and U listed items are found in some pharmaceuticals. Some preservatives within
             pharmaceuticals make them hazardous waste.
             b. Characteristic properties include ignitability, corrosivity, reactivity, toxicity.

                   (1) Ignitability characteristics include liquids with flash points below 60 degrees Celsius, non-
                   liquids that cause fire through specific conditions, and ignitable compressed gases and oxidizers.

     3-22. TLAMMs function to efficiently move medical materiel for tactical units and OCONUS customers
     without just-in-time (next-day) logistical support from the prime vendor. However, there are some key
     differences that pharmacy personnel must be aware of when ordering through a TLAMM versus directly
     through the prime vendor.

             a. TLAMMs are typically demand-based for stocking, so the more an item is requested, the more likely
             the TLAMM will stock a specific National Drug Code (known as NDC). Likewise, for items that have
             low demand, the stock levels on hand are expected to be lower, or possibly even non-stocked.

                   Note. TLAMMs stock limited numbers of pharmaceuticals; therefore, a TLAMM often has
                   fewer package size selections than that of the prime vendor.

             b. Delivery times from the TLAMM may vary but is often quicker than OCONUS prime vendor
             delivery times. TLAMMs may deliver based on order volume or standard transportation routes, or a
             combination of both. Some pharmacies or units may get deliveries multiple times per week, while others
             receive deliveries less frequently.

             c. Pharmaceuticals and medical supplies stocked at the TLAMM will arrive much faster than non-
             stocked items. The TLAMM customer support section will provide an authorized stockage list (known as
             ASL) upon request. Additionally, the TLAMM customer support section will provide information on
             delivery lead times and any delays.
