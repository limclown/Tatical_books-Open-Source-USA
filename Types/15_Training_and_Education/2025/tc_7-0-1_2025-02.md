                                 Preface

TC 7-0.1, After Action Reviews, provides Army leaders the fundamental processes and procedures to plan
and conduct after action reviews (AAR). Users of this TC must familiarize themselves with Army training
doctrine as described in ADP 7-0 and FM 7-0.

The principal audience for TC 7-0.1 is all members of the profession of arms. Commanders and staffs of
Army headquarters serving as joint task force or multinational headquarters must also refer to applicable joint
or multinational doctrine concerning the range of military operations and joint or multinational forces.
Trainers and educators throughout the Army also use this publication.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure that their decisions and actions comply with applicable United
States, international, and, in some cases, host-nation laws and regulations. Commanders at all levels ensure
that their Soldiers operate in accordance with the law of war and the rules of engagement. (See FM 6-27.)

TC 7-0.1 uses joint terms where applicable. Selected joint and Army terms and definitions appear in both the
glossary and the text. For definitions shown in the text, the term is italicized, and the number of the proponent
publication follows the definition.

TC 7-0.1 applies to the Active Army, Army National Guard/Army National Guard of the United States and
United States Army Reserve unless otherwise stated.

The proponent of TC 7-0.1 is the United States Army Combined Arms Center. The preparing agency is the
Training Management Directorate (TMD) of the United States Army Combined Arms Center – Training
(CAC-T). Send comments and recommendations on DA Form 2028 (Recommended Changes to Publications
and Blank Forms) to Commander, United States Army Combined Arms Center – Training and Fort
Leavenworth, ATZL-CTD (TC 7-0.1), 410 Kearny Avenue, Building 45, Fort Leavenworth, KS 66027-1356,
or email trainingdoctrine@army.mil.

                              Introduction

TC 7-0.1, After Action Reviews (AAR), expands on the fundamental concepts of after action reviews as a
primary tool leaders use in planning and conducting effective training evaluations as discussed in the Army’s
training doctrine in ADP 7-0 and FM 7-0. TC 7-0.1 guides leaders to develop AARs that lead training
participants in the self-discovery of how to improve task performance. This publication applies to all Army
leaders and the three components of the Total Army: the Regular Army, the Army National Guard, and the
Army Reserve.

TC 7-0.1 contains four chapters:

Chapter 1 discusses the purpose of AARs and their general characteristics, establishing a four step process
guiding leaders through the execution of a successful AAR. It also includes a discussion of how the AAR
process is used in combat situations to quickly and effectively apply lessons learned to improve operational
performance.

Chapter 2 introduces the procedures leaders use in planning and preparing AARs. It discusses the unit’s
overall approach to evaluation planning and how AARs are used to improve performance weaknesses while
sustaining performance strengths.

Chapter 3 provides a guide for leaders in conducting AARs. This includes introductory remarks, introducing
the rules for all participants to follow and the overall agenda for the AAR.

Chapter 4 gives an overview of the actions that must take place following an AAR. These actions ensure the
results of the AAR process are used to continually improve unit performance and that lessons learned are
shared across the Army.

                                                 Chapter 1

                 After Action Review Fundamentals

OVERVIEW

     1-1. An after action review (AAR) is a guided analysis of an organization’s performance, conducted at
     appropriate times during and at the conclusion of a training event or operation, with the objective of
     improving future performance (FM 7-0). It includes a facilitator, event participants, and other observers. The
     AAR provides valuable and timely feedback essential to correcting training deficiencies. Feedback is direct,
     immediate, and standards based.

     1-2. AARs are professional discussions that require the active participation of those trained. AARs enable
     and encourage participants to discover for themselves what happened and then develop a plan for improving
     task performance. These reviews provide candid insights into task performance strengths and weaknesses
     from various perspectives and feedback. AARs focus directly on the commander’s guidance, training
     objectives, and performance standards as indicated on the task training and evaluation outline (T&EO).

     1-3. Leaders avoid creating an environment of pointing out failures since AARs are not a critique. The
     climate of the AAR encourages candid and open discussion of task performance without stifling learning and
     team building. Soldiers and leaders participating in an AAR actively self-discover what happened and why.
     Through this process, they learn and remember more than they would from a critique alone. Characteristics
     of an AAR—

          • Are conducted during or immediately after each event.
          • Focuses directly on task performance and training objectives.
          • Are based on doctrine and published standards.
          • Uses leading and open-ended questions to encourage self-discovery and important lessons from the

                training.
          • Allows many Soldiers and leaders, including opposing forces (OPFORs), to participate so that more

                of the training is recalled and lessons learned are shared across more of the organization.
          • Links performance to future training and assigns responsibility for a timeline to improve task

                performance.

     1-4. Another benefit of AARs comes from immediately evaluating training performance and quickly
     applying sound remedies to improve future performance. Commanders also use AAR results as a feedback
     tool in assessing unit mission-essential task (MET) proficiency.

     1-5. AARs are the dynamic link between task performance and execution to standard. They provide
     commanders a critical evaluation component to plan future Soldier and unit training. Through the
     professional and candid discussion of events, Soldiers objectively measure their performance against
     approved standards and identify specific ways to improve proficiency and establish better techniques and
     procedures.

TYPES OF AFTER ACTION REVIEWS

     1-6. There are two types of AARs, formal and informal. A formal AAR is resource-intensive and involves
     planning for and preparing the AAR site, supporting training aids, and supporting personnel. Informal AARs
     require less planning and preparation and are typically done immediately following a training event. Any
     AAR—formal or informal—analyzes decisions made during the execution of tasks. See table 1-1 for
     comparisons.

Formal

   1-7. Leaders plan formal AARs at the same time they finalize their training event planning (six to eight weeks
   before execution). They also require the selection and training of facilitators and evaluators. Facilitators are
   responsible for the conduct of the AAR. Formal AARs require more planning and preparation than informal
   AARs. They require lead time for site reconnaissance and selection, coordination for training aids (such as
   terrain models or map blow-ups), and selection, set up, and maintenance of the AAR site.

   1-8. Formal AARs are typically conducted at company level and above. An exception is an AAR of crew,
   section, or small-unit performance after gunnery tables or after a platoon situational training exercise (STX).
   Squad and platoon AARs are held before the execution of formal company and higher echelon AARs. This
   allows all levels of the unit to benefit from an AAR experience. It also provides facilitators with observations
   and trends to address during the formal AAR.

   1-9. During formal AARs, the facilitator focuses the discussion of events on training objectives. At the
   conclusion, the leader reviews key points and issues identified during the training (reinforcing learning that
   took place during the discussion) and once again focuses on the commander’s training objectives.

Informal

     1-11. Informal AARs are led by designated facilitators, usually within the unit’s chain of command and
     drawn from Soldiers and small-unit leaders at platoon-level and below. At company and battalion levels,
     facilitators may conduct informal AARs when resources for formal AARs, including time, are unavailable.
     Informal AARs use the standard AAR agenda.

     1-12. Informal AARs:
         • Are conducted by internal chain of command.
         • Take less time.
         • Use simple training aids.
         • Are conducted as needed, primarily based on leader assessment following the observed action.
         • Are conducted at the training site.

     1-13. Facilitators use informal AARs as on-the-spot coaching tools while observing Soldier and unit
     performance during training. For example, after destroying an enemy observation post (OP) during a
     movement to contact, a squad leader conducts an informal AAR to make corrections and reinforce strengths.
     Using nothing more than pinecones to represent squad members, the facilitator and Soldiers can discuss the
     contact from start to finish. The squad can quickly:

1-14. Informal AARs provide immediate feedback to Soldiers, leaders, and units as training progresses.
Better techniques and solutions gathered during informal AARs are immediately employed as the unit
continues training. During lower echelon informal AARs, leaders often collect teaching points and trends
they use as discussion points during higher echelon formal AARs.

1-15. Informal AARs maximize training value because all unit members are actively involved. They learn
what to do, how to do it better, and the importance of the roles they play in task accomplishment. They then
know how to execute tasks to standard. The most significant difference between formal AARs and informal
AARs is that informal AARs require fewer training resources and few, if any, training aids. Although
informal AARs are part of the unit evaluation plan, they are more commonly conducted when the facilitator
feels the unit benefits the most. Providing immediate feedback while the training is still fresh in Soldiers'
minds is a significant strength of informal AARs.

AFTER ACTION REVIEWS IN COMBAT

     1-19. The need to evaluate and assess unit performance and proficiency does not stop when a unit goes into
     combat. Training on current and emerging tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) is an integral part of
     combat operations, although limited time and proximity to the enemy may restrict the type and extent of
     training. Only through training and applying lessons learned does combat performance improve and reduce
     the stiff penalties combat inflicts on the untrained.

     1-20. The AAR is an effective technique to use in a combat environment. An AAR takes little time, and
     leaders can conduct them almost anywhere consistent with unit security requirements. Conducting AARs
     helps overcome the steep learning curve that exists in a unit exposed to combat and helps the unit ensure that
     it does not repeat mistakes. It also helps them sustain strengths. By integrating training into combat operations
     and using tools such as AARs, leaders increase their unit's chances for success on the battlefield.

Steps of the after action review process

     1-21. Effective AARs, formal or informal, require leaders to plan, prepare for, execute, assess and integrate.
     AAR planning is part of each training event or mission. Successful leaders understand the unit’s mission and
     the commander’s guidance for the training event. During planning, commanders identify opportunities to
     conduct AARs, assign facilitator and evaluator responsibilities, and ensure time and resources are allocated
     to conduct AARs. Training focuses on collective and individual tasks to demonstrate proficiency. These tasks
     require that leaders understand the commander’s guidance, intent, concept of the operation, and tasks being
     trained. At all echelons during training, there are several opportunities to conduct on the spot coaching and
     lead informal AARs.

                                                 Chapter 2

                        Plan and Prepare an AAR

OVERVIEW

     2-1. AARs are a primary component of a unit’s overarching training evaluation plan developed for every
     training event. Detailed evaluation planning must occur before training execution. The focus of this planning
     is to evaluate the effectiveness of training, and AARs contribute to that process.

STEP 1: PLAN AN AAR

     2-2. In conjunction with the unit evaluation plan, leaders develop a supporting AAR plan for every training
     event. It contains:

         • Who facilitates AARs?
         • Who observes and evaluates the training?
         • Who attends AARs?
         • When and where do AARs occur?
         • What training aids/resources are required?

     2-3. Leaders use the AAR plan to identify critical places and events to observe task performance to provide
     the unit with an accurate and complete evaluation. Examples include unit road marches, actions on the
     objective, maintenance collection points, etc. By identifying these events and assigning responsibilities, unit
     leaders ensure training is observed and performance recorded at the right place and at the right time.

     2-4. AAR plans also designate who will facilitate, evaluate/observe, and control a particular event. The term
     Observer, Coach, Trainer refers to individuals tasked to observe and record performance for the training
     exercise as well as to facilitate AARs (when trained and certified). (See figure 2-1 for an example of an
     informal AAR plan.)

                   Facilitator: 1LT Smith
                   Evaluators/Observers: 1LT Smith; SFC Davis
                   Element: 1st platoon
                   Training objectives: Conduct Support by Fire (07-PLT-3000)
                   Who attends: All participants
                   When held: Immediately following break in contact
                   Location: Vicinity GH44319218, behind 2nd Squad
                   Special requirements: LTC Goode will provide closing comments

                                        Figure 2-1. Example informal AAR plan

     2-5. To maximize the effectiveness of AARs, facilitators plan and rehearse AARs before training begins.
     During planning, leaders assign facilitator and evaluator responsibilities and identify tentative times and
     locations for AARs. This ensures the allocation of time and resources to conduct AARs and reinforces the
     important role AARs play in realizing the full benefit of training.

     2-6. The amount and level of detail needed during the planning and preparation process depends on the type
     of AAR conducted, and on the resources available to conduct them. AAR planning has several distinct steps
     that are the basis of the AAR plan. These include:

         • Selecting and training qualified facilitators.
         • Identifying qualified evaluators/observers.
         • Identifying when AARs are occurring during training.
         • Determining who is attending AARs.

Designate Facilitators, Select, Train, and Certify Observers/Evaluators

     2-7. When planning an AAR, leaders designate a primary facilitator for the AAR. The facilitator has the
     responsibility for overall coordination and execution of the AAR. They can also serve as an
     evaluator/observer. Typically, facilitators and evaluators/observers are equal to or above the rank of the
     evaluated unit leader.

     2-8. When planning an AAR, leaders select evaluators who:
         • Can perform the observed tasks themselves to Army standards.
         • Are knowledgeable on the duties they are to observe.
         • Are knowledgeable on unit organization, task doctrine, and unit SOPs.

     2-9. When using external evaluators, leaders ensure they are of at least equal in rank to the leader of the unit
     evaluated. If leaders must choose between experience and understanding of current TTPs versus rank, they
     go with experience. A staff sergeant with experience as a tank platoon sergeant can observe the platoon better
     than a sergeant first class who has no platoon sergeant experience.

     2-10. Evaluators should not have duties which detract from their evaluator duties. If this is not possible,
     leaders in the chain of command conduct the AARs for subordinate units. For example, squad leaders
     evaluate the performance of Soldiers in their squads and limit AAR discussion to individual actions. Platoon
     leaders or platoon sergeants do the same for squads, company commanders or first sergeants for platoons,
     and so on. If possible, they avoid evaluating their own duties and tasks.

     2-11. Leaders must train their evaluators. Each evaluator is trained and certified to lead AARs for the element
     he observes and provides input to the facilitator at the next higher echelon. Evaluators are trained in the use
     of the methods, techniques, and procedures in this training circular. If possible, trainers assign someone with
     AAR experience to accompany and assist an inexperienced facilitator until proficient. Leaders conduct AARs
     to help facilitators improve their performance. Inexperienced facilitators observe properly conducted AARs
     before attempting to lead one. Leaders includes classes on small-group discussion techniques in evaluator
     instruction.

     2-12. The commander responsible for the evaluation is also responsible for certifying and training evaluators
     (FM 7-0). This means the commander ensures each evaluator is fully trained to observe and render an
     objective evaluation. This certification is recorded in unit records and signed by the commander for future
     reference.

Identify Task Evaluation Standards

     2-13. Every individual and collective task has a training & evaluation outline (T&EO) which provides the
     task, conditions, standards, performance steps and measures, and much more (when evaluating weapons, the
     weapon’s prescribing training circular (TC) provides qualification standards). T&EOs are produced by the
     task proponent and are doctrine-based. They are available on the Army Training Network (ATN) at
     https://atn.army.mil. The senior facilitator distributes them to subordinate evaluators who review and use
     them to focus their observations. The senior facilitator is specific on what their subordinates are to evaluate
     and the standards of performance the unit leader/commander desires. When possible, the evaluator lists the
     steps from the task T&EO the unit must accomplish to properly complete the action. By listing each step, an
     evaluator can readily detect an error, especially in a drill when the actions of one Soldier may affect others.

     2-14. The performance steps in each T&EO provide the standard for successfully completing each task and
     help structure consistent observations. Using the evaluation plan, the evaluator concentrates efforts on
     identifying critical places and times where and when unit performance is best observed and evaluated. This
     ensures feedback is directly focused on tasks trained and provides the unit leaders the information needed to
     improve or sustain task proficiency.

                                                                                                                  Plan and Prepare an AAR

Scheduling Stopping Points

     2-15. Facilitators schedule time to conduct AARs as an integrated part of each training event. When possible,
     they plan for an AAR at the end of each critical phase or major training event. For example, a leader plans a
     stopping point after issuing an operation order (OPORD), when the unit arrives at a new position, after it
     consolidates on an objective, and so on.

     2-16. For planning purposes, facilitators allow approximately 30-45 minutes for platoon level AARs, one
     hour for company-level AARs, and two hours for battalion-level and above. Soldiers receive better feedback
     on their performance and remember the lessons longer if the AAR is not rushed. Facilitators ensure all key
     learning points are addressed and do not waste time on insignificant issues.

Determine Attendance

     2-17. The AAR plan specifies who attends each AAR. Normally, only key personnel attend. At times,
     however, the more participants present, the better the feedback. Facilitators must select as many participants
     as appropriate for the task and the AAR site. At each echelon, an AAR has a primary set of participants. At
     squad and platoon levels, everyone normally attends and participates. At company or higher levels, it is not
     practical to have everyone attend. In this case, friendly and OPFOR commanders, unit leaders, and other key
     personnel (fire support team (FIST) chief, radio telephone operator (RTO), etc.) are the primary participants.

Selecting AAR Sites

     2-18. An AAR usually occurs at or near the training exercise site. Facilitators identify and inspect AAR sites
     and prepare a site diagram showing the placement of training aids and other equipment. Designated AAR
     sites also allow pre-positioning of training aids and rapid assembly of key personnel, minimizing wasted
     time.

     2-19. Ideally, the AAR site allows Soldiers to see the terrain where the exercise took place. If this is not
     possible, the trainer finds a location which allows them to see the terrain where the most critical or significant
     actions are expected to occur. If needed, the trainer has a terrain model or enlarged map or sketch and a copy
     of the unit's graphics so everyone can relate key events to the actual terrain.

     2-20. The facilitator makes Soldiers attending the AAR as comfortable as possible (by removing
     helmets/headgear, etc.), and if possible, providing shelter from the elements (sun, cold, rain, snow), providing
     refreshments when practicable (coffee, water), creating an open environment where participants can focus
     on the AAR without distractions. Participants should not face into the sun, and key leaders should have seats
     up front. For formal AARs, locate vehicle parking and equipment security areas far enough away from the
     AAR site to prevent distractions.

Choosing Training Aids

     2-21. Training aids add to an AAR's effectiveness. Facilitators choose them carefully and request them well
     in advance. Training aids must directly support discussion of the training and promote learning. Local training
     support center (TSC) catalogs list training aids available to each unit. Dry-erase boards, terrain models, and
     digital maps are all worthwhile under the right conditions. (See Table 1-1.)

     2-22. Terrain visibility, group size, suitability to task, and availability of electrical power are all things to
     consider when selecting training aids. The key is planning and coordination. Only use a training aid if it
     makes the AAR better.

     2-23. To select the right training aids, facilitators should ask:
         • What points do I need to make during the AAR, and what training aids assist me making those points?
         • Does the aid illustrate one or more of the main points?
         • Can I use the actual terrain or equipment?
         • Does the aid have any restrictions or special requirements, such as additional generators?
         • Are participants able to see and hear it?
         • Is it necessary for the discussion or just nice to have?

Chapter 2

Reviewing the AAR Plan

     2-24. The AAR plan is a guide and is reviewed regularly to make sure it meets the training needs of the unit.
     CAUTION: Remember that every change takes preparation and planning time away from subordinate
     evaluators and facilitators. Changes may impact the quality of feedback.

     2-25. The purpose of the AAR plan is to allow leaders as much time as possible to prepare for the AAR.
     Frequent or unnecessary changes prevent that.

STEP 2: PREPARE AN AAR

     2-26. Preparation is the key to the effective execution of any plan. Preparing for an AAR begins before the
     training and continues until the actual event. Facilitators and evaluators use the time before the training event
     to brush up on their knowledge. They must be tactically and technically proficient. Therefore, they review
     current doctrine, technical information, and applicable unit SOPs to ensure they have the tools needed to
     observe unit and individual performances.

Review Key Directives

     2-27. To gain understanding of both the focus of unit training and the exercise plan, facilitators and evaluators
     review the unit's training objectives, mission, orders, TACSOP and unit METL. The focus is on the training
     objectives evaluators are observing to effectively lead the unit in its discussion during the AAR.

     2-28. Unit orders, including OPORDs, warning orders (WARNORDs), and fragmentary orders
     (FRAGORDs) issued before and during training establish initial conditions for tasks the units must perform.
     The unit METL provides an understanding of the unit’s overall capabilities in terms of measurable tasks.

Identify Key Events to Observe

     2-29. Evaluators focus their observations on the actions required to achieve the training objectives. To do
     this effectively, they identify the key events critical to accomplishing each objective. By identifying key
     events, evaluators ensure they position themselves in the right place at the right time to observe the unit's
     performance.

     2-30. Examples of key events include:
         • Issuance of OPORDs, WARNORDs, and FRAGOs.
         • Troop-leading procedures (TLPs).
         • Contact with opposing forces.
         • Resupply and reconstitution operations.
         • Intelligence preparation of the battlefield (IPB).
         • Passage of lines.

Observe the Training and Take Notes

     2-31. All unit activities have three phases: planning, preparation, and execution. These phases assist the
     evaluator in structuring their observation plan and note taking. Evaluators keep an accurate written record of
     what is seen and heard to record events, actions, and observations by time sequence. This prevents loss of
     valuable information and feedback.

     2-32. The evaluator includes the date-time group (DTG) of each observation to easily integrate their
     observations into those of other evaluators. This provides a comprehensive and detailed overview of what
     happened. When time is available, the evaluator reviews their notes and fills in any details not captured
     earlier. (See figure 2-2.)

     2-37. After the AAR facilitator has gathered all the information, they arrange information in chronological

           Step 3: Conducting an After Action Review

OVERVIEW

     3-1. Once training is observed and evaluated, participants gather at the dictated AAR site. Once everyone is
     situated, the AAR process begins. The facilitator starts with some type of "attention getter" - a joke, an
     appropriate anecdote, or historical example that relates to the training, exercise, event, or conduct of the
     AAR. This method puts participants at ease and helps them link their just completed task performance to the
     coming discussion.

AGENDA

     3-2. At the start of the AAR, the facilitator reviews the agenda of the AAR to ensure everyone understands
     what the purpose is, and to ensure participants understand the rules. A sample agenda is:

         • Introduction and rules.
         • Topics covered include:

                 Review of objectives and intent.
                 Training objectives.
                 Commander's mission/intent (what was supposed to happen).
                 OPFOR commander’s mission/intent.
                 Relevant doctrine, tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs).
                 Summary of events (what happened).
                 Discussion of key issues (chronological order of events, warfighting functions, key

                     events/themes/issues).
                 Sustaining / improving performance.
                 Safety issues.
         • Closing comments (summary).

INTRODUCTION AND RULES

     3-3. The introduction and rules of the AAR includes the following thoughts:
         • An AAR is a dynamic, candid, professional discussion of training, focused on unit performance and
              measured against the Army standards for the tasks trained. Everyone is expected to participate if they
              have an insight, observation, or question which assists the unit in identifying and correcting
              deficiencies or maintaining strengths.
         • An AAR is not a critique. No one, regardless of rank, position, or strength of personality, has all the
              information or answers. AARs maximize training benefits by allowing Soldiers, regardless of rank,
              to learn from each other.
         • An AAR does not grade success or failure. There are always weaknesses to improve and strengths to
              sustain.

     3-4. Soldier participation is directly related to the atmosphere created during the introduction. The facilitator
     makes a concerted effort to draw in and include Soldiers who seem reluctant to participate. The following
     techniques assist the facilitator in creating an atmosphere conducive to maximum participation and group
     learning. When covering AAR rules, the facilitator emphasizes the importance of:

         • Participants entering the discussion only when necessary – and pertinent to the discussion.
         • No one dominating the discussion – everyone is given the opportunity to share their

              experience/insights.
         • Differing thoughts and observations are permissible and encouraged.
         • Learning and applying what is learned from the actions to improve future performance.

Chapter 3

TOPICS

     3-5. AAR topics provide a sequence of facilitator-led discussions that follow a logical framework leading
     participants to a common understanding of what happened and how to perform better. This area of the AAR
     is critical for the facilitator to lead participants toward an agreed upon course of action to implement.

Review Objectives and Intent

     3-6. Training Objectives and AAR Focus: A well-structured training event raises more issues than can be
     addressed in the time available, so the facilitator coordinates ahead of time with the unit leader to identify
     three to five topics for focused discussion. Generally, these topics are aligned to the established training
     objectives for the exercise. The facilitator should begin the AAR by reminding the audience of their training
     objectives for the event/exercise and reviewing the focus areas agreed to by the unit leader and facilitator.

     3-7. Commander/leader Mission and Intent (what was supposed to happen): Using maps, operational
     graphics, terrain boards, and so on, the commander/leader restates the mission and intent. Then, if necessary,
     the facilitator guides the discussion to ensure everyone understands the plan and the commander's intent.
     Another technique is to have subordinate leaders restate the mission and discuss their commander's intent.

     3-8. OPFOR Commander's Mission and Intent: In a formal AAR, the OPFOR commander explains his plan
     to defeat friendly forces. He uses the same training aids as the friendly force commander so participants can
     understand the relationship of both plans.

Summary of Key Events (What Happened)

     3-9. AARs are more effective when the facilitator establishes a common baseline of understanding of what
     took place during the training event. Well designed exercises provide ample objective data through concept
     sketches, detailed timelines, training objectives and evaluator/observer notes. The facilitator and participants
     determine to the extent possible, what happened during the event, phase or operation. The leader gathers as
     many views or perspectives (such as from the OPFOR, squad leader, team leader and rifleman) as possible.
     These varied perspectives help the facilitator to establish a common understanding. Leaders then understand
     the complexity of an event and work to solve complex, ill-defined problems. Combat training centers and
     warfighter exercises may provide facilitators more options through AAR playback tools and training devices.
     An effective facilitator compiles this data, re-checks to verify accuracy and is prepared to back brief the unit
     on what took place. This ensures that all participants start the discussion with a common understanding of
     what took place. Without this objective data up front, the AAR risks devolving into a battle of contrasting
     narratives and opinions.

Understanding of Root Causes (Why It Happened)

     3-10. Once a common understanding has been achieved, the facilitator now guides the review using a series
     of directed questions intended to open a dialogue between and among participants. The facilitator does not
     ask yes or no questions but encourages participation and guides discussion by using open-ended and leading
     questions. An open-ended question has no specific answer and allows participants to reply based on what
     was significant to them. Open-ended questions are also much less likely to put Soldiers on the defensive.
     This is more effective in finding out what happened. For example, it is better to ask, "SGT Johnson, what
     happened when your Bradley crested the hill?" rather than, "SGT Johnson, why didn't you engage the enemy
     tanks to your front?”

     3-11. As a rule, it is more effective to ask direct questions of the more junior personnel in the training
     audience so that their perspective can be heard and understood by the unit leadership. A good facilitator is
     comfortable allowing the conversation to expand so that participants are communicating with each other and
     not just with the facilitator. As the discussion expands and more Soldiers add their perspectives, the facilitator
     steers the conversation toward key root causes that led to specific outcomes during the training event. Often
     these key root causes are recorded on a whiteboard or butcher block for all participants to see. If done
     properly, these root causes emerge organically from the discussion. Remember, this is not a critique or
     lecture; the facilitator does not tell the Soldiers or leaders what was good or bad. However, the facilitator
     ensures specific issues are revealed, both positive and negative in nature.

                                                                                         Step 3: Conducting an After Action Review

Discussion of Relevant Doctrine, OPORDs, Standard Operating Procedures or Historical
Context

     3-12. Once the facilitator feels that the unit has sufficiently analyzed a particular topic, they close out the
     discussion with a reference to unit OPORDs, Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), Army doctrine or
     approved training circulars. This is accomplished with doctrinal teach slides that are prepared before the
     exercise or by asking participants to read a select passage from a doctrinal manual. The idea is to show the
     unit how the proper application of the unit’s SOP or Army doctrinal principles might have addressed some
     of the root causes identified in the discussion. In nearly every case, a proper understanding of Army doctrine
     can provide a better framework for tackling tough tactical problems. In cases where doctrine does not cover
     a particular issue well, examples from past conflicts in history often provide a powerful teaching tool to
     highlight the importance of disciplined execution, and the consequences of mistakes.

AAR Methodology

     3-13. There are many approaches the facilitators use to help organize and facilitate discussion amongst
     participants in the AAR. Leaders consider organizing the discussion using one of three techniques:

         • Chronological order of events.
         • Warfighting functions.
         • Key Events/Themes/Issues.

     3-14. Chronological Order of Events: This technique is logical, structured, and easy to understand. It follows
     the flow of training from start to finish and allows Soldiers to see the effects of their actions on other units
     and events. By covering actions in the sequential order in which they took place, Soldiers and leaders are
     better able to recall what happened.

     3-15. Warfighting Functions: By focusing on each warfighting function and discussing it across all phases of
     the training event/exercise, participants can identify systemic strengths and weaknesses. This technique is
     particularly useful in training staff sections whose duties and responsibilities directly relate to one or more
     warfighting function. However, facilitators must ensure they are careful not to lose sight of the big picture.
     They do not get into long discussions about the warfighting functions which do not relate to mission
     accomplishment. The Army’s warfighting functions are:

         • Command and Control.
         • Movement and Maneuver.
         • Intelligence.
         • Fires.
         • Protection.
         • Sustainment.

     3-16. Key Events/Themes/Issues. A key events discussion focuses on critical training events which directly
     support training objectives the chain of command identified before the event/exercise began. Keeping a tight
     focus on these events prevents the discussion from becoming sidetracked by issues which do not relate to
     training objectives. This technique is particularly effective when time is limited.

     3-17. One of the strengths of the AAR format is its flexibility. The leader could use the chronological format
     to structure the discussion, then, if a particular warfighting function seems to have systemic issues that the
     group needs to address, follow that warfighting function across the entire exercise. Once that topic is
     exhausted, the AAR proceeds using the chronological format. Each technique generates discussion, and
     identifies unit strengths, weaknesses, and training the unit needs to improve proficiency.

     3-18. However, facilitators must remember to:
         • Be specific, avoiding generalizations.
         • Be thorough.
         • Not dwell on issues unrelated to mission accomplishment.
         • Focus on actions.
         • Relate performance to the accomplishment of training objectives.
         • Identify corrective action for areas of weakness.
         • Continually summarize.

Chapter 3

Safety Issues

     3-19. Safety is every Soldier’s business and applies to everything a unit does in the field and in garrison.
     Safety is specifically addressed in every AAR and discussed in detail when it impacts unit effectiveness or
     Soldier well-being. It is important to treat safety precautions as integral parts of every operation.

     3-20. All incidents or near incidents of fratricide, whether inflicted by direct fire, indirect fire, or close air
     support (CAS), are discussed in detail. The leader focuses on identifying the cause of the fratricide and assists
     in developing SOPs and TTPs to prevent it in the future. Regardless of the environment (training or combat),
     the leader swiftly deals with all fratricide incidents. As soon as possible after the event, an AAR is conducted
     to discuss the circumstances surrounding the event, using the following discussion points:

         • How and why did the incident occur?
         • How were friendly personnel and equipment identified?
         • What fire control measures were in place where the fratricide occurred and how effective were they?
         • How did the commander's risk assessment and overall intent for the mission address the issue of

              fratricide?

Immediate Actions and Future Recommendations (The So What)

     3-21. The AAR is a problem-solving process. The purpose of the discussion is for participants to discover
     strengths and weaknesses, propose solutions, and adopt a course of action to correct problems. An effective
     AAR should produce a consensus on immediate fixes for implementation by the organization and who
     specifically is responsible for ensuring their completion. It is vital that the facilitator leave time (often 15
     minutes or so) at the end of the discussion to summarize root causes, and then ask the unit leadership to
     identify the immediate fixes that they will prioritize. Most importantly the group should ‘pin the rose’ on a
     specific individual to lead the implementation of an agreed upon fix. This is best recorded on a white board
     for all to see. If the facilitator plans to conduct multiple AARs with the same training audience over the
     course of an exercise, it is useful to save this record as a starting point for the next AAR. Following this
     discussion, the facilitator ends the AAR on a positive note, linking conclusions to future training, then leaves
     the immediate area to allow unit leaders and Soldiers time to discuss the training in private.

  Step 4: Follow Up (Using the Results of the After Action
                                    Review)

OVERVIEW

     4-1. Nearly as important as the AAR itself are the leaders’ actions following an AAR. While the AAR is an
     invaluable tool in identifying training shortfalls and correcting these, the lessons learned and how they’re
     applied and shared throughout the unit and the Army are critical in improving overall training proficiency.

IMMEDIATE RETRAINING

     4-2. Retraining is immediately necessary to address particularly weak task proficiencies. By applying AAR
     results, a unit can improve its performance to meet the Army standard. However, the focus of this effort is
     not to get a ‘passing grade,’ but to improve Soldier and unit task performance. By the end of an AAR, Soldiers
     must clearly understand what was good, bad, or average about their performance.

     4-3. FM 7-0 requires leaders to schedule time for retraining as a normal part of the planning process. The
     unit always retrains and performs tasks to standard before progressing to the next level of task complexity.
     This reinforces the learning process by immediately correcting substandard performance. When there is not
     enough time to retrain tasks, the leader integrates those tasks into the unit's training plan and reschedules
     them. As part of this process, leaders ensure that deficient supporting tasks identified during the AAR are
     also scheduled and retrained.

REVISE STANDARD OPERATING PROCEDURES

     4-4. After action reviews may reveal shortfalls, or better operating techniques. If so, unit leaders revise unit
     SOPs to reflect these improvements and changes. This is a continuous process as the unit learns from
     performance and integrates what it learned through updates to its SOPs. In this way, unit leaders ensure that
     lessons learned are captured for future performance.

EVALUATE THE AAR PROCESS

     4-5. Following any training event and exercise, unit leaders review their AAR procedures – in planning,
     preparing, and conducting AARs. From selecting evaluators, to positioning them to observe training, to
     facilitator actions, every aspect of the AAR process is evaluated for effectiveness. This process is also
     valuable in teaching new facilitators the unit’s AAR process – and provides feedback to revising unit SOPs.

INTEGRATE AND SHARE LESSONS LEARNED

     4-6. Implementing and sharing lessons learned from AARs is an important final step in the AAR process.
     Commanders use AARs and lessons learned to shape future training and operations to correct deficiencies
     and sustain superior performance. Lessons learned from the AAR process are always recorded.

Recording AAR Results

     4-7. Unit leaders ensure the results of AARs are recorded for future review and use. Whether maintaining
     AAR documentation and results locally in unit files or using web-based systems like the Digital Training
     Management System (DTMS), this is a vital last step in the AAR process. Just as importantly, reviewing
     these records after the fact is very important. For example, a unit planning a field training exercise (FTX),
     refers to all available AAR records and reports prior to the next unit FTX. Some examples of AAR records
     the unit retains include:

         • Formal AAR reports (see below).
         • Informal notes and observations.
         • Multi-media records of unit performance and AARs conducted during the training. These may

              include audio and video files; unit graphics; instrumented displays, etc.

After Action Reports
     4-8. Formally recording AAR results is done using after action reports. These are an important collection
     technique. (See FM 6-0 for more on after action reports.) The report provides observations and insights from
     lessons learned providing unit leaders an opportunity to reflect on the successes and shortcomings of
     operations and training, as well as share these lessons with other units and across the Army. Effective leaders
     use the after action report for building future training plans or for planning future operations.
     4-9. The after action report differs from the after action review since it is a written record highlighting unit
     accomplishments and lessons learned. The after action report works well as a historical reference provided
     to other organizations preparing to conduct a similar operation or training event.
     4-10. The following is a sample content of a brigade-level after action report. Commanders tailor the report
     as appropriate (See FM 6-0 for more details):
          1. Report cover page.
          2. Preface or foreword signed by the commander.
          3. Table of contents.
          4. Executive summary and chronology of significant events.
          5. Detailed task organization. Include any significant changes and dates as appropriate.
          6. Pre-deployment phase with dates.
          7. Deployment and reception, staging, onward movement, and integration (RSOI) with dates.
          8. Relief in place or transfer of authority with dates (if applicable).
          9. Operational phase with dates.
          10. Redeployment activities with dates.
          11. Post-deployment activities.
          12. Provide an index or listing of all mid-tour and final unit after action report products, significant
     command briefings, or reports published separately.

          14. Appendixes.
