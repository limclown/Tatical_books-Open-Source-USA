                                 Preface

TC 1-19.30, Percussion Techniques, provides Army percussionists and auxiliary percussionists with common
techniques and considerations for the successful performance of their job. It describes percussion instruments
used by Army Bands, with performance and maintenance techniques for each instrument. This publication is
a reference guide to be used when needed.

The principal audience for TC 1-19.30 is Army percussionists, auxiliary percussionists, trainers, and musical
supervisors. Army percussionists hold military occupational specialty (MOS) 42R and additional skill
identifier (ASI) 9M. Auxiliary percussionists are MOS 42R and normally hold the ASI 9H, 9K, 9N, 9T, 9U,
9V, or 9X.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure that their decisions and actions comply with applicable United
States, international, and in some cases host-nation laws and regulations. Commanders at all levels ensure
that their Soldiers operate in accordance with the law of armed conflict and the rules of engagement (see FM
6-27/MCTP 11-10C).

This publication is not the proponent publication for any Army terms.

This publication serves as the primary reference for both resident and nonresident percussion techniques
instruction presented to Soldiers, noncommissioned officers, warrant officer bandmasters, and commissioned
officers. Trainers and first-line supervisors should ensure Soldiers holding ASI of 9M, 9H, 9K, 9N, 9T, 9U,
9V, and 9X (skill level 1-4) have access to this publication. It should be made available in the Soldiers’ work
area, unit learning center, and unit libraries.

As this publication is a guide, and as all possible situations and eventualities cannot be foreseen or covered
by the manual, great reliance must be placed upon the application of sound judgment and common sense by
all members of an Army Band. In situations not covered by this manual and where doubt arises as to the
proper action to be taken, the individual must consider their mission and apply sound judgment in making
the required decision(s).

TC 1-19.30 applies to the Active Army, the Army National Guard, the Army National Guard of the United
States, and the United States Army Reserve unless otherwise stated.

                              Introduction

TC 1-19.30 is a reference guide for MOS percussionists (MOS 42R, with the ASI of 9M) and auxiliary
percussionists (MOS 42R, with the ASI of 9H, 9K, 9N, 9T, 9U, 9V, and 9X).

This revision of TC 1-19.30 reorganizes the content for better readability and discusses how Army
percussionists support Army Bands operations.

Army percussionists are expected to be proficient in a wider variety of instruments than any other ASI in the
proponent. While civilian percussionists may specialize on one percussion instrument, the Army requires a
percussionist to be responsible for over 50 percussion instruments. It is not possible to attain proficiency in
all percussion instruments solely in the time allotted during Army institutional training (e.g., advanced
individual training (AIT)). Therefore, this publication is intended to be used as a reference when a
percussionist or auxiliary percussionist is tasked to perform on an instrument with which they are less
familiar.

TC 1-19.30 should be used in conjunction with TC 1-19.10 as a reference for Soldiers seeking further
knowledge of Army Bands ceremonial music topics discussed in chapter three of this publication.

       Text conventions: This publication uses unique text conventions in chapter three to facilitate
       understanding: preparatory commands are Italicized in Bold, commands of execution and
       directives are CAPITALIZED IN BOLD, references to positions and movements are Italicized,
       and names of music or bugle calls are placed in “Italicized Quotations.”

Although this publication can be used as a reference, Soldiers are highly encouraged to research for
themselves the wealth of resources available in print and on the internet. A brief list of recommended
resources is provided in appendix A. For advanced percussion studies, Soldiers are encouraged to pursue
private lessons with local professionals, or with U.S. Army School of Music percussion instructors via
Microsoft Teams or similar live video call mediums. Additionally, Soldiers are encouraged to request
mentorship through the Army Musician Mentorship Program (AMMP).

TC 1-19.30 contains four chapters and one appendix:

Chapter 1 describes the history, task organization, and general rules for percussion performance in Army
Bands.

Chapter 2 provides an overview of the drum set in Army Bands small ensembles.

Chapter 3 provides an overview of military ceremonial music with performance considerations and
techniques for the percussion instruments used in ceremonial ensembles.

Chapter 4 provides general considerations and playing techniques for the most common instruments used in
concert band.

Appendix A provides instrument-specific resources for self-study and maintenance.

                                                 Chapter 1

                       Percussion in Army Bands

    From rolling in the national colors during a military ceremony to performing in concert halls,
    Army percussionists are expected to be proficient on a wider variety of instruments and can
    perform in more instrumental ensembles than any other ASI in Army Bands. This chapter
    describes the history, task organization, and general rules for percussion performance in Army
    Bands.

HISTORY

     1-1. The Army has employed drummers since its inception. The use of drummers in American military bands
     derives from the European tradition. Colonial militias based on British design used fife and drums for field
     music. This music was used to summon Soldiers, practice drills, and conduct movements on the battlefield.
     Several regiments in the militias had “bands of musick” that operated in addition to the fifes and drums to
     provide concert music for troop morale, entertainment, and civil engagement.

     1-2. The Army continued to use the drum as a signaling device from its victory during the American
     Revolution through the Civil War, where it gave way to the bugle. During the Civil War, drummers were
     used for conducting troop movement on and off the battlefield, with several being awarded the Medal of
     Honor. Off the battlefield, drummers entertained Soldiers and civilians while performing with brass or wind
     bands which became the most accessible means of presenting popular music of the time.

     1-3. As popular music changed through the “Golden Age of the March,” to the introduction of ragtime and
     jazz in the beginning of the 20th century, to the introduction of rock and roll and beyond, the skills required
     of Army percussionists have changed dramatically. Today, modern Army Bands continue to provide field
     music while providing musical support to military ceremonies. Bands also provide popular music while
     engaging local, regional, national, and international audiences, both at home and abroad (see ATP 1-19).

     1-4. With these varied mission requirements, today’s Army percussionists must be versatile enough to
     perform in a variety of musical situations. They must:

          • Adapt their drum set skills to perform modern popular music as well as popular music from the last
                hundred years.

          • Maintain the rudimental proficiency required for martial field music.
          • Maintain proficiency on the performance of concert percussion instruments.

TASK ORGANIZATION IN ARMY BANDS

     1-5. Army Bands have a variety of resources with which to accomplish musical support missions. Bands
     operate as the collective unit, or they are task-organized into small, individually employed music performance
     teams (MPT). A music performance team is a team that offers the flexibility to support concurrent musical
     missions in multiple geographical areas (ATP 1-19). Army percussionists are utilized in a variety of musical
     elements that fall into three main categories: small ensembles, ceremonial ensembles, and concert band.

SMALL ENSEMBLES

     1-6. Depending on local mission requirements, the largest percentage of an Army percussionist’s job is
     performing on drum set in small ensembles. Percussionists are assigned to MPTs with a variety of
     instrumentation, and repertoire spanning many musical genres and styles (see paragraphs 2-1 through 2-3).

CEREMONIAL ENSEMBLES

     1-7. Bands provide support to military ceremonies as a whole unit or smaller groups of musicians to form a
     ceremonial ensemble. Percussionists primarily perform on snare drum, bass drum, and cymbals while
     assigned to ceremonial ensembles. Depending on the availability of personnel, non-ASI percussionists (ASIs
     9H, 9K, 9N, 9T, 9U, and 9V) that do not double on another brass or wind instrument, may be assigned as

Marching Band

     1-8. The marching band is an instrumental element that is organized into a prescribed formation and is led
     by a drum major or conductor. The percussion section consists of a bass drum, cymbals, and one to three
     snare drums, but varies depending on the formation size and available personnel. This ensemble is the only

Ceremonial Band

     1-9. Ceremonial bands are led by a conductor, and musicians perform either seated or standing. There are no
     prescribed formations for these ensembles, they conform to the space available and may adopt a variety of
     shapes such as platoon in line, platoon in column, concert arcs, or horseshoe formations (see TC 1-19.10).
     The percussion section consists of a bass drum, cymbals, and a snare drum, but can vary based on musical
     and mission requirements.

CONCERT BAND

     1-10. Typically using all available unit personnel, the concert band is an instrumental element capable of
     performing symphonic music and arrangements from musical theatre and popular music. Percussionists
     perform on a wide variety of pitched instruments (e.g., timpani, keyboard percussion, etc.), non-pitched
     instruments (e.g., snare drum, bass drum, cymbals, etc.), and drum set, often playing multiple instruments
     within a single piece depending on musical requirements and available personnel. Rhythm players (ASIs 9N,
     9T, 9U) are often tasked as auxiliary percussionists due to the number of instruments and personnel required
     for modern symphonic compositions.

GENERAL RULES FOR PERCUSSION PERFORMANCE

     1-11. Army percussionists must understand that it is their personal responsibility to achieve and sustain a
     high level of proficiency on their assigned instrument(s). The reality of most percussionists and percussion
     sections is that players have varying strengths and weaknesses based on their personal performance
     experience, education, and musical backgrounds. Weaknesses are often highlighted due to a lack of
     experience and the sheer number of instruments in the percussion family, which each require many years of
     study to master.

     1-12. Experience is the percussionist’s greatest asset. Only through listening, study, practice, trial and error,
     and performance can the percussionist learn the techniques necessary to perform the repertoire. This
     publication is designed to guide percussionists’ study and explain common techniques and considerations for
     the successful performance of their job.

     1-13. Many skills are transferrable on percussion instruments, as instruments share common techniques and
     striking areas. For example, the matched grip used on snare drum can be transferred for use on timpani or
     keyboard percussion instruments. Therefore, with small technique adjustments, basic techniques on many
     percussion instruments can easily be learned.

TONE PRODUCTION

     1-14. Applying technique to performance requires that the performer develop knowledge in five categories
     of percussive tone production: striking areas, striking implement choice, stroke technique, muffling
     technique, and instrument selection. Percussionists must become proficient in applying these categories for
     each instrument they play, as they can be used as a checklist for accurate practice and performance. To
     address these categories further, discussion of instrument specifics in chapters two through four will examine
     (when applicable):

          • Physical composition and construction.
          • Acoustical characteristics.
          • Correct tuning.

Striking Areas

     1-15. The sound of a percussion instrument changes drastically depending on where it is struck.
     Percussionists must know the physical sound qualities of each instrument with respect to tone production.
     Only then can they determine the best striking areas to use for performance. The player must apply their
     knowledge of those qualities, use their imagination, be creative, and experiment to produce the sounds
     required by the music.

Striking Implement Choice

     1-16. Musical timbre and articulation are directly related to the implement used to strike a percussion
     instrument. Articulations must be analyzed to determine the attack and timbre needed for the music. Choose
     implements according to these general rules:

          • Select an implement with the appropriate mass (i.e., weight) to achieve the desired or characteristic
                sound of the instrument (determined by the genre or style of music). For example:
                 Do not use heavy marching snare sticks when playing jazz drum set.
                 Do not use lighter drum set sticks when playing concert snare drum.

          • The harder the implement, the sharper the attack (i.e., how articulate the sound is).
          • The softer the implement, the mellower the attack (i.e., how round the sound is).
          • Never strike an instrument with anything harder than the surface or material of the instrument (e.g.,

                never use a metal mallet on wood).

Stroke Technique

     1-17. The motions involved in striking any percussion instrument determine the intensity of the sound. There
     are myriad grips, techniques, and approaches used to strike percussion instruments, and many are used
     interchangeably or in conjunction with each other. Regardless of the grip or technique used, in its simplest
     form, a stroke has two parts, the attack and rebound.

Attack

     1-18. Attack is the motion of the striking implement to the point of impact on the instrument. The height and
     speed of the stroke determines the intensity of the sound.

Rebound

     1-19. Rebound is the motion of the striking implement after impact. A natural rebound, when the implement
     freely bounces off the playing surface, is preferred in most circumstances. Controlling the natural rebound
     allows for quicker dynamic changes (e.g., differentiating between accented and unaccented notes).

Muffling Techniques

     1-20. Muffling is the control of tone duration. The style of articulation, acoustics of the performance venue,
     resonance and pitch of the instrument, tempo, complexity of the melodic lines, and the harmonic structure of
     the music determine the type and degree of muffling required. Instruments which require muffling or
     resonance control can be muffled manually (e.g., with the fingers, hand, or body) or with a muffling device
     (e.g., towel, handkerchief, gels, tone rings, etc.). Muffling varies based on the instrument and style of music
     being performed, but can be applied via two general rules:

          • Always give the note its full value (e.g., articulations or note duration).
          • Avoid changing the instrument’s characteristic sound (e.g., over muffling).

     1-21. Instrument selection can have a significant effect on overall tone production and each of the previous
     categories. The myriads of variations in percussion instruments allow performers endless opportunities to

ENSEMBLE DYNAMICS

     1-22. A commonly overlooked aspect of percussion performance is the application of dynamics. Percussion
     instruments in general are very loud and can easily overpower both large and small ensembles. Percussionists
     and non-9M ASI performers that have little to no experience with varying styles of music or performing in
     different ensembles often overplay percussion instruments. Percussionists must approach dynamics within
     ensemble dynamics. Playing with ensemble dynamics is matching the volume of the entire ensemble or
     section within an ensemble. Unless the music is notated otherwise (e.g., part marked as “solo”) or the
     ensemble leader specifically requests the instrument to play louder, it is recommended to play slightly softer
     than the marked dynamic. This will help the instrument to blend into the texture and avoid overpowering the
     ensemble.

                                                 Chapter 2

                              Small Ensembles

    Army Bands are task-organized into small individually employed MPTs that perform a
    spectrum of diverse musical genres and styles. These teams employ percussionists in a variety
    of ways through drum set and world percussion performance. This chapter provides
    percussionists with an overview and performance considerations for the drum set in Army

SECTION I – TASK ORGANIZATION

     2-1. Throughout their career in Army Bands, percussionists will be assigned to perform on drum set in
     various MPTs, each presenting unique challenges and opportunities. The contemporary Army percussionist
     must possess a multifaceted skill set, capable of navigating a diverse musical landscape. With repertoire
     spanning from modern pop and rock to intricate jazz ensembles, to ceremonial marches and vibrant salsa
     rhythms, percussionists must be proficient in adapting between musical genres and styles.

     2-2. Regardless of the type of ensemble assigned, Army percussionists should resist the temptation of taking
     shortcuts. While there are countless resources available in print and on the internet, true proficiency on drum
     set stems from a deep immersion in each musical genre and style. It is through dedicated practice and a
     commitment to mastering diverse rhythms and techniques that percussionists ensure their performances
     resonate with stylistic authenticity.

     2-3. Percussionists may be assigned to perform on drum set or percussion in the following MPTs:
          • Rhythm Ensembles – Jazz or popular elements consisting of rhythm instruments with optional
                horns or vocals. Examples include big band, New Orleans jazz band, jazz combo, contemporary pop
                or rock band, Latin band, and country band.
          • Brass Ensembles – Chamber or popular elements consisting of brass instruments with occasional
                percussion. Examples include brass quintet, trombone quartet, and brass band.
          • Woodwind Ensembles – Instrumental elements consisting of woodwind instruments, French horn,
                and occasionally percussion. Examples include woodwind quintet, clarinet quartet, and non-
                traditional instrumentation mostly consisting of woodwind instruments.
          • Other – Instrumental elements performing regionally-specific music. Examples include German
                fest band, pipes and drums, and steel drum ensemble.

SECTION II – THE DRUM SET IN SMALL ENSEMBLES

     2-4. This section describes the function of the drum set and its role within small ensembles.

FUNCTION OF THE DRUM SET

     2-5. The drum set has the following functions:
          • To provide a steady pulse for the ensemble.
          • To help define the feel, style, and genre.
          • To clearly mark the form.
          • To provide accents, kicks, and fills to assist other members of the ensemble in the performance of
                their parts.

TIME AND PULSE

     2-6. All musicians have personal responsibility to correctly interpret time and pulse. However, it is the
     drummer and rhythm section’s interpretation that will set the foundation for ensemble cohesion. Drummers
     develop a solid foundation of time and pulse through experience playing with other musicians and consistent
     practice with a metronome and recordings.

GENRE AND STYLE

     2-7. As popular music has evolved, the repertoire for drum set has expanded to include a variety of musical
     genres and styles. In its simplest form, genre is used to categorize types of music (e.g., rock, pop, jazz, world,
     etc.), and style informs how the music is performed (e.g., straight or swung). Understanding both is essential
     for authentic performance on drum set.

     2-8. How a drummer contributes to the music greatly informs both the genre and style of the music being
     performed. For example, it would be stylistically incorrect to perform a straight eighth-note rock groove
     when performing big band jazz. With the exception of arrangements in a contrasting style or when
     specifically directed by the ensemble leader, drummers should perform stylistically authentic grooves
     appropriate for both the genre and style of the music.

SONG FORM

     2-9. Every piece of music has a form that should be analyzed and understood to ensure proper performance.
     When performers know the form of the tune, they rarely get lost, will play better because they can listen and
     contribute rather than be buried in their music, and be able to treat the tune as a whole, rather than
     mechanically, measure by measure.

     2-10. A phrase is a grouping of measures into a larger unit. Phrases typically fall in 4, 8, 12, and 16 bar
     groups. To mark the form, the drummer must be able to hear and feel these phrases. This greatly aids
     counting, reading, and playing. The simplest form is the three-part ABA form: A = Statement, B = Departure
     or Bridge, and A = Return or Restatement.

     2-11. Most popular music uses a variation of this form. The ‘A’ phrase is commonly repeated, producing an
     AABA form. The ‘B’ phrase, better known as the bridge, is a transition or release that departs from the ‘A’
     phrase melodically and harmonically. The ‘A’ phrase returns to complete the form, usually differing in
     musical texture and dynamics from the original ‘A’ phrase. If more than half of the last ‘A’ phrase is
     materially different, then the form will be AABC.

     2-12. Drummers must know the sequence of these phrases to play effectively with the ensemble. It is good
     practice to emphasize the beginning of a phrase to let the band know exactly where they are, especially during
     improvised solos. This can be done by accenting the down beat on a crash cymbal, or by varying the timbre
     on the drum set (e.g., switching from the hi-hat to ride cymbal) to indicate a contrast or change in the form.

     2-13. Fills are used to connect phrases together and should be played to fit the musical texture at that point
     in the music. By analyzing the form, the drummer will know how to treat each fill and contribute tastefully
     to the overall effectiveness of the piece.

ACCENTS AND KICKS

     2-14. Accents, kicks, and fills are used to assist the band in the performance of their parts, or to mark sections
     within the form, and are divided into three parts: the prep, the accent or kick, and the return.

The Prep

     2-15. The prep is the note(s) that set(s) up the figure to be accented or kicked. The prep should be in the style
     of the music being performed. Preps are usually played on the snare drum but may be played on any drum
     including the bass drum.

The Kick

     2-16. The kick is the note(s) or figure(s) being emphasized. The figure may be on the beat, off the beat, or
     several notes played in succession. Long notes/figures will usually, but not always, be accented with the bass
     drum and cymbal. Short notes will usually be played on a shorter sounding instrument (e.g., snare drum or
     tom-tom). The drummer must not destroy the integrity of the articulation of the line being accented (e.g.,
     playing a long sound for a short figure).

The Return

     2-17. Following the accent or kick, or when marking the form, the drummer resumes playing time on the
     next full beat.

FILLS

     2-18. Fills are functionally extended preps. In most cases, a fill will terminate in a kick with a section, the
     entire band/ensemble, or by marking the new section of the form. The following concepts must be considered
     when performing fills:

          • Individual technique (i.e., “chops”).
          • Rhythmic motifs within the style.
          • Dynamic contrasts.
          • Use of available instrumental timbres for tonal variety.

             Note. Many drummers have a tendency to fluctuate their time when performing fills. Drummers
             must maintain strict time when setting up an ensemble figure or new section of the form.

RHYTHM ENSEMBLES

     2-19. Rhythm ensemble is a task organization identification and is associated with a type of ensemble (i.e.,
     instrumentation), rather than a specific genre of music. Rhythm ensembles in Army Bands are smaller in size
     and vary in instrumentation, with the exception of the larger big band jazz ensemble. These ensembles
     include, but are not limited to, big band, New Orleans jazz band, jazz combo, contemporary pop or rock band,
     Latin band, and country band. Ensembles typically have an amplified rhythm section (e.g., bass, guitar(s),
     and piano). These ensembles have a versatile repertoire including, but not limited to, the genres and subgenres
     of rock, funk, Latin, and jazz.

     2-20. The following are considerations for the drummer’s role in the genres commonly performed in rhythm
     ensembles. The information provided is not meant to be all inclusive but rather a broad overview. These
     concepts can be applied to other ensembles when performing these genres.

ROCK

     2-21. There are many approaches to the rock genre, encompassing various subgenres and generational styles
     (including subgenres of pop and country). Most rock genres rely on foundational straight eighth-note grooves
     that have stood the test of time. Despite their simplicity, mere replication of notes falls short when performing
     these grooves. Authentic sounding grooves in this genre result from the balance between the component parts
     of the drum set.

     2-22. The bass drum and snare drum are the dominant voices in most rock drum grooves. Bass drum notes
     should be played with intensity and often match the bass player. Backbeats performed on the snare drum
     must be consistent and require relentless practice and refinement. The hi-hat and ride cymbal maintain time
     within the groove and provide sonic variation between sections of songs (e.g., hi-hat for verses and ride
     cymbal for choruses). Cymbals should be played slightly lower in volume to allow the bass drum and snare
     drum to maintain their dominance.

FUNK

     2-23. Funk is characterized by the interlocking rhythmic pattern of the electric bass and drum set. The
     grooves commonly associated with funk music range from foundational straight eighth-note grooves like
     those used in rock, to complex linear grooves requiring greater limb independence (e.g., any drum groove in
     which no two limbs play at the same). Depending on the generational style of funk, these grooves may be
     played with a straight, swung, or a “between the cracks” (i.e., between straight and swung) interpretation of
     eighth notes. Groove authenticity relies on eighth-note interpretation and balance between the component
     parts of the drum set.

     2-24. The bass drum and snare drum remain the dominant voices in funk grooves. Bass drum notes should
     be played with intensity and are often more syncopated to match the bass player. Backbeats must maintain

LATIN

     2-25. Latin music encompasses the musical genres and styles of the Spanish and Portuguese speaking
     countries of the Western hemisphere. These genres are heavily influenced by African rhythms and are
     traditionally performed with a section of percussion instruments. Modern interpretations require the drum set
     player to perform an approximation of the percussion section’s parts, requiring advanced limb independence.
     The ability to synthesize these disparate percussion parts into a unified whole is paramount for authenticity
     in these styles, and varies based on the country of origin, and instruments being imitated.

     2-26. There is not a dominant voice in most Latin drum grooves, but rather a dominant motor rhythm known
     as the clave. Drummers must balance the volume between their limbs in service of the groove while
     maintaining and supporting the clave rhythm. The bass drum often matches or supports the underlying bass
     groove. When performing with traditional percussion instruments, drum set parts should change based on the
     instruments being played (e.g., avoid playing congas parts on tom-toms if playing with a conga player).

JAZZ

     2-27. Jazz music serves as a cultural touchstone, reflecting the spirit of its time while leaving an indelible
     mark on the trajectory of the popular music of today. Early jazz evolved with popular dances, becoming the
     preferred dance music of the first half of the 20th century. As the genre evolved into its many subgenres it
     shifted from the more straightforward and rhythmically uniform style associated with dance music to the
     introduction of new levels of complexity and dynamism requiring greater musical and technical prowess
     from its performers.

     2-28. The defining characteristic of jazz and the swing style of drumming is the division of the beat. This
     division uses the 2/3:1/3 ratio emphasizing the triplet, rather than the 1/2:1/2 ratio of straight eighth-notes.
     The ride cymbal pattern and its variations are the dominant voice in jazz grooves, as it establishes the swing
     feel for the ensemble. The other essential voice in jazz grooves is the hi-hat, which provides the “backbeat”
     of the groove depending on the meter (e.g., on two and four in 4/4, or two and three in 3/4). The bass drum
     and snare drum voices are more interactive and serve to accompany or react to other musicians in the
     ensemble. Later subgenres of jazz see the drum set move away from being a straightforward timekeeper to
     more of a melodic voice in compositions.

SECTION III – PERFORMANCE CHARTS

     2-45. This section provides guidance on reading and creating performance charts used for drum set.

MANUSCRIPT/PUBLISHER CONVENTIONS

     2-46. There are variations from arranger to arranger and publisher to publisher, however, most drum set parts
     are written within the convention of other percussion parts, with:

          • The bass drum notated on the bottom space (i.e., F space in treble clef or A space of bass clef).
          • The snare drum on the 3rd space (i.e., C space in treble clef or E space of bass clef).
          • Other drums/cymbals identified on the part (e.g., drums within the staff, and cymbals above (e.g.,

                played with the hands) and below the staff (e.g., played with the feet)).

TYPES OF CHARTS

     2-47. There are two common types of drum parts: verbatim parts, and style sheets.

VERBATIM PARTS

     2-48. The most literal version of a verbatim part is a transcription. These parts are written note for note from
     a recorded performance. These parts are helpful when studying other drummers or when trying to replicate
     recognizable popular recordings and performances.

     2-49. The most common types of verbatim parts are not strict transcriptions, but rather include drum parts,
     patterns, or ensemble hits to be performed. The notation is a guide to illustrate the composer/arranger’s intent.
     How closely the drummer follows these parts will be dependent on their experience level and the ensemble
     leader’s preference.

STYLE SHEETS

     2-50. Most drum set parts are written as style sheets. These are often minimally written, outlining the generic
     style of the music (e.g., swing, rock, or Latin) and illustrating kicks and fills where appropriate or required.
     They may or may not identify sections of the form. These types of parts rely on drummers to interpret the
     music (see figure 2-1). When performing from style sheets, inexperienced drummers are encouraged to study
     recordings (when possible) and mark their parts with notes that will aid in performance. See paragraph 2-56
     and 2-59 for suggested notes that may be added to style sheets.

CREATING CHARTS

     2-51. For many rhythm ensembles (e.g., contemporary pop or rock band, country band, and jazz combo),

     2-52. Although many drummers perform from memory, they are encouraged to create charts when learning
     new tunes for rehearsals and as a resource for substitute drummers. There are many different ways to create
     parts for drum set and their level of detail may vary based on personal preference, experience, and familiarity
     with the source material.

SECTION IV – INDIVIDUAL TECHNICAL SKILLS

     2-60. This section provides a broad overview of the individual technical skills required of drum set players.
     The information provided may apply to several subject areas.

TECHNIQUE

     2-61. When discussing technique on drum set, it primarily applies to limb independence, coordination, and
     control over the hands and feet. Coordination and balance between limbs must reach a high level to overcome
     rigidity in grooves and requires diligent work. For example, when performing a basic rock groove:

          • For the hands:
                 The lead/dominant hand should maintain consistent eighth notes on the hi-hat with accents on
                     every beat using the stick's shaft, while striking the upbeats with the stick's tip.
                 The snare drum must maintain consistent and intense backbeats on beats two and four.

          • For the feet:
                 The bass drum foot should be powerful while matching the intensity of the backbeat.
                 The pressure applied to the hi-hat pedal will determine how open the hi-hats sound.

     2-62. Rigid coordination, such as accenting bass drum notes (e.g., syncopations) with the hi-hat, indicates a
     lack of separation between limb movements. The alignment of limbs and notes must be precise and accurate.
     For example, if playing a “4 on the floor” groove, bass drum notes on beats two and four should precisely
     align with snare drum notes on the same beats.

ACCURACY

     2-63. Accuracy on drum set covers a gamut of considerations, including:
          • Stylistic accuracy (e.g., Is the groove correct for the style of music?).
          • Attention to detail in note placement:
                 Are limbs aligning together in time?
                 Are rhythms being played correctly (e.g., straight or swung) and in time?
          • Marking the form:
                 Is the drummer clearly marking the form by hitting a cymbal crash at the beginning of each
                     section?

TIME AND RHYTHM

     2-65. As described in paragraph 2-6, all musicians have responsibility for maintaining time and pulse.
     Consistency in tempo is fundamental for drummers in every musical genre, ensuring predictability for the
     ensemble. Maintaining an even spacing between beats is essential for achieving a steady tempo, which, in
     turn, enhances the groove and overall feel of the music. A steady tempo not only facilitates musical cohesion
     but also contributes to the enjoyment and engagement of both performers and listeners.

     2-66. Beyond technical proficiency, accuracy, and tempo maintenance, drummers must also cultivate a deep
     understanding of musical context and stylistic nuances. This includes familiarizing themselves with the
     rhythmic conventions (e.g., straight, swung, or “between the cracks”) of different genres and adapting their
     playing accordingly. Whether it is the driving eighth notes of rock, the intricate syncopations of funk, or the
     dynamic improvisations of jazz, drummers must tailor their rhythmic approach to suit the demands of each
     style and time signature.

FEEL/GROOVE

     2-67. Feel and groove is how music flows, a seemingly intangible quality that resonates with both performers
     and listeners alike. It is about feeling the rhythm rather than overthinking it. For drummers, it is about creating
     a groove that is catchy, makes people move, and keeps them engaged. This involves:

          • Keeping steady time.
          • Playing a groove that matches the music's style and genre.
          • Staying in sync with other musicians.

     2-68. Finding the right balance between consistency and creativity is key and may be as simple as adding
     interesting touches while staying true to the groove (e.g., ghost notes on the snare drum or hi-hat). However,
     drummers should not let their creativity derail the established groove. Alterations to grooves should be within
     the abilities of the performer and should serve the music rather than the performer’s ego.

     2-69. Performers can easily evaluate their feel by watching the audience:
          • Is the groove infectious?
          • Are they dancing?
          • Are they nodding their heads?

MUSICALITY

     2-70. Musicality on drum set results from a combination of the previous skills. Drummers should make
     informed musical choices that align with the style, form, and the context of the music. They should
     understand the nuances of the genre and employ stylistic elements that enhance rather than detract from the
     music. Being attuned to the groove, understanding stylistic nuances, and navigating musical dynamics

                         Ceremonial Ensembles

    Army Bands provide support to military ceremonies as a whole unit configured into a marching
    band or a smaller group to form a ceremonial band. This chapter provides an overview of
    ceremonial music and performance considerations and techniques for the percussion
    instruments commonly used in ceremonial ensembles.

SECTION I – CEREMONIAL MUSIC

     3-1. Army Bands have been an integral part of military ceremonies since the Army’s inception. Traditionally,
     ceremonial music helped to build enthusiasm, maintain motivation, and increase devotion to duty. Today,
     bands preserve tradition by performing honors to the host and to the Nation while also performing marching
     music to augment the ceremony. Ceremonial music includes official music, marches, patriotic selections, and
     organizational songs. See ATP 1-19 and TC 1-19.10 for more details.

SECTION PERSONNEL

     3-2. Percussionists primarily perform on snare drum, bass drum, and cymbals while assigned to ceremonial
     ensembles. Section leaders are strongly encouraged to rotate percussionists through the section to ensure that
     percussionists are maintaining proficiency on bass drum and cymbals. When possible, it is recommended
     that the percussion section for ceremonial ensembles consist of ASI percussionists.

     3-3. Depending on the number of concurrent missions and availability of personnel, non-ASI percussionists
     (ASIs 9H, 9K, 9N, 9T, 9U, 9V, and 9X) that do not double on another brass or wind instrument, may be
     assigned as auxiliary percussionists to perform bass drum or cymbals. These ASIs receive training and
     perform on auxiliary percussion during AIT. ASI percussionists serve as subject matter experts for the
     ceremonial percussion section and are responsible for ensuring that non-ASI percussionists are properly
     trained.

     3-4. Careful consideration should be given prior to using non-ASI percussionists in ceremonial ensembles
     such as:

          • Have they received training on proper playing techniques for bass drum and cymbals?
          • Are they familiar with commonly used ceremonial music, including cadences?
          • If playing cymbals, have they been trained on commonly performed and often unwritten “solo”

                crashes?
          • How much experience do they have marching and playing?
          • Can they set and maintain consistent tempo while marching (e.g., on bass drum)?
          • Is there time in the schedule to sufficiently train them?

PERFORMANCE PRACTICE

     3-5. Ceremonial music can be performed stationary (e.g., ceremonial band, while seated or standing) or while
     on the move (e.g., marching band). A common practice for the performance of ceremonial music (stationary
     or on the move) is the use of flip folders. Musicians use flip folders to position their music in front of, or near
     their faces, allowing for a line of site between their instrument, music, and the drum major or conductor. The
     use of flip folders is advantageous while performing music on the move and is often used for stationary
     ceremonies where traditional music stands cannot or should not be used.

     3-6. The percussion section is the only section that is unable to consistently use printed music or flip folders
     during ceremonial music performances. Over time, this has created differing performance practices for
     percussionists when performing ceremonial music while stationary or while on the move.

WHILE STATIONARY

     3-7. The percussion section for stationary performances consists of a bass drum, cymbals, and a snare drum.
     When performing for stationary ceremonies the percussion section brings a single music stand and perform
     printed parts as written. Music may be placed in a flip folder or a three-ring binder with sheet protectors.

Bass Drum

     3-8. When performing from printed parts, the bass drum plays an important role in the projection and control
     of ensemble dynamics for the band. The bass drum dynamics and accents should be balanced with and
     support the tuba or bass part. Dynamics should not be as out of context as they might be when moving troops.

     3-9. When performing marches, most bass drummers will not play on every beat as notated. If they do play
     on every beat, the dynamics should support the sound of the tuba/low brass section and not as a timekeeper.
     The part can be interpreted as a drum set player would “feather” the bass drum for older styles of swing: the
     part is present, but not necessarily audible.

     3-10. To clearly define the spacing of the bass drum part, the player must first listen to the harmonic rhythm
     (i.e., frequency of chord changes) of the march and attempt to outline that rhythm with the bass drum. This
     gives greater freedom to play accents and prep cymbal crashes. Usually, the harmonic rhythm increases closer
     to cadence points or ends of phrases, so the bass drum part may become more involved in these sections.

Cymbals

     3-11. When playing marches, the cymbal player often reads and doubles the bass drum on all parts where the
     dynamic level is mezzo-forte (mf) or above unless specifically notated otherwise (e.g., separately notated or
     labeled as “solo”). In older printings of marches, the note duration often implied dynamic shading or accents
     which corresponded to accented or longer notes in the melodic line (see figure 3-1). In figure 3-1, dotted
     quarter notes are interpreted and played at a lower dynamic level, while the dotted half note would be
     accented.

                                              Figure 3-1. Cymbal notation

    3-12. Playing cymbals within ensemble dynamics is crucial when performing marches. Players should avoid
    overplaying dynamics and creating static white noise. It is common practice to omit cymbals during softer
    sections of marches that primarily feature the woodwinds, as this matches and supports the change in
    ensemble timbre and balance. Omitting cymbals during these sections also gives the performer a break, as
    playing cymbals throughout an entire march can be very tiring.

    3-13. Over time, conductors (and sometimes their percussion sections) have developed and added special
    accented or “solo” cymbal notes throughout many commonly performed marches. These notes are often
    unwritten and are added to enhance melodic peaks, accents within melodies, or dramatic rests in the music.
    These unwritten parts often create ambiguity and frustration for conductors, percussionists, and non-ASI
    percussionists, as they may vary from conductor to conductor, or performer to performer.

    3-14. Section leaders should consult with conductors prior to rehearsing or performing marches to determine
    any preferred additions or alterations to cymbal parts. Pencil-in any alterations to the percussion parts and
    disseminate the changes to members of the section. This is crucial for non-ASI percussionists as they are
    often unfamiliar with these alterations. Performers may be exposed to several versions of these cymbal
    alterations throughout their careers. When in doubt, consult with the conductor.

Bass Drum with Attached Cymbal

     3-15. Depending on the availability of personnel or to reduce the number of personnel in the section, a
     common practice is to use one player to perform bass drum with attached cymbal. There are several
     commercial off-the-shelf cymbal attachments, and the type used will depend on the bass drum (e.g., rope
     tensioned or modern). When selecting cymbals, it is recommended to use a mis-matched pair of cymbals
     (e.g., different diameters). Mount the larger cymbal to the bass drum (upside down), while holding and
     playing with the smaller cymbal. This allows the performer to see where the cymbals make contact, while
     also allowing adjustment of the angle of contact to prevent a vacuum between the cymbals.

     3-16. Careful consideration should be used when using bass drum with attached cymbal, as players may not
     have experience or the coordination to perform the parts simultaneously. Non-ASI percussionists should not
     be assigned to perform bass drum with attached cymbal without proper training and guided practice with an
     ASI percussionist.

Snare Drum

     3-17. When playing marches while stationary, the snare drummer should read and perform printed parts as
     written. The parts should be balanced with and support the horn section when performing offbeats. Roll
     interpretation will depend on the drum used and the tempo and meter of the march. When using smaller,
     higher tensioned drums (e.g., 6 ½ x 14 or smaller), rolls may be performed as closed or “buzzed” rolls. If
     using a deeper drum with lower tension (e.g., 10x14, 14x14, or rope drum (16x16)) rolls may be performed
     as closed or open double-stroke rolls. When using deeper and larger drums, dynamics and accents should be
     performed with consideration of ensemble dynamics, to avoid overpowering the ensemble.

     3-18. Regardless of the type of roll performed (e.g., open or closed), the tempo of the march will determine
     roll speed. Offbeat rolls are performed as either five-stroke or seven-stroke rolls. When performing marches
     in compound-duple (e.g., 6/8), offbeat rolls must be slightly compressed to align with the third partial of the
     beat to maintain the natural lilt of the time signature.

             Note. Regarding tempo, it is common practice to perform offbeat rolls as seven-stroke rolls for
             tempos up to 110 beats per minute. Five-stroke rolls are used for tempos at 110 beats per minute
             and higher.

     3-19. Whether performing in simple duple or compound-duple, a common issue for performers (especially
     in the final strain) is rushing or dragging offbeat rolls. When performing faster tempos, seven-stroke rolls
     may be too long (i.e., duration) or too fast (i.e., hand speed) to fit within an offbeat causing the performer to
     drag. The five-stroke roll on the other hand, may be too short in both duration and hand speed causing the
     performer to rush.

     3-20. A solution is to perform an alternative six-stroke roll, sometimes referred to as a “flam five.” These
     rolls are non-alternating and use an inverted open roll sticking (see figure 3-2). While tricky to perform, these
     rolls bridge the gap between the five- and seven-stroke roll and allow the performer to maintain consistent
     time. For further discussion on the use of alternative six-stroke rolls, refer to Keith Brion’s Sousa’s Marches:
     As He Performed Them.

     3-27. While there are ways to attach flip folders to marching snare drums, their practical use is limited. Flip

     making it difficult to simultaneously read the music while maintaining situational awareness of commands
     and signals from the drum major. A level of complexity is added when using rope tensioned snare drums
     since the drum naturally moves in conjunction with the leg making the use of flip folders impractical.

     3-28. A common practice is for percussionists to perform marches from memory using simplified parts.
     These simplified parts utilize easy to remember “check patterns,” which are one to two bar phrases. The
     practice of memorizing common ceremonial music, including music consistently played on the move, is
     highly encouraged (see TC 1-19.10). The percussion section leader is further encouraged to create a
     standardized set of simplified parts for use on the move. See section four of this chapter for discussion on
     creating simplified snare drum parts for marching band.

MARCH FORM

     3-29. It is helpful when rehearsing, memorizing, or performing marches, to identify and understand march
     form. While there is not a standard form (i.e., all the same), commonly performed marches share many of the
     same building blocks including the introduction, first strain, second strain, trio, break strain, and final strain.
     Sections and their variations are identified and separated by double bar lines or repeats.

INTRODUCTION

     3-30. Introductions vary in length but are usually four to eight measures long. Percussion parts during
     introductions may accent the melodic line with short notes. They may also match the melodic line’s longer
     notes (e.g., long rolls in the snare drum part, or longer cymbal notes). “Solo” cymbal notes may be and are
     often added in the last measure to setup the first strain (e.g., “Washington Post March” – J.P. Sousa).

FIRST STRAIN

     3-31. The first strain is sixteen measures repeated or thirty-two measures in length. Percussion parts should
     support rather than lead the ensemble. The bass drum (while stationary) should support the attack of the
     tubas. The cymbals accentuate the melodic line (in the brass) by matching or supporting long notes. The snare
     drum compliments the horn section’s offbeats but often supports the melodic line through rolls. Percussion
     parts often increase in rhythmic density to match the melody approaching cadence points, ends of phrases,
     and leading into the second strain.

SECOND STRAIN

     3-32. The second strain is sixteen measures repeated or thirty-two measures in length. Like the first strain,
     percussion parts should support rather than lead the ensemble. The second strain is often louder in dynamic
     and more powerful in character but may also have dynamic contrast when repeated (e.g., loud then soft, or
     soft then loud).

TRIO

     3-33. The trio is thirty-two measures but may be sixteen measures repeated. The trio may be preceded by an
     introduction of four or eight measures (e.g., “Bravura” – C.E. Duble). The ensemble’s orchestration often
     drastically changes to feature the woodwinds, while often omitting the trumpets and trombones. Percussion
     parts are usually sparse during trios and are played at a softer dynamic to support the change in ensemble
     balance. Cymbal parts that keep steady pulse (i.e., doubling the bass drum) may be omitted during trios to
     avoid overpowering the woodwinds. The practice of omitting cymbals during trios is encouraged while on
     the move, especially during pass in review ceremonies to allow the player to rest.

BREAK STRAIN

     3-34. The break strain, sometimes referred to as the “dogfight,” is typically sixteen measures in length. This
     section, as the name implies, provides a break between the trio and the final strain. They are often very loud
     and contain short rhythmic ideas (e.g., fanfare or rhythmic “stabs”) played by most of the ensemble.
     Percussion parts are sparse and either double the ensemble’s rhythmic patterns or support sustained notes
     with snare drum rolls and longer cymbal crashes. Added “solo” cymbal notes appear in break strains and
     often occur during moments of rest, immediately preceding or following an ensemble rhythmic moment.

FINAL STRAIN (TRIO REPEATED)

     3-35. The final strain of a march is a repeat of the trio with heavier orchestration (e.g., woodwinds and brass
     doubling the melody). They are sixteen measures repeated or thirty-two measures in length. If the trio ends
     a march, it usually has a short percussive note called a “stinger” as the last note of the march. “Stingers” are
     typically only performed while stationary, but performers should confirm with conductors prior to
     performance.

     3-36. Percussion parts should support the ensemble. Snare drum parts in final strains are often continuously
     repeated short roll figures. Players should pick a roll speed (e.g., five-, alternative six-, or seven-stroke) that
     allows them to maintain consistent time. While on the move, especially during parades and longer
     ceremonies, the band may rush or drag the final strain. The snare drummer(s) and bass drummer can and
     should help prevent tempo fluctuations during the final strain.

     3-37. For many marches conductors will cue extra cymbal or bass drum notes in final strains to enhance
     melodic peaks or accents within melodies. Cymbal players are encouraged to carefully watch the conductor
     for any added cues during final strains.

ALTERNATE ENDINGS

     3-38. There are some common alternate endings to marches such as:
          • The final strain repeats back to the break strain and finishes with the final strain.
          • With a marking at the end of the trio to go back to another section of the march using:
                 A da capo (D.C. al fine) – these marches return to the beginning of the march, and end
                     immediately before the trio, marked by “Fine.” When returning to the beginning, it is common
                     practice to perform second endings for the first and second strains. Performers should confirm
                     with conductors prior to performance.
                 A dal segno (D.S. al fine) – these marches will return to the sign and play until the “Fine”
                     marking.

             Note. Although unwritten, a common practice is for percussion sections to perform a Seven-Count
             Cutoff to end a march. When performing in parades, the seven-count cutoff is followed by a stick-
             tap or cadence depending on the drum major’s command.

SECTION III – HERALDIC ROPE TENSIONED DRUMS

     3-82. Heraldic rope tensioned drums (often simply referred to as rope drums) have been used by
     percussionists since the Army’s inception. Their use in Army Bands today varies by unit and largely depends
     on several factors including:

          • Availability of instruments.
          • Commander or section preference.
          • Snare drummers’ proficiency using traditional grip.
          • Section personnel having knowledge of maintenance requirements.
          • Difficulty associated with marching (e.g., snare drum).

     3-83. Many Soldiers do not have experience using, maintaining, or marching with rope drums. This section
     provides a brief overview of rope drums, including their construction, maintenance, and considerations for
     marching.

CONSTRUCTION

     3-84. Rope drums come in several different sizes for both snare drum and bass drum. Snare drums range
     from 16 to 20 inches in depth and 16 to 17 inches in diameter. Bass drums range from 14 to 20 inches in
     depth and 24 to 30 inches in diameter.

     3-85. Unlike their modern counterparts, rope drums are composed almost entirely of wood, with the notable
     exception of the snare strainer system, and any optional accessories such as carry hook(s), rim guards, and
     feet (see figure 3-16). These drums are noticeably lighter than modern drums and produce less strain on
     player’s bodies over extended usage. The main components of a drum include:

          • Hoops.
          • Heads.
          • Shell.
          • Rope.
          • Leather ears.
          • Carry hook(s).
          • Feet.
          • Rim guards (if applicable).
          • Snare strainer.
          • Pigtail knot.
          • Drag rope.

HEADS

     3-86. Heads for snare drums can be made of plastic, synthetic/hybrid, or calfskin membranes. The most
     common heads used by Army Bands are synthetic/hybrid heads (e.g., a woven fiber blend, such as the Rotex
     head from Loyal Drums) on the batter side, and plastic heads on the snare side.

     3-87. Bass drums use modern plastic marching bass drumheads, as used on modern corps-style drums. These
     plastic heads provide the best sound and durability for ceremonial music use. Recommended commercial off-
     the-shelf options include the Evans MX2 or Remo Powermax head, which include pre-installed internal
     muffling.

             Note. While calfskin drumheads are the traditional and most stylistically appropriate choice for
             rope drums, synthetic and plastic heads offer better durability, weather resistance, and cost
             effectiveness for Army Band usage.

ROPE

     3-88. As the name implies, rope drums are tensioned by rope. One single length of rope is pulled through the
     drum, providing tension to the heads. The rope passes through holes in the hoops, then through leather ears,
     terminating in a pigtail knot which secures the rope and maintains tension around the drum. Common types
     of rope used include:

          • Hemp.
          • Natural linen.
          • Synthetic polyester fiber (e.g., Filament Dacron).

EARS

     3-89. The heads can be “tuned” (also referred to as braced) by adding further tension with leather ears. Much
     like using a drum key to tighten the tension rods on a modern drum, pushing (or pulling) the ears down on a
     rope drum will add tension to the rope causing the drum to raise in pitch. It is important to pull ears in pairs
     (e.g., one ear on each side of the drum) moving around the shell, when tensioning the drum (see figure 3-17).

     3-90. When drums are not in use (e.g., while stored) the ears should remain loose (i.e., without tension on

SNARES

     3-91. The most common type of snares on a rope tension drum are natural gut snares, and like calfskin heads,
     they may be affected by weather changes. The snare strainer on a rope drum does not typically include a
     quick release lever. The snares are tensioned by a screw and are secured in place by a locking nut/washer.
     When adjusting snare tension, ensure that the locking nut/washer is secure to avoid losing the screw (e.g.,
     falls out while marching) or losing tension on the snares.

     3-92. If the use of a rope drum with the snares off is required (e.g., during a funeral), it may be difficult to
     loosen the snares enough without completely loosening the snare strainer and removing the screw and locking
     nut/washer. If this is the case, it may be best to remove the snare unit completely. If removing the snares is
     impractical, another option is to loosen the snares enough to place a piece of cloth between the snares and
     the bottom head.

BASS DRUM

     3-93. Bass drums have the same component parts as snare drums apart from the snare strainer. The carry
     hooks are positioned on the shell of the drum rather than on the hoop. Depending on the manufacturer and
     type of harness used, carry hooks may be set up as two points (e.g., equidistant to attach to shoulder straps),
     or as three points (e.g., equidistant shoulder hooks and one hook that attaches to the belt). This allows the
     drum to be held in front of the torso with the playing surfaces facing outwards.

DISASSEMBLY

     3-95. Disassembling a rope drum begins with the drag rope and pigtail. The drag rope and pigtail are tied in
     such a way that they can be easily untied and pulled out for re-tensioning or rope removal. It is of the utmost
     importance when doing so that even tension is maintained around the drum to prevent warping of the shell
     or hoops.

     3-96. The steps for disassembling a rope drum are:
          • Detach and untie the drag rope.
          • Slowly release the tension of the pigtail by unwinding the knot.
          • Release the rope tension in small increments. Starting from the end point of the rope (e.g., the
                pigtail), pull a little bit of tension out of each strand of rope, one at a time, zigzagging around the
                drum until the heads visibly begin to wrinkle.

             Note. When the heads begin to visibly wrinkle, it is then safe to completely pull out or remove the
             rope from the drum. If the rope will not be re-used, it may be quicker to cut the rope once the
             heads visibly begin to wrinkle, to aid in faster removal.

          • When disassembling a snare drum, remove or loosen the snare strainer to allow for removal of the
                bottom head.

          • When disassembling a snare drum, using painter’s or electrical tape, mark the location of the pigtail
                and the carry hook on both the rim and the shell to ensure proper placement during reassembly.

          • If disassembling a bass drum, using painter’s or electrical tape, mark the location of the pigtail on
                both the rim and the shell to ensure proper placement during reassembly.

ASSEMBLY

     3-97. Once the drum is disassembled check for:
          • Warping of the shell or hoops.
          • Check for loose internal hardware (e.g., mounting hooks for bass drum).
          • Condition of the heads (e.g., excessive dents, pulls, tears, or ripping).

     3-98. To assemble the drum, ensure that there is a clean work surface, free of dust or debris, and that there
     are no missing components.

             Note. The following paragraphs apply to the assembly of a snare drum. The drum will be
             assembled upside down with the carry hook on the work surface, facing the operator. These steps
             may also be used for assembling bass drums. When assembling a bass drum, ensure that the taped
             locations marked during disassembly (see paragraph 3-96) are on the top of the drum (e.g., the
             side opposite the work surface) and facing the operator.

Component Assembly

     3-99. The steps for assembling the components of a rope drum are:
          • Place the batter hoop down, upside down (e.g., flat side facing up).
          • Seat the batter head, upside down, so that the flat side of the batter hoop makes full contact with the
                counter hoop of the batter head.
          • Place the drum shell onto the batter head, upside down, so that the bearing edge of the shell is seated
                just inside the counter hoop of the batter head.
          • If the drum has artwork, ensure that it will be aligned front and center of the drum.

             Note. Artwork will typically be centered opposite the carry hook(s) for snare drums and bass
             drums.

          • Place the snare side head, right side up, on top of the shell so that the bearing edge is seated just
                inside the counter hoop.

          • Feed the rope through the hole just to the left of the snare bed, starting from the inside (i.e., the head
                side of the hoop) and pulling out and then down (i.e., toward the operator). Pull the entire length of
                the rope until the eye splice knot rests against the inside of the hoop. From here, the rope will be fed
                and pulled around the drum to the left.
          • For bass drum, feed the rope through the hole marked with tape during disassembly. Starting from
                the inside (i.e., the head side of the hoop) and pulling out and then down (i.e., toward the operator).
                From here, follow the remainder of the step above.

             Note. Rope can be ordered in pre-measured lengths based on drum size from manufacturers (e.g.,
             Cooperman, Loyal Drums). When ordering rope, it is recommended to request that the eye splice
             is pre-tied.

          • Feed the rope down and to the left, through an ear, ensuring that it is properly oriented (i.e., upside
                down), with the wide (larger) opening facing the snare head (top head for bass drum) and the smaller
                opening facing the batter head (bottom head for bass drum).

          • Wrap the rope around the batter hoop, feeding it back through the hole, from the inside then out,
                pulling the rope back up and to the left.

             Note. Over time, friction from the rope creates divots in the rim which are especially obvious on
             painted rims. When pulling the rope around the rim ensure that the rope sits inside these divots.

          • Feed the rope back through the same ear (the smaller opening), and wrap around the snare hoop,
                feeding through the next hole (to the left of the starting hole), from the inside then outwards, pulling
                the rope down and to the left.

             Note. At the conclusion of this step the rope should form the first “V.” The ear should rest against
             the batter hoop. Ensure the rope is pulled tight enough to remove excess slack, but not so tight as
             to disrupt the positioning of other components (e.g., unseating the head from the bearing edge).

          • Repeat the previous three steps moving around the drum (to the left), returning to the eye splice
                loop.

             Note. As the rope is pulled around the drum, ensure that it is threaded through the ears and that
             ears are facing the same direction. Each “V” should have an ear.

          • When returning to the starting hole, instead of feeding the rope through the hole in the hoop, feed it
                through the loop of the eye splice knot. Ensure the loop is pulled to the outside of the hoop. The
                tension of the rope being pulled should assist in this.

     •    Secure the rope with the thumb by pressing it against itself at the intersection with the loop. The
          pressure maintained at this intersection should hold the tension of the rope.
     •    With the opposite hand, twist the rope with the fingers to tighten the strands/weave of the rope. Once
     •    the rope strands are tight, twist the rope in a circular (clockwise) fashion, as close as possible to the
          intersection point. As the rope is twisted it should fold over on itself, making a C-shape.
          While maintaining the tension of the strands/weave of the rope, continue to wrap the rope clockwise,
          around and behind itself, pulling straight down to secure.
          Repeat the previous step as many times as necessary until the rope holds the tension on itself and
          does not unwrap when released. Often this will need to be done four to five times, but the greater
          the tension is on the drum, the smaller the pigtail will be.

Note. If the pigtail becomes too long (e.g., two to three inches away from the drum), then there is
still too much slack remaining in the rope. If this happens, leave the pigtail secured and re-pull the
rope. Once extraneous slack is removed, retie the pigtail knot. Repeat this process until the pigtail
becomes smaller (e.g., four to five wraps at the most).

Drag Rope

     3-103. After completion of the pigtail, excess rope can be used to create what is called a drag rope (see figure
     3-19). The drag rope serves multiple functions including:

          • It is aesthetically pleasing.
          • Allows excess rope to be preserved.
          • Provides a comfortable way to carry the drum when not playing.

                                                    Figure 3-19. Drag rope

     3-104. The steps to create a drag rope are:
          • Start by laying the excess rope across the bottom head, stretching from the pigtail straight across to
                the opposite side of the drum.
          • At the point where the rope crosses the hoop (to the left of the pigtail in figure 3-19), create a small
                loop.
          • Pinch the rope just beyond this loop into a U-shape and pull it through the loop.
          • Once through, always keep two fingers inside the newly created loop. Using those fingers, reach
                and pull the next section of rope through and tight.

             Note. Repeat the previous step as many times as needed to cover the diameter of the drum.

          • To close the drag rope:
                 On the last loop simply pull the rope straight through instead of using a pinched U-shape.
                 Tuck the rope underneath itself on the opposite side of the drum (i.e., opposite the pigtail).
                 Depending on how much rope is left, either tie it off or feed it back through the drag rope.

     3-105. Over time, the heads and rope will stretch, causing the drum to lower in pitch. This is undesirable and
     requires regular maintenance and tuning. While more tedious than modern rod-tensioned drums, tuning of
     rope tensioned drums can be both simple and quick. For general maintenance and tuning much of the
     assembly process can be applied.

     3-106. The steps for general maintenance and tuning are:
          • Ensure all ears are completely loosened (i.e., pulled up towards the batter head).
          • Detach and untie the drag rope.
          • Turn the drum on its side so that the ropes (i.e., the “Vs”) are facing the operator.
          • Rotate/spin the drum placing the beginning section of rope (i.e., the start of the assembly process –
                eye splice or pigtail knot) so that it is facing the operator.
          • Take hold of the rope while letting the drum hang (using gravity as an advantage) and pull the rope.
          • Ensure not to release any slack and transfer the weight of the drum to the next section of rope (i.e.,
                the following side of the “V”).

             Note. Repeat the previous two steps moving around the drum returning to the pigtail.

          • From here it may be best to rotate the drum so that the drumhead (e.g., the batter head on the snare
                drum) is facing the operator to allow the operator to brace the drum between their legs.

          • Without releasing any tension (i.e., continue holding the rope), pull and release (unwind) the pigtail
                knot.

          • Pull any excess new slack created through the eye splice loop.
          • Place the thumb at the intersection point with the loop.
          • Tie a new pigtail knot and check the pitch of the drum.
          • If the desired tension has been reached and the new pigtail remains small, remake the drag rope.
          • Tuning is complete.

             Note. The further the operator pulls around the drum the more rope will be pulled through. To
             ensure even tension around the drum, start small and increase the intensity of each pull working
             around the drum.

MARCHING

     3-107. Although preferred for their traditional sound and connection to history and tradition, marching with
     rope drums provides a unique set of challenges. Except for those with experience in the fife and drum corps,
     or historically black colleges and universities community, most Army percussionists have little experience
     marching with sling worn drums. Rope snare drums are worn with a cross-body sling (or strap), with the
     drum hanging via a carry hook. When worn, the drum rests on the performer’s left leg, and while on the move
     the drum naturally moves with the leg.

     3-108. The difficulty of marching with a rope drum can be contributed to a combination of factors including:
          • The depth of the drum.
          • The length of the sling/strap (i.e., is it adjustable?).
          • Where the drum rests on the leg (e.g., below, on, or above the knee).
          • The height of the performer.
          • Step size.
          • Navigating Left (Right) Square Turns unique to Army Bands ceremonial marching performances.

     3-109. These factors present challenges and are often highlighted by differences in individual body size and
     mechanics. This often leaves many performers intimidated and frustrated when using and marching with rope
     drums. Performers are encouraged to experiment and adjust the drum to their unique body size and
     mechanics. Making small incremental adjustments to the drum’s setup can greatly affect freedom of
     movement and ease of playing while on the move.

DRUM ADJUSTMENT

     3-110. The most important factor for marching with rope snare drums is the adjustment of how and where
     the drum sits on the performer’s body. With the primary limitation of drum depth, the easiest way to adjust
     the drum’s position is by adjusting the sling. Generally, the sling should be adjusted so that the carry hook
     sits just below or above the top of the hip bone. This position allows the drum to sit in a comfortable playing
     position for most players.

MARCHING TECHNIQUE ADJUSTMENT

     3-113. After adjustments have been made to both the sling and knee position, performers must often modify
     their marching technique. To prevent excess motion or bouncing of the drum while marching, performers
     often appear to have a slight limp. The limp is a result of keeping the left leg predominately straight while
     marching. Movement is initiated from the knee and hip rather than the foot. When marching in place (e.g.,
     Mark Time March) performers may lower their heel height to prevent excess up and down motion of the
     drum. Strive for a uniform heel height across the entire section when marching in place.

     3-114. The band’s step size can also have an impact on marching with rope drums, especially when
     performing Left (Right) Square Turns. Although step size should remain constant, the reality in most
     situations is that step size fluctuates. As step size increases the snare drummer’s limp becomes more
     pronounced and performers often have difficulty maintaining spacing between ranks. When performing
     square turns, performers in the back of the formation must take larger steps to reach pivot points. When
     combined with larger step size, the sharp pivot required for turns often causes the drum to swing away from
     the performer. Although not preferred, an option to prevent excessive drum swing during square turns is to
     stop playing and grab the drum with the left hand during the pivot. Performers should resume playing upon
     completion of the turn. In a section of two to three snare drummers the momentary gap in playing should not
     be noticeable.

     3-137. This section provides percussionists with general rules for marching bass drum performance, the
     manual of instruments and maintenance. Musical performance practice is addressed in sections one and two

GENERAL RULES FOR PERFORMANCE

     3-138. The marching bass drum should provide a sharp, distinct, and deep tone. It emphasizes strong beats
     of the music, making it easy for the band and other troop formations to march in step, primarily by playing
     “Heavy Left.” The bass drummer must be able to set and maintain consistent tempo while on the move.

     3-139. The following are general rules for performance on marching bass drum, including instrument setup,
     grip, stroke technique, and striking area. The information provided is only a guide and represents one of many
     ways to succeed while performing on marching bass drum.

INSTRUMENT SETUP

     3-140. Marching bass drums used in Army Bands are available in sizes from 14 to 20 inches in depth and 24
     to 30 inches in diameter. Heads should be tuned to the same pitch, to allow for a consistent sound when
     playing “Heavy Left.” If used, rope drums will naturally be tuned to the same pitch as the heads are tensioned
     equally. Tuning is dependent on drum size but generally sounds best when tuned to notes between C2 and
     C3.

     3-141. Drums may be worn using a modern drum corps-style carrier/harness, or by using adjustable cloth or
     leather straps. Regardless of how worn, the drum must be adjusted to the player. This is often overlooked
     especially for non-ASI percussionists.

     3-142. The drum should be positioned so that as the performer moves their arms/hands into playing position,
     the forearm is parallel (e.g., 90° angle) or slightly below parallel (e.g., 75–85° angle) to the marching surface.
     The hand will maintain a 45° angle from the forearm. For most players this is the most relaxed and
     comfortable position for the arms and hands and places the mallet head in the center of the drum. This position
     is referred to as Prepare to Play in the manual of instruments. Once in this position, if the mallet head does
     not sit in the center of the head, then the drum must be adjusted (see figure 3-25).

     3-144. With the lack of technical demand and frequent use of non-ASI percussionists, an easy grip to train
     and use for marching band is a back of hand grip. Most modern bass drum implements have a flared taper
     towards the handle (i.e., the shaft is thicker towards the bottom). Traditional wooden mallets used on rope
     drums often include a notched bulb design that encourages a back of hand grip.

     3-145. To form the grip, place the mallet diagonally across the hand. Following the design of the mallet, wrap

     only enough pressure to secure the mallet and resist the urge to squeeze the mallet into the hand. The middle
     and pointer finger wrap around the mallet naturally without tension or squeezing. The thumb wraps around
     and touches the pointer finger. The top of the grip should remain open to allow the mallet to freely move
     within the hand (see figure 3-26).

     3-146. Resist the urge to squeeze the hand as if holding a baseball bat, as this creates unnecessary tension in

STROKE TECHNIQUE

     3-148. For general-purpose playing an easy stroke to use and train is a tear drop stroke. It is called a tear drop
     stroke because the path of the mallet head forms a tear drop as it travels through the air. When used with a
     loose back of hand grip this motion provides the power and volume necessary for performing “Heavy Left”
     without straining to force the sound.

     3-149. From Prepare to Play the motion can be broken into four parts:

                performing a chest fly.
          • Once in the open position, rotate and extend the forearm away from the body.
          • Pull the elbow towards the body.
          • As the elbow moves towards the body, the forearm rotates towards the drumhead, allowing the
                mallet to strike the drum.

     3-150. When performed in time it should be a continuous motion. The point of the tear drop is at the

SECTION VI – CYMBALS

     3-160. This section provides percussionists with general rules for marching cymbal performance, the manual
     of instruments and maintenance. Musical performance practice is addressed in sections one and two of this
     chapter.

GENERAL RULES FOR PERFORMANCE

     3-161. Hand cymbals used in marching band range in size from 16 to 20 inches in diameter and vary in
     thickness. When selecting cymbals for outside use there must be a balance between size and weight. Larger
     and heavier cymbals will project better (i.e., easier to play loud), but their weight requires greater physical
     stamina over long periods. Smaller and lighter cymbals, while easier to play over long periods, may distort
     or wash out (i.e., sound thin) when trying to project over the band.

     3-162. If possible, the section should select and assign a pair of cymbals strictly for outside use. Choose
     commonly used commercial off-the-shelf cymbals designed for outdoor use such as:

          • 18-inch A Zildjian Stadium – Medium.
          • 18-inch Sabian Marching Band AAX.

     3-163. The following are general rules for performance on marching cymbals, including instrument setup,
     grip, and stroke technique. The information provided is only a guide and represents one of many ways of
     performing on marching cymbals.

INSTRUMENT SETUP

     3-164. Cymbals are held by straps made of leather or nylon. Although leather straps require a slight break-in
     period most players prefer them. This break-in period softens the material making it flexible, allowing the
     straps to stretch and mold to the player’s hand(s). Over time the cymbal knot also becomes more secure and
     easier to tie. Leather straps are also double sided, with a rough unfinished side that is easier to grip when the
     hands are sweaty or a more secure grip is desired, and a smoother side for players with drier hands. Nylon
     straps do not require a break-in period but are prone to fray or break with heavy use.

     3-165. Straps are secured underneath the bell of the cymbal by the cymbal knot, which is a variation of the
     crown or friendship knot. Begin by pulling the four ends of the straps through the cymbal hole. The size of
     the hand loop will determine the length of material available to tie the knot. The steps for how to properly tie

     3-170. Control of the stroke is necessary to produce proper tone quality on hand cymbals. Sloppy technique
     leads to failure in producing clean, brilliant cymbal crashes. Improper technique can lead to creating an air

     edge-to-edge or flat with all the edges meeting simultaneously. Depending on the level of vacuum, the
     cymbals could either stick/suction together or invert themselves.

MAINTENANCE

     3-182. There should be very little maintenance required for marching cymbals. Damage, abuse, neglect, or
     disuse to the cymbal plates and straps will require maintenance. Overtime and heavy use cymbal straps may
     stretch requiring the knot to be adjusted and re-tied. Straps may also wear down from friction, appearing as
     cuts through leather or fraying in nylon near the cymbal hole.

     3-183. Operator-level maintenance is encouraged before each use, including:
          • Check for cracks or damage to the cymbal plate(s).
          • Check straps for wear or damage.
          • Ensure hand loop size is appropriate for user.
          • Check the cymbal knot for correctness, strength, and tightness. Secure double knot if able.
          • Ensure pads are present and secured to the straps (if applicable).

     3-184. Cymbals should be cleaned as needed. The steps for operator-level cleaning are:
          • Clean using commercial off-the-shelf cymbal cleaner following manufacturer’s directions.
          • Do not use heavy abrasive cleaners.
          • Dry immediately with a clean, soft cloth.

                                Concert Band

    Army Bands perform symphonic music while configured in a concert band – an instrumental
    ensemble capable of performing symphonic music by the addition of instruments not adapted
    to marching (see ATP 1-19). This chapter provides general considerations and playing
    techniques for the most common instruments used in concert band.

SECTION I – GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS

     4-1. Symphonic music can be demanding on percussion sections due to the number of players and
     instruments required for each musical selection. When performing modern concert band/wind ensemble
     literature, most Army Band percussion sections are unable to perform all the printed parts, which require the
     use of non-ASI percussionists. Aside from ceremonial ensembles, concert band requires the greatest
     supplemental use of non-ASI percussionists. During AIT, Soldiers in the following ASIs receive training and
     perform on auxiliary percussion in concert band: 9N, 9T, and 9U.

     4-2. Many non-ASI percussionists have little to no experience playing percussion instruments or playing in
     concert band. Percussion notation and the basic playing techniques of most instruments can be confusing.
     Usually, but not always, there is at least one experienced ASI percussionist that can act as a subject matter
     expert to assist in the training of these non-ASI percussionists. Careful consideration should be given prior
     to using non-ASI percussionists such as:

          • Have they received training on percussion instruments other than bass drum and cymbals?
          • Is there time in the schedule to sufficiently train them on proper playing techniques?

PERCUSSION NOTATION

     4-3. Percussion notation in concert band music is a common concern for both ASI percussionists and non-
     ASI percussionists, as notation can differ by composer, editor, or publisher. Parts may be notated as:

          • A single line with:
                 Notation below, on, or above the line, to differentiate between instruments. The instruments are
                     usually clearly labeled at the beginning of the part and sometimes relabeled after multiple bars
                     of rest.
                 Notation with different note head symbols (e.g., dot, diamond, or “x”) to differentiate between
                     non-pitched instruments.

          • A traditional staff with:
                 Lower sounding instruments notated on the bottom (i.e., F space in treble clef or A space of
                     bass clef).
                 Mid-range instruments (e.g., snare drum, tambourine in the middle (i.e., C space in treble clef
                     or E space of bass clef).
                 Higher sounding instruments (e.g., triangle, cymbal) on the higher spaces or just above the staff
                     (i.e., top space E or G above the staff in treble clef, and top space G or B above the staff in bass
                     clef).

          • Combined with mallet percussion on a traditional staff:
                 Mallet instrument(s) will be notated in the staff.
                 Percussion instruments will either be clearly labeled within the staff, or notated with a different
                     note head (e.g., diamond or “x”).

          • A percussion score with separate staves containing any of the above combinations.

     4-4. Aside from the variety in the general presentation of percussion parts, composers have also struggled
     with how to accurately convey note lengths for percussion instruments between the notation and the resulting
     sound (i.e., how long or short a note sounds relative to the instrument). This notational issue causes ambiguity
     for performers when interpreting their parts.

     4-5. Percussion instruments can broadly be divided into two categories:
          • Sustaining instruments – those that ring or sustain after the initial attack (e.g., cymbals, timpani,
                triangle).
          • Non-sustaining instruments – those with a short, pointed attack (e.g., snare drum, tambourine,
                xylophone).

     4-6. Typically, duration is shown with traditional notation used for any other instrument (e.g., a whole note
     would sustain for four beats). However, to account for sustaining and non-sustaining percussion instruments
     there are several additional common conventions used to notate sustained note values including:

          • Roll indications:
                 Tremolo (tr).
                 Slashes on the note stem (two or three depending on the note value).

          • Duration indications:
                 Using ties (i.e., the end of the tie stops the sound).
                 Using an open tie (i.e., let the note sustain).
                 Laissez vibrer or let vibrate (l.v.) – often used in conjunction with an open tie.

     4-7. Generally, percussionists will not perform a roll unless it is indicated within the part. The performer’s
     experience and ear will ultimately guide their interpretation of how to perform their part(s). A common
     discrepancy seen in percussion parts (especially bass drum and cymbal parts) is when the passage is notated
     in a short note value (e.g., eighth note), when the desired sound is longer (e.g., half note or longer).

     4-8. Regardless of the type of notation, performers must study their parts and understand the notational
     intricacies within each. This is especially important when assigning parts to non-ASI percussionists, and
     section leaders should train and assist non-ASI percussionists unfamiliar with reading percussion notation.

PART SELECTION AND ASSIGNMENTS

     4-9. When programming concert repertoire, commanders should carefully consider the number of players
     required to accurately perform each piece. When repertoire is selected without consideration of the required
     and available players, it places stress on the percussion section and section leader to determine how or if the
     parts can be performed.

     4-10. Assigning parts and personnel for concert band can be a daunting task for section leaders, especially
     for those with little to no section leader experience. When assigning parts, section leaders must consider:

          • Commander’s intent:
                 Use of non-ASI percussionists (e.g., do they have a preference on the use of these players?).
                 Permission to omit parts/instruments (if necessary).

          • The number of rehearsals scheduled.
          • The number of 9Ms available.
          • The number of non-ASI percussionists available.
          • Strengths and weaknesses of players combined with the difficulty of the parts.
          • Instruments available (see paragraphs 4-26 through 4-28).

     4-11. Being proactive is essential when it comes to assigning parts for concert band. The section leader should
     begin the process of assigning parts as soon as repertoire is determined. Once the unit’s librarian distributes
     parts to the band, it is recommended that the section leader double checks the parts against the score. This
     will serve two purposes:

          • To verify that there are no missing or misplaced parts.
          • To determine the availability of non-ASI percussionists (e.g., 9N, 9T, 9U).

     4-12. Once the section leader has verified the availability of non-ASI percussionists and that all necessary
     parts are accessible, they may begin the process of assigning parts. To begin, the section leader must analyze
     the music to determine how many personnel are required for each piece, the equipment needed, how many
     parts, if any, must be substituted or omitted due to lack of equipment or personnel, and production pace (i.e.,
     time between pieces).

PERSONNEL

     4-13. There are several areas of concern when determining the number of personnel needed for concert band
     including part formats, publisher organization of parts, and strengths and weaknesses of players.

Part Formats

     4-14. Parts for concert band are published in a variety of formats which often makes determining the number
     of required personnel difficult. Parts may be labeled by:

          • Single instrument (e.g., snare drum, bass drum, timpani, etc.) with one player/instrument per part.
          • Percussion 1, 2, 3, etc., with one player covering multiple instruments within the part.

             Note. Some parts labeled as “Percussion 1” may require multiple players playing separate
             instruments within the part (e.g., bass drum, cymbals, suspended cymbal). In this example, the
             section leader must determine if the part requires two or three players.

          • A single undivided part with all instruments needed for the entire section (sometimes including
                timpani) on one part.

          • A percussion score with separate staves. The staves may be labeled as:
                 Single instrument (e.g., snare drum, bass drum, xylophone).
                 Percussion 1, 2, 3, etc. with multiple instruments required for a single player.
                 Percussion 1, 2, 3, etc. with multiple instruments requiring multiple players simultaneously.

             Note. Usually, but not always, timpani parts are separate from percussion parts.

     4-15. Regardless of the type of part, section leaders must look for sections within the part(s) where several
     instruments are written to sound at the same time. Usually, but not always, the number of instruments written
     to sound at the same time corresponds to the number of personnel needed within a part.

Publisher Organization of Parts

     4-16. Another common issue (that often occurs due to poor editing) is that parts may be divided, but the
     publisher’s organization or division is impractical due to personnel, space, or equipment constraints. Some
     examples include:

          • Creating a separate part that could be covered by one or several other players.
          • Having separate mallet parts (e.g., bells, xylophone, chimes) when one player could cover multiple

                instruments.
          • Dividing an instrument into several separate parts, forcing players to share an instrument (e.g.,

                mallet instruments), or requiring multiples of the same instrument (e.g., cymbals) if the players are
                unable to share due to logistical concerns.
          • Parts written in score format that unnecessarily change players or instruments within the part.
          • Parts written for one player that include a fast switch between instruments requiring assistance from
                another player.

     4-17. When reviewing a piece, look at the composer/editor/publisher’s organization first. Sometimes it is the
     most logical arrangement of equipment and players. Next look at the unit’s percussion section and available
     auxiliary players. When assigning parts, answer the following:

          • If using the composer/editor/publisher’s organization, are there enough personnel to cover all the
                parts?

          • Can the section leader combine or “pencil/write in” parts for the players available?
                 For example, if the piece requires seven players and currently only three are assigned to the
                     section, either there must be aggressive doubling or rewriting of parts, or some parts must be
                     omitted.
                 Another example would be if the publisher has parts for multiple players but several of those
                     parts could be combined into a single part for one player.

MULTIPLE PERCUSSION PERFORMANCE

     4-32. Percussion parts in concert band often require one person to play two or more instruments
     simultaneously, or several instruments throughout a part. This is referred to as multiple percussion
     performance. Many (but not all) percussionists have experience playing multiple instruments simultaneously
     and this should be taken into consideration when analyzing and assigning parts. Non-ASI percussionists
     should not be expected to cover multiple instruments within a part or to play two percussion instruments
     simultaneously. Several factors are involved in successfully performing multiple parts including ease of
     reading, and logic of setup.

Ease of Reading

     4-33. Parts written for several instruments (e.g., Perc 1 – snare drum, woodblock, tambourine, suspended
     cymbal) can be notated in many ways, and differ by composer, editor, or publisher. For parts intended for a
     single player, most publishers include a “key” at the top of the part labeling instruments. If there is not a
     “key,” it is recommended to write one into the part.

     4-34. When learning and practicing these parts, performers are encouraged to pencil-in notes throughout the
     parts as necessary. For parts with multiple measures of rest between instruments, mark the part with each
     instrument change (e.g., mark the last note played on an instrument with “To X” (X= the new instrument)).

     4-35. Sometimes publishers will separate instruments into parts to be played by several players. Often these
     parts can be combined into a single part for one player or divided or added into multiple players’ parts, turning
     these into multiple percussion parts. If combining parts, consider penciling in cues. If writing in cues makes
     the part difficult to read, consider rewriting the part to make it more legible. Sometimes it may be more
     efficient to rewrite parts, as parts can then be saved and reused in the section.

Logic of Setup

     4-36. One of the most difficult things about performing multiple percussion parts is determining the setup.
     There is no prescribed way to set up instruments when playing multiple percussion parts, and setup often
     comes down to personal preference. The instruments must be accessible and arranged in a logical fashion. If
     the part requires multiple-pitched toms, arrange them according to personal preference (e.g., low to high, or
     high to low). If the part calls for multiple timbred instruments, consider arranging them in pitch relation (e.g.,
     low to high, or high to low). If pitch class accessibility is not important, place the most often played
     instrument (e.g., snare drum) centered on the music stand, and arrange all other instruments (e.g., triangle,
     tambourine, etc.) comfortably within arm’s reach.

     4-37. In addition to the instruments, the performer must have all mallets, sticks, and other striking implements
     within easy reach. This is particularly important when playing a wide variety of different instruments such
     as bass drum, snare drum, suspended cymbal, and triangle all on the same part.

SECTION II – KEYBOARD PERCUSSION

     4-49. The family of keyboard percussion instruments (often referred to as mallets instruments or simply
     mallets) have a wide role in concert band music, ranging from doubling woodwind lines to featured solo
     moments. Contemporary concert band/wind ensemble literature often requires multiple mallet parts. For
     smaller percussion sections, the ASI percussionists may not be able to cover all or any of these parts, which
     requires the use of non-ASI percussionists (e.g., 9Ns). Usually, but not always, there is at least one
     experienced ASI percussionist in the section that can cover the bulk of the mallet parts or assist the training
     of non-ASI percussionists.

     4-50. This section describes the common characteristics of the keyboard percussion instruments commonly
     used in Army Bands: the marimba, xylophone, vibraphone, orchestral bells, and chimes.

COMMON CHARACTERISTICS

     4-51. Aside from bar material (wood vs. metal), keyboard percussion instruments have several common
     characteristics including their construction, striking point on the bars, and playing technique.

CONSTRUCTION

     4-52. Keyboard percussion instruments are manufactured in the same set up as piano keyboards, with raised
     accidental keys. The exception is the vibraphone, which has all keys on a flat playing surface due to the
     dampening pedal mechanism. The bars (tubes for chimes) are suspended by cords, strings, or pins via nodal
     points. Sound projection is assisted by resonators on xylophone, marimba, and vibraphone. Chimes and
     orchestral bells do not require resonators, although most orchestra bell sets are held in wooden cases which
     act as a resonating chamber.

     4-53. Resonators are hollow tubes lined up beneath the center of the bars to reinforce the intensity of the
     tone. Resonator tubes are closed at one end to provide an acoustic air spring that vibrates and resonates with
     the sound waves produced by the bar. The effective length of the tube corresponds to the pitch of its assigned
     note (i.e., the lower the note, the longer the tube). See figure 4-3 for examples.

STRIKING POINT OF THE BARS

     4-54. There are two schools of thought on the correct striking point of the bars. These points relate to all
     instruments except chimes, which has their own specific striking point. The preferred striking point is the
     center or slightly off-center of the bar. This provides the most consistent sound, the greatest vibration of the
     bar, and is directly over the resonator openings (see figure 4-4).

                            Figure 4-4. Keyboard percussion striking points

4-55. The alternate striking point is at the end/edge of the bar. The edge of the bar produces a resonant tone,
but projection is slightly restricted. This works well for marimba and xylophone when executing fast passages
and using more than two mallets. While some use this striking point for all accidental keys, others will avoid
using it unless necessary.

4-56. Either striking area is correct and is determined by personal preference, specific mallet instrument (e.g.,
vibraphone and orchestral bells), or musical requirements. The player must decide if the distance the mallet(s)
must travel to reach the center of the bar is practical in the tempo of the passage and their technique. Avoid
striking the node of the bar (i.e., location of the cord, string, or pins) as this area of the bar produces the least
amount of tone and resonance.

PLAYING TECHNIQUE

     4-57. Use a matched grip (i.e., both palms facing down) for two-mallet playing. As with most other
     percussion instruments the most effective stroke on mallet instruments is a direct up-down stroke. Mallet
     instruments have little natural rebound, but in most cases, the existing rebound coupled with the flex of the
     mallet handle will provide sufficient rebound and response. As much as is practical, keep the mallet shafts
     parallel to the playing surface, and keep the mallets relatively close to the bars to minimize excess motion.
     This also aids the player’s proprioception to sense the position of the bars more accurately.

     4-58. Sticking is often a problem on keyboard percussion. Traditionally, passages are sticked alternately
     hand-to-hand for an even and consistent sound. This is usually the best sticking method, but problems often
     arise that require double sticking (i.e., striking two consecutive notes with the same mallet) for a more
     consistent flow or speed. Whenever possible, avoid double sticking great interval leaps. Use the sticking
     method best suited for fluid execution of the passage.

     4-59. Sustained notes are played as rolls on the marimba and xylophone. Rolls are performed as rapidly
     alternating single strokes that must be played evenly and smoothly. The roll speed will be determined by the
     type of mallet used and the register of the instrument (e.g., lower registers require slower roll speed, while
     higher registers require faster speed). They may be played on one note or between two notes and are notated
     with a tremolo sign or written as 32nd notes.

MAINTENANCE

     4-60. Maintenance requirements for mallet instruments are very similar, with the primary difference being
     the difference in bar material. Mallet instruments should be covered when not in use to prevent dirt and dust
     buildup on the bars.

     4-61. The steps for inspecting keyboard percussion instruments and identifying any deficiencies are:
          • Check the bars for cracks, dents, or damage.
          • Check the spacers between the bars:
                 Are any bent, missing, or damaged?
                 Check rubber post isolators for dry rot.
          • Check the bar support cords for proper tension and bar height.
          • Check for bent or damaged mounting posts.
          • Check all bolts, screws, and nuts.
                 Ensure proper tension.
                 Check for stripped or missing hardware.
          • Check the pedal spring for proper tension (vibraphone or bells (if applicable)).
          • Check the felt strip for even muffling (vibraphone or bells (if applicable)).
          • Check resonator tubes for dents or damage.
          • Check the vibraphone motor (if applicable).
                 Ensure fans turn freely.
                 Ensure the motor works properly (i.e., speed changes).
          • Ensure wheel casters roll freely and lock properly.

     4-62. The steps for cleaning key board percussion instruments are:
          • Wipe the instrument with a clean, soft cloth.
          • Lightly oil rosewood marimba and xylophone bars with wax-free lemon oil.
          • Do not use metal polish on metal or wood bars.
          • Lightly oil the vibraphone resonator fan drive rods with light machine oil.
          • Lightly oil the vibraphone foot pedal pivot points.
          • Clean quarterly or as needed.

     has increased significantly.

IMPLEMENT SELECTION

     4-65. Yarn-covered mallets are most often used on marimba, but there are exceptions. For a more articulate
     attack sound, rubber mallets may be used. Cord-covered mallets (which are more suitable for vibraphone)
     may be used, but usually the harder versions sound harsh and brittle when used on marimba. The mallet
     shafts (handles) come in a variety of materials and offer different amounts of flex when used.

Yarn Mallets

     4-67. These mallets have a rubber or synthetic core (which may be covered in additional layers of rubber)
     and are covered with varying types of synthetic or natural yarn. Yarn mallets may be used in the following
     applications:

          • Hard yarn mallets are good for ensemble and solo (e.g., concerto) playing because they provide
                clear articulation throughout the entire range of the instrument while allowing the instrument to be
                heard over band accompaniment. Avoid over playing with harder mallets in lower registers to
                prevent damage to the bars.

          • Medium or medium-hard yarn mallets are also good for ensemble playing and are recommended for
                best overall tone production throughout the instrument.

          • Soft yarn mallets are excellent for background or accompanying parts. They produce an organ-like
                tone quality in the lower octaves, but the sound in the upper octaves is choked.

          • Extra soft yarn mallets are used only in the lower octaves for a pizzicato string bass effect or a
                mellow sustained sound.

Rubber Mallets

     4-68. Rubber mallets are another option and provide better articulation throughout all registers of the

          • Soft rubber mallets are similar in sound to medium yarn mallets but provide more attack.

Shaft Material

     4-69. The choice of mallet shaft (handle) is up to personal preference, with two common options being
     wooden (e.g., birch, cedar, maple, or hickory) or rattan. Wooden handles have almost no flex and are
     preferred by soloists for maintaining consistent interval spreads, which aid in accuracy for four-mallet
     playing. Rattan handles have a range of thicknesses and degrees of flex. Many players prefer rattan for two-
     mallet playing.

XYLOPHONE

     4-70. The xylophone is one of the oldest instruments in the mallet family. Its characteristic high, brittle sound
     makes it usable as a solo voice, as an added accompanying timbre with other instruments in concert band, or
     to accentuate rhythmic passages. In many cases, the xylophone is used to double woodwind lines to add
     additional brightness to the line.

CHARACTERISTICS

     4-71. The bars of the xylophone are made from rosewood, Kelon® or Acoustilon®. The bars are typically
     the same width throughout the range. Xylophones have resonators but can be used without them due to the
     piercing tone quality of the instrument (see figure 4-6).

IMPLEMENT SELECTION

     4-73. Implement selection for xylophone follows similar considerations to marimba. To produce the tone
     characteristic of the xylophone, use hard rubber, synthetic plastic, or wooden (e.g., rosewood) mallets. For

VIBRAPHONE

     4-76. Often associated with jazz music, the vibraphone has seen increased usage in contemporary concert
     band/wind ensemble literature. Its distinctive sound makes it usable as a solo voice, as an added
     accompanying timbre to other instruments in concert band, or to accentuate rhythmic passages. Performance
     on vibraphone requires understanding of the instrument’s characteristics, dampening techniques, and
     implement selection.

CHARACTERISTICS

     4-77. Vibraphone bars are made from anodized aluminum. The material produces a tone much longer in
     duration than wooden bars. The bars are generally graduated in width (i.e., wider for the low notes and
     narrower for the high notes). The keyboard is flat because of a dampening mechanism that requires the bars
     to be at the same level as the felt dampening bar. The dampening bar is attached to a pedal operated by the
     foot. The dampening pedal is operated in a similar manner as the damper pedal on a piano, but the pedal must
     be depressed before the attack for the note to sustain (see figure 4-7).

     4-78. The vibraphone name comes from the vibrato effect produced by fans (rotating discs held by a single

DAMPENING TECHNIQUES

     4-79. Specific performance problems on the vibraphone relate to pedaling and dampening. Pedaling on the
     vibraphone is much like a piano, but, with the smaller range and vibrato (when the motor is turned on), the
     pedal is used less than the piano.

     4-80. Three methods of dampening are available, and they can be used separately or in combination with
     each other: they are pedal dampening, hand dampening, and mallet dampening.

Pedal Dampening

     4-81. The notes are muffled according to their duration and phrase markings. The pedal must be depressed
     before the attack for the note to sustain and released to dampen the note.

Hand Dampening

     4-82. This technique uses the fingers, hands, or a combination of both. With the pedal depressed using hand
     damping allows the player to mute single notes within a passage while allowing other notes to continue
     ringing.

Mallet Dampening

     4-83. This technique is accomplished by pressing the mallets against the bars. This method allows desired
     notes to ring while muffling others. This method is also effective in creating a legato or slurred effect. Muffle
     one bar with the mallet while simultaneously attacking the next note. The pedal must be depressed for this
     dampening method. Using mallet dampening allows the vibraphone to be more expressive with the sustained
     notes, playing ties and slurs with almost wind-instrument accuracy. Vibraphone Technique, by David
     Friedman, is an excellent book on the subject (see Appendix A).

IMPLEMENT SELECTION

     4-84. A wide variety of mallets are available for the vibraphone. Cord or yarn-covered mallets are used
     almost exclusively on vibes. For general playing, varying degrees of hardness in cord or yarn-covered mallets
     will suffice and several sets may be necessary for solo playing. As with marimba and xylophone, when buying
     mallets specifically for vibe playing, buy sets of four, or two pairs.

     4-85. Mallets for vibraphone are generally categorized by hardness levels:
          • Soft mallets provide less attack and more tone. They are used mostly for background
                accompaniments.
          • Medium mallets are good for general-purpose playing.
          • Hard mallets are used for solo passages where less tone and more precise articulation is required.
          • Rubber mallets are generally used for practice purposes, special effects, or for additional attack and
                articulation.

SECTION III – TIMPANI

     4-97. Timpani are the largest tunable, definite pitch drums in the percussion section, capable of dynamics
     softer or louder than any other instrument in the band. They have a versatile instrumental role that ranges
     from punctuating the tonic and dominant to functioning as a soloist.

     4-98. Timpani are available in sizes from 32 to 20 inches in diameter. The required pitch(es) will determine
     the size used, as each drum has a practical range of a perfect fifth (see figure 4-11). The larger the diameter,
     the lower the possible pitch. A large portion of early concert band literature can be performed on at least two

     century pushed the boundaries of timpani writing with many compositions requiring four drums (32”, 29”,
     26”, 23”). If higher notes are required (F3-C4) a fifth drum (20”) is added.

PEDAL TYPES

     4-99. Modern timpani change pitch using a foot pedal to adjust the tension of the head. The use of a pedal
     facilitates fast tuning changes, the performance of glissandos, and other advanced techniques. The two
     common pedal mechanisms are balanced action and locking.

BALANCED ACTION PEDAL

     4-100. The pitch of a balanced action drum is maintained through the balance of tension between the timpani
     head and the pedal spring. This balance sets the pitch of the drum and allows the player to remove their foot
     from the pedal. The lowest pitch (least amount of head tension) is achieved with the pedal in the bottom
     position (heel down). As the pedal is pressed forward (towards the drum with the foot) the head tension
     tightens and raises the pitch. When properly maintained, this pedal system is very easy to operate and is
     useful when playing glissandos and pieces that require frequent pitch changes (see figure 4-12).

             Note. Some balanced action drums are equipped with a hand-operated adjustment knob for fine-
             tuning.

GENERAL RULES FOR PERFORMANCE

     4-102. Professional timpanists devote countless hours to the mastery of their instrument and there is a wealth
     of published resources available. Army percussionists are not expected to be experts on timpani, but they
     should have a basic understanding of the fundamentals required to perform on the instrument(s) as required
     in concert band. This includes the instrument’s setup, playing areas, stroke technique, implement selection,
     muffling, and tuning.

SETUP

     4-103. There are two common playing set-ups for timpani: German and American. The German set-up places
     the smallest drum to the left, and the largest drum on the right (i.e., 23”, 26”, 29”, 32”). The American set-
     up is the opposite: largest on the left and smallest on the right (i.e., 32”, 29”, 26”, 23”). Set-up is determined
     by personal preference; however not all members of the section may play the same set-up which may cause
     logistical concerns if players rotate during concerts. The center of the timpani set-up (i.e., the 29”, 26” drums)
     should be in line with the music stand and the conductor.

     4-104. Playing position is very important. Some players prefer to stand, while others prefer to sit. This is up
     to personal preference, and determined by the following:

          • Arrange the timpani in a semi-circle ensuring all pedals are within easy reach of the feet.
          • The drums should be close together but not touching. This allows free movement of the heads while

                tuning and prevents the drums from rattling against each other.
          • Players should center themselves within the setup keeping the drums they are playing in front of

                them. Being centered allows the player to perform passages across several drums.
          • Playing position should allow the performer to pivot/rotate at the waist when playing across several

                drums.

PLAYING AREAS

     4-105. Generally, the playing area on the timpani head is two to three inches from the rim, depending on the
     size of the drum. The playing area on a larger drum will be further away from the rim. Playing near the rim
     produces a thin sound and playing close to or in the center produces a dull sound with several high overtones
     and minimal fundamental pitch (see figure 4-14).

                                           Figure 4-14. Timpani playing areas

     4-106. Playing areas should be reachable by turning from the waist. Using a stool is recommended for easier
     movement unless the player is too short to reach the pedals. If a stool is used, ensure that it is spaced so that
     the feet can comfortably reach the pedals, and the hands can reach the correct areas for playing and hand
     muffling. If standing, use a comfortable, relaxed stance leaning slightly forward from the waist for better
     leverage. In either case, movement from drum to drum should primarily be from the waist.

STROKE TECHNIQUE

     4-107. The basic stroke for percussion instruments consists of two parts: a down motion (the stroke) and an
     up motion (the rebound). On most instruments the rebound is aided by the relatively constant tension of the
     playing surface (e.g., snare drum). On timpani however, it is more difficult since the relative tension varies
     from drum to drum and note to note. These variations do not allow for the maximum use of the energy
     generated by the stroke. This means that the player may have to assist the rebound a bit (especially on larger
     drums or lower notes).

     4-108. There are three common grips taught and used by professional timpanists, and the best grip to use
     depends on player preference. A complete examination of grips may require further study through published
     materials or with a qualified instructor. The three common grips used are:

          • German grip – is a matched grip with the hands turned over and palms facing the floor. It is similar
                to the matched grip for snare drum. This grip primarily uses the wrist to produce the stroke.

          • French grip – is made by holding the hands with the thumbs up and palms facing each other. The
                wrist is used to initiate the stroke (e.g., like turning a doorknob). This grip relies heavily on the
                fingers to control the stroke and for playing rolls.

          • American grip – is a matched grip with the hands turned over with the thumb approximately 45°
                from the playing surface. This grip is similar to the matched grip for snare drum and allows the use
                of wrist and finger strokes.

     4-109. Rolls are performed as rapidly alternating single strokes. The speed of the stroke is critical to tone
     production of the timpani roll. The size and pitch of the head will determine the speed of the roll. The smaller
     the timpani and higher the pitch (i.e., higher head tension), the faster the roll speed. The larger the timpani
     and lower the pitch (i.e., lower head tension), the slower the roll. This gives great flexibility when playing

IMPLEMENT SELECTION

     4-110. There should be at least three pairs of mallets available when playing timpani:
          • Hard mallets for staccato and rapid rhythmic passages.
          • Medium mallets for general-purpose playing.
          • Soft mallets for smooth legato notes or sustained roll passages.

     4-111. Using mallets with a solid core material (e.g., wood, hard felt, or cork) will provide enough weight to
     produce the desired sound. The articulation of the notes and the technique used will ultimately determine the
     type of mallet(s) to use.

     4-112. Several types of mallets are available, providing a wide range of possible sounds. They range from
     wooden mallets that produce a very articulated staccato tone to extra soft mallets that provide a velvet-like
     tone with minimal contact sound. Special effects or composer’s instructions may require the use of other
     implements such as drumsticks, yarn mallets, or superball-type mallets. Use musical discretion and common
     sense when using these non-traditional implements, making sure to prevent damage to the heads.

MUFFLING

     4-113. When muffling on timpani, the object is to give notes their full value while keeping them from
     interfering with the succeeding harmonic structure. When playing notes on two or more timpani in
     succession, the ringing tones produce a clustered harmonic effect. Muffling one timpani while striking the
     other will produce a clean and precise tone.

     4-114. The best method of muffling is to play with one hand and use the other hand to muffle the head. The
     muffling hand must make total contact with the fingers spread in the playing area. Be careful not to slap the
     head or bend the pitch by applying too much pressure on the head. A sweeping motion with the hand and
     fingers inward towards the rim of the drum usually works best.

     4-115. Fast passages require muffling and playing with the same hand. While holding the mallet between the
     thumb and forefinger, use the other fingers to muffle the head. Do not let the mallet strike the head while
     muffling. The best way to muffle is to use a sweeping or wiping motion with as much hand contact as
     possible. (see figure 4-15).

     4-116. When using hand muffling techniques, dirt and oil from the fingertips and hands will transfer to the

     cleaning the hands prior to playing and cleaning the heads with a lint free cloth (e.g., microfiber cleaning

     4-117. Mechanical muffling is accomplished with circular felt pads, handkerchiefs, or similar devices. They

     They are placed in different areas of the head for different effects. Mechanical muffling can also be used to

TUNING

     4-118. Tuning timpani can be approached in several different ways. The best and recommended method is
     to approach the desired pitch from below. This is achieved by lowering the pedal (loosening the head) below
     the desired note and moving the pedal up (tightening the head) to the desired note.

     4-119. Using a fixed reference pitch (e.g., note within the band, pitch from a piano, or tuning fork), strike or
     hum into the drum while moving the pedal up to the desired pitch. Players can check their tuning by striking
     the drum again to verify the tuning. If using the humming method, the drum will “sing back” or resonate the
     desired pitch.

     4-120. Avoid using mallet instruments as a reference source, as most modern instruments are tuned to A=442,
     while bands tune to A=440. It is also recommended to avoid using pitch pipes for tuning. Pitch pipes are
     often out of tune themselves and are affected by the amount of air used, causing the pitch to be sharp or flat.

     4-121. The best practice when tuning is to have the ear close to the drum to hear the fundamental pitch. The
     further away from the drum the ear moves, the more overtones will be heard.

             Note. Modern timpani are often equipped with tuning gauges with movable letters that correspond
             to pitches within the range of the drum attached to the frame. Gauges (i.e., the movable letters)
             may be adjusted and set by using an electronic tuner. Unless properly maintained and checked for
             accuracy tuning gauges should be used with caution, especially following long periods of storage,
             travel, or maintenance. When performing, as the intonation changes within the ensemble the player
             may have to adjust pitches relative to the gauge (e.g., sharp (i.e., above the gauge letter) or flat
             (i.e., below the gauge letter)).

     4-122. Factors to consider when tuning several drums at once include:
          • The requirements of the music (e.g., how many different pitches are in the part?).
          • The range of the drums (e.g., do not expect to get a top space G (bass clef) on the 29-inch drum.).
          • The tension of the head relative to the pitch (e.g., a second space C (bass clef) may sound better on
                the 29-inch drum (higher head tension) than on the 26-inch drum (lower head tension).
          • The proximity of the pitch changes (e.g., can the pitch stay on the same drum, or does the pitch
                require moving to a different drum?).

     4-123. If there is a part with multiple tuning changes, here are ways to approach the problem (see figure 4-
     16):

          • Look over the part and find out how many drums are required (i.e., how many pitches and the ranges
                between pitches that occur in a single excerpt). Label the part with the starting pitches (e.g., F-A-C-
                Eb in figure 4-16). If there are only two pitches at a given time, the player may either play the piece
                with two drums and change pitches frequently or use more drums to save time.

          • Find the most difficult tuning changes and see if there is a note in the part that can be used as a
                reference. If not, relate the new note to a previous note played. Ear training is essential, and players
                must be able to sing any interval within the space of one octave. This will help to find odd pitches
                when tuning on the fly.

          • Mark the part with each tuning change (e.g., mark the last note played with “To X,” or an arrow
                up/down “To X” (X= the new pitch)). Then practice making the tuning changes. Use a fixed pitch
                source to check progress and accuracy.

MAINTENANCE

     4-124. Operator-level maintenance on timpani is often neglected as many percussionists are unsure how to
     properly maintain the instruments. Problems usually arise from improperly seated heads, tuning and clearing
     the heads, and pedal mechanism issues. These problems are the result of misuse/mistreatment during
     transport or storage and neglect of routine maintenance overtime.

             Note. This publication only provides general maintenance considerations. Operators should follow
             the specific steps provided in manufacturer’s maintenance paperwork. See appendix A for more
             information.

SEATING THE HEAD

     4-125. Timpani heads must be centered in the middle of the bowl with an even amount of space between the
     counter hoop (rim) and the bowl’s edge. This allows the head to evenly stretch and freely move over the bowl
     during tuning. An improperly seated head will damage and shorten the life of the head, while also preventing
     it from being in tune with itself (also referred to as being clear).

TUNING AND CLEARING THE HEAD

     4-126. Since timpani are tuned to specific pitches, the head must be tuned to the correct range (i.e., lowest
     and highest notes, see figure 4-11) while also being perfectly in tune with itself. This is achieved by tuning
     the head following manufacturer’s specifications for the lowest pitch on each drum. Once the lowest pitch is
     achieved, ensure even tension by matching the pitch at all tuning bolts/tension rods. This process will clear
     all unwanted harmonics and puts the head in-tune with itself. This process will be completed as needed, with
     a minimum of once every six months. If tuning issues persist after the head is cleared and tuned to its proper
     range, there may be a problem with the pedal mechanism.

PEDAL MECHANISM ISSUES

     4-127. A common issue for balanced action pedal mechanisms is pedal slipping (e.g., the pedal will not hold
     the desired pitch and the pedal either moves up or down). This occurs when the pedal spring is out of balance
     with the tension of the head. Prior to adjusting the pedal spring, the player must ensure that the head is cleared
     and tuned to the appropriate bottom note. If the pedal will not stay on the bottom note (pedal moves up), then
     the spring tension is too tight and should be loosened. If the pedal will not stay on a higher note (pedal moves
     down), then the spring tension is too loose, and should be tightened.

PROPER HANDLING DURING TRANSPORT AND STORAGE

     4-129. For balanced action drums, the frame (sometimes referred to as struts) covers the tuning bolts/tension
     rods. When moving, rolling, or carrying the drums ensure to grip the drum by the frame. Pedals should be
     placed in the top position of the range (i.e., highest head tension) to prevent the head from being unseated
     (e.g., if the drum is dropped or hit while going through a door frame).

     4-130. The tuning bolts/tension rods on locking pedal tympani are usually uncovered and there are separate
     frame supports for gripping. When moving, rolling, or carrying the drums ensure to grip the drum by the
     frame supports, not the tuning bolts/tension rods. Use extra caution with moving through doors to avoid
     damage to exposed tuning bolts/tension rods or unseating the head.

             Note. Do not pull, grip, move, or carry timpani by their tuning bolts/tension rods or rims. Doing
             so may unseat, detune, or damage the head, which may cause issues with the tuning mechanism.

     4-131. When possible, transport timpani in hard cases. Ensure that the drum’s wheel caster locks are engaged,
     and the case’s retention strap (if applicable) are used to prevent movement inside the case. When transported
     in cases, pedals may be placed in the middle of the drum’s range to keep an even amount of tension on the
     head and pedal mechanism.

     4-132. Timpani hard cases may be stacked during transport. If stacked, ensure that smaller cases are placed
     on bigger cases (e.g., 23” on 26,” and 29” on 32”), with wheel caster locks engaged. If stacking cases, DO
     NOT flip the cases up-side-down. This will place extraneous stress on the heads and rims.

     4-133. If cases are unavailable, balanced action drums can be secured to a wall of the equipment truck with
     ratchet straps. Straps should only be wrapped around the frame of the drum (at least two tuning bolt/tension
     rod supports/struts). Ensure that the pedals are angled towards the wall, and heads are covered with drop
     covers or hard timpani head covers to prevent damage to heads. Place the pedal in the top position (highest
     head tension) to prevent the head from being unseated if other equipment bumps or moves inside the vehicle.

             Note. Locking pedal drums should always travel in hard cases during vehicle transport to prevent
             damage to exposed tuning bolts/tension rods.

     4-134. For prolonged periods of storage, drums should be stored in a temperature-controlled environment.
     They may be stored in or out of cases (situation and space permitting). Ensure that the heads are covered to
     prevent accumulation of dust or dirt. To further prolong head life, place the pedals in the bottom position.

     4-135. Following prolonged periods of storage, check for any changes in functionality:
          • Have any of the heads unseated?
          • Have the drums maintained their proper tonal ranges?
          • Are the heads still clear and in tune?
          • Do the pedal mechanisms operate smoothly and hold pitch?

     4-136. If any issues are present, troubleshoot using information in paragraphs 4-124 through 4-128. If issues
     persist, consult the manufacturer’s maintenance paperwork.

TUNING

     4-147. After selecting a preferred drum, head type, and snare type(s), attention should be given to proper
     tuning. To achieve the characteristic sound for symphonic music the performer must consider how they tune
     the heads and snares.

Head Tuning

     4-148. There are many ways to tune snare drums. The type of tuning is dictated by the style of music being
     performed and the associated characteristic sound. For concert snare drums, tuning starts with the batter
     (playing) side head. Start by leveling and clearing the head to ensure that each tension rod is pulled down
     evenly across the drum, allowing for even tension of the head. Once the head is level, tune in small increments
     to reach the desired pitch. After the desired pitch is reached, re-check that the head is level and clear.

     4-149. The depth of the drum will determine the tuning pitch, with the recommended following notes:
          • Drums 2 ½ to 4 inches in depth are tuned to Bb4 to C4.
          • Drums 5 to 6 ½ inches in depth are tuned to G4 to Bb4.

     4-150. The snare side (bottom) head is tuned in the range of a major 3rd to a tritone higher than the top head
     for drums ranging in size from 2 ½ to 6 ½ inches in depth. For deeper drums (10 to 16 inches in depth), the

     required for concert music. The brand and thickness of head will be determining factors for any tuning
     scheme and experimentation is encouraged to achieve the player’s desired sound.

Snare Tuning

     4-151. For snare tuning, whether using a single snare type or a “multi-sonic/phonic” snare system, check the
     snare response at all dynamic levels. The snares should not be so loose that the drum sounds fuzzy, or so
     tight that the drum sounds choked. The snares should be tuned to achieve clear articulation while also
     allowing for smooth buzz rolls.

     4-152. If using a drum with a “mutli-sonic/phonic” snare system it is recommended to follow the
     manufacturer’s guidance for individual snare tuning, but most of these systems are used in the following way:

          • Wire snares are tuned for softer dynamics with responsiveness at ppp to mp.
          • Cable snares are tuned for mid-level dynamics with responsiveness at mp to mf/f.
          • Gut snares are tuned for louder dynamics with responsiveness at f to fff.

     4-153. A common tuning method when using a “multi-sonic/phonic” snare system is the following:
          • Start with the thickest material (gut or coated cable) and tighten them until the response is slightly
                choked at ff.
          • Next tune the medium material (uncoated cable or wire) and tighten them until they respond in the
                mp to mf dynamic range.
          • Finally, tune the thinnest material (fine guitar string or curly wire) and tighten them until they
                respond at pp approximately an inch from the edge of the drum.

     4-154. When used in combination and tuned to recommended dynamic ranges, combination snare systems
     provide a consistent sound across all dynamics levels. Usually, but not always, if the drum sounds slightly
     choked off from the players perspective, then it will sound great from the audience’s perspective.

     4-155. During rehearsal or performance, it is recommended to keep the snares disengaged whenever the drum
     is not in use to avoid unwanted sympathetic vibration of the snares. When storing or transporting snare drums,
     it is recommended to keep the snares engaged to keep them properly seated to the bottom head.

     4-156. Concert snare drumsticks are made from a variety of materials including hickory, rosewood,
     persimmon, maple, or carbon fiber. The length, diameter, and weight of the stick to be used are determined
     by the size of the drum being used and the musical application. For example, a large stick will overpower a
     smaller drum and choke the sound. Smaller sticks (e.g., those used for drum set) are not heavy enough to
     provide a full sound for concert music. While stick choice involves personal preference, it is recommended
     to select a stick designed for concert band or orchestral use. These sticks fall in-between drum set (lighter)
     and marching (longer and heavier) sticks.

     4-157. Parts are often written for brushes to provide a lighter sound or a swish effect. Depending on the
     requirements of the part, brushes used for drum set may not be loud enough to cut through the ensemble. The
     performer may have to play louder than indicated or use a thicker or heavier pair of brushes. If the part is
     fairly rhythmic, performers may substitute plastic brushes for better volume, clarity, and articulation.

     4-158. Other implements such as yarn, felt, or timpani mallets can be used effectively for a tom-tom sound
     (with the snares off). Use discretion when playing with alternative implements to achieve the desired sound
     without damaging the head.

Rolls

     4-160. Accurate performance of symphonic music requires the percussionist to have a mastery of the closed
     or “buzzed” roll, as most but not all rolls are performed as closed rolls. Details in closed rolls can be broken
     down by roll duration and roll shape.

     4-161. To address roll duration, it is recommended to find a hand speed (sometimes called a motor rhythm)
     to meter rolls such as:

          • Depending on the tempo, pick a rhythm (e.g., eighth-notes, triplets, or sixteenth notes) to buzz.
                There should not be a discernable rhythm heard, but rather it is used as a guide to stay in time.

          • Crescendos can be performed as a single rhythm or by changing the hand speed by speeding up the
                motor rhythm (e.g., for a whole note duration, play the first two beats as triplets and the last two
                beats as sixteenths).

          • Decrescendos can be performed as a single rhythm or by changing the hand speed by slowing down
                the motor rhythm (e.g., for a whole note duration, play the first two beats as sixteenths and the last
                two beats as triplets).

     4-162. Roll shape refers to the density of overlapping buzzed strokes played by each hand paired with the
     chosen motor rhythm. Buzzed strokes can be performed in densities ranging from three to six bounces per
     stroke. There are many different approaches to define and aid roll shape. A common approach is to select a
     consistent buzz stroke density while finding a hand speed (motor rhythm) within the tempo that produces a
     full sound (e.g., three bounces per stroke and played as triplets or sixteenth notes at 120 beats per minute).

     4-163. Players are encouraged to experiment with roll duration and shape as each drum is unique and may
     sound completely different depending on the performers technique and choices made. The following are
     different ways to approach rolls:

          • Playing with lower density buzz strokes at faster hand speeds (e.g., three bounces per stroke and
                motor rhythm of quintuplets at 90 beats per minutes).

          • Playing with higher density buzz strokes and higher hand speeds at slower tempos (e.g., four to five
                bounces per stroke and motor rhythm of quintuplets at 60 beats per minute).

          • To round out the sound and prevent fatigue for longer rolls at louder dynamics, play with lower
                density strokes and slower hand speeds (e.g., three bounces per stroke and motor rhythm of triplets
                at 160 beats per minute).

             Note. The goal of closed rolls is to produce a full sustained sound without discernable accents,
             rhythms, or pulses. Rolls are the percussionist’s way of replicating the wind player’s long tone.

     4-164. Players may perform rolls as open double stroke rolls when parts are written for Field Drum. These
     parts are written to replicate the sound of the rudimental drumming tradition, specifically rope tensioned
     drums. When performing these types of parts, players should follow conventions for open rudimental rolls
     (e.g., 5, 7, 9, 15-stroke rolls). Another common convention is to perform concert marches from the late 19th
     and early 20th centuries with open double stroke rolls. See paragraphs 3-17 through 3-20 for more
     information.

SECTION V – BASS DRUM

     4-175. Unlike its role in ceremonial music, where it is used primarily for keeping time during troop
     movement, the bass drum has greater musical responsibilities in concert music. The bass drum provides a
     dark timbre to concert music that stresses accents and boosts dynamics, while also providing rhythmic drive.

GENERAL RULES FOR PERFORMANCE

     4-176. Concert bass drums are available from 14 to 20 inches in depth, and 28 to 40 inches in diameter. The
     size of the drum will determine its pitch range (i.e., how high or low it can be tuned) and resonance (i.e., the
     amount of sustain). Smaller drums have a higher pitch and do not produce the resonant depth required for
     most standard wind band repertoire. Larger drums have a lower fundamental pitch and greater resonance but
     can be difficult to control rhythmically.

     4-177. Since the bass drum has a greater musical role in concert music the fundamentals required to perform
     on the instrument are slightly different than those used for marching and troop movement. They are the
     playing areas, stroke technique, implement selection, muffling, and tuning.

PLAYING AREAS

     4-178. The playing areas on the concert bass drum are in part dependent on the desired tone. Since concert
     bass drums are larger in diameter with lower head tension, they can be harder to control. The goal of
     performing on concert bass drum should be to play a full sound with the correct articulation.

     4-179. Striking the head in the center or at the node provides a sharp, dead attack without many low overtones
     sounding. This area can be used for staccato rhythmic passages, or shorter articulated notes. When playing
     on calfskin heads, avoid overplaying the center or spine area of the head (a slightly thicker area that bisects
     the head), as this can cause the head to tear or break.

     4-180. Most general playing will be done by striking the head slightly off center (usually one or two mallet-
     head widths from center). This provides the most tone, while still allowing for some degree of articulation.
     This area is great for legato notes where long note durations are desired while providing a good combination
     of high and low overtones.

     4-181. Striking the head near the rim (approximately 2-3 inches from the rim) provides a thin, ringing sound
     with higher overtones predominating. It can be used for softer passages or when a thinner sound is desired
     (see figure 4-17).

STROKE TECHNIQUE

     4-183. The strokes and techniques used on the concert bass drum can be divided into single strokes and rolls.

Single Strokes

     4-184. There are a variety of single strokes used on the concert bass drum. A direct in/out stroke (i.e., the
     mallet travels in a straight line perpendicular to the head) is the most useful overall stroke. Some players
     prefer using a rounder “J-stroke” (i.e., the mallet moves as if drawing the letter J). This stroke type can
     cushion the attack of the note as it hits the head to produce a rounder, fuller sound.

     4-185. When performing single strokes/notes, hold the mallet towards the bottom of the shaft to let the weight
     of the mallet do the bulk of the work. This will produce the fullest sounding tone from the bass drum. Choking
     up or holding the middle of the mallet is less efficient and will produce a thinner tone.

Rolls

     4-186. Rolls on bass drum are played with slow alternating single strokes. A general-purpose roll can be
     played with the beating spots approximately halfway between the center of the head and the rim (hands in
     the 12 and 6 o’clock position of a clock face). They can also be played on different areas of the head (closer
     to the rim, or both mallets in the same beating spot) to achieve different sounds and effects.

     4-187. Due to the larger diameter and lower head tension of concert bass drums, the player must find a roll
     speed that is in phase with the vibrations of the head. Listen for an even sound while rolling and let the mallets
     rebound naturally. At softer dynamics the roll speed will be slower. As the dynamic increases the hand speed
     must increase as the vibrations of the head increase. Rolling too fast will interfere with the natural vibrations
     of the head and may begin to sound like a distinct rhythm. Experimentation is encouraged (e.g., drum size,
     head material, and implement choice) with the goal of producing a continuous, non-pulsating, low rumble.

IMPLEMENT SELECTION

     4-188. There are a variety of commercial off-the-shelf striking implements available for use on concert bass
     drum. With various shaft, core, and covering materials used, these mallets allow the percussionist to produce
     a plethora of sounds and articulations. Most parts can be performed with some combination of the following:

          • Hard mallets - wooden (e.g., uncovered or covered with a thin layer of chamois) or hard felt used
                for staccato and rapid rhythmic passages (preferably a matching pair).

          • Medium mallets - covered in layers of felt or wool for general-purpose playing. These mallets are
                excellent for single legato notes that require a combination of depth and articulation.

          • Soft mallets - feature a heavy core covered with thick and soft felt or wool. These mallets are used
                for single legato notes that primarily provide depth rather than articulation.

          • Rollers - are slightly lighter in weight and easier to control when playing rolls (preferably a
                matching pair).

                Note. Use caution and avoid overplaying when using uncovered or wooden mallets, as they
                may dent the head.

     4-189. Composers write for a variety of effects and sounds that require a wide assortment of non-traditional
     implements or mallets (e.g., brushes, timpani mallets, snare drumsticks, rubber “super-ball” mallets). When
     using non-traditional implements the main concern should be performing with good musical taste while
     avoiding damage to the head or instrument.

MUFFLING

     4-190. The style of articulation, acoustics of the venue, resonance and pitch of the instrument, tempo of the
     passage, and instrument(s) in the band being supported will dictate the type and degree of muffling required.
     Notes must be given their full duration, and this can be difficult while playing the bass drum.

     4-191. Muffling on bass drum must be done with some discretion. If over muffled, the drum will lose its
     tone and have a sound closer to a drum set bass drum. If under muffled, the drum will continue to ring and
     lack definition. Calfskin heads need much less muffling on or after attacks, and only light muffling to stop
     sound at the end of the piece.

     4-192. Most muffling is done on the batter (playing) side only, using a sweeping motion similar to that used
     on timpani. With the non-playing hand, sweep from the edge of the drum (near the rim) to a point nearer the
     center of the drum to stop the sound after an attack.

     4-193. There are several advanced muffling techniques available. If the part requires a drier sound, the player
     may use (see figure 4-18):

          • The non-playing hand (resting on the batter head).

          • The knee or leg on the batter head.

                resonant (non-playing side) head. This technique is especially useful while playing concert marches

TUNING

     4-195. The concert bass drum should be the lowest sounding non-pitched instrument in the percussion
     section. To achieve a characteristic bass drum sound, the heads should be tuned to different tensions. Start
     with the resonant (non-playing side). For general-purpose playing, tighten the head until a low sustained
     sound is achieved. If a defined pitch is heard, the head is tuned too high. Once the resonant head has been
     tuned to a low non-pitched sustained sound, flip the drum over and tune the batter (playing side) head. The
     batter head should be tuned slightly higher (approximately one to two half turns of each tension rod). The
     slightly higher tension of the batter head will aid in the articulation of rhythmic passages.

     4-196. Once the drum is tuned, adjustment of the tension/tuning is based on the following:
          • If a defined pitch is heard, the resonant head is too high/tight.
          • If the drum does not ring long enough, the resonant head is too low.

SECTION VI – CYMBALS

     4-204. Composers use cymbals to highlight important or climatic moments in symphonic music. Whether a
     crash played by a pair of hand cymbals or a smooth crescendo from a suspended cymbal roll, cymbals allow
     the percussionist to add a brilliant and penetrating timbre to compositions.

HAND CYMBALS

     4-205. Hand cymbals used in symphonic music range in size from 14 to 22 inches in diameter and vary in
     thickness from paper-thin to extra thick. To provide a greater spectrum of overtones, cymbals of the same
     size should have a slight difference of tone or pitch between them.

     4-206. Large and thick cymbals produce higher fundamental pitches due to the abundance of higher partials
     produced by the thicker metal. These cymbals will sustain longer and may be played louder without the
     danger of overplaying or distortion. Large, thick cymbals however make rhythmic precision difficult and
     require greater physical stamina. Smaller and thinner cymbals produce lower fundamental pitches, yet have
     shorter sustain, faster decay, and distort or wash out faster (thereby limiting dynamic range).

     4-207. The diameter and thickness of the cymbal is chosen using the following considerations:
          • Requirements of the music:
                 Does the part require a “dark” or “brilliant” sound?
                 Dynamic extremes within the part.
                 The instruments being accompanied or supported (if playing with woodwinds, will the cymbals
                     overpower their sound?).
                 Is it a solo crash or part of the ensemble sound?
          • The musical taste of the performer and conductor.

             Note. The player must be willing to experiment with different cymbals and choose accordingly.

NOTATION

     4-208. Cymbal parts use traditional notation but there is often a discrepancy of note length (see paragraphs
     4-4 through 4-8). When performing cymbals in concert band players should listen for instruments that they
     are matching or supporting to determine note length (e.g., cymbal part is notated as an eighth note, but brass
     is playing a whole note).

     4-211. The differences in concert band cymbal setup are the use of pads and the length of the hand loop.
     Players often avoid using cymbal pads for concert band unless necessary. When pads are not used the cymbal
     straps may be pulled further through the bell of the cymbal making the hand loop shorter, while providing
     extra slack on the inside of the cymbal to tie a tighter cymbal knot(s).

Stroke Technique

     4-214. There are many approaches to playing cymbals in symphonic music. The information provided here

     described for right-hand dominant players but may also be adapted for left-hand dominant players.

Orchestral Crash

     4-215. For general-purpose playing in concert band use the orchestral crash technique described in
     paragraphs 3-175 through 3-176 and shown in figure 3-33.

     4-216. The orchestral crash described in chapter three uses a downward dominant hand motion. An
     alternative would be to use an upward dominant hand motion. From the Prepare to Play position lower the
     right hand away from the center line of the body so that the cymbal is parallel to the ground. For the crash,
     bring the right hand up striking the top edge of the right cymbal approximately one to two inches from the
     top edge of the left cymbal. Upon impact, the right hand turns up and out (i.e., 45° angle towards right
     shoulder), while the left hand turns down and out (i.e., 45° angle towards the ground). When performed in
     time this will be a single continuous motion.

     cymbals impact, relax the hands and arms to allow the cymbals to rebound off each other and continue
     through the motion (see figure 4-20).

Repeated Notes

     4-219. Playing cymbals for an extended length of time requires strength and endurance (e.g., performing the

     repeated notes technique described in paragraph 3-178 and figure 3-35.

     hand tilted so that the bottom edge of the cymbal strikes first. Ensure that the cymbals remain slightly offset,

Muffling

     4-221. When muffling the cymbals, always give the note its full value. When composers fail to notate parts
     according to the length of sound(s) desired, muffling is left to the player’s discretion. Listen to the rest of the
     band and judge note lengths according to the band’s articulations. When playing “tutti” parts (e.g., playing
     with the bass drum) the cymbals should only be dampened at the ends of phrases or when the attack is
     staccato.

     4-222. Muffle the cymbals quickly and easily by drawing them into the body (e.g., chest, stomach, armpits,
     or squeezing them into the forearms). When muffling cymbals in dress uniforms, avoid contact with any
     items on the uniform jacket (e.g., name tag, badges, medals, or buttons).

     specifically branded “concert” or “symphonic” suspended cymbals, most medium or medium-heavy crash
     cymbals in the 17–20-inch range work well for most suspended cymbal parts. Important factors to consider
     when selecting a suspended cymbal include:

          • How responsive is the cymbal at various dynamic levels:
                 How does the cymbal respond for a pp roll?
                 Does the sound choke or spread at louder dynamics?

          • If the part needs rhythmic articulation, how clear is the sound? (i.e., what is the balance between
                wash and articulation?).

     4-226. A problem occurs when the part requires rolls and articulated rhythmic passages. The obvious choice
     would be to use a cymbal that sounds good for both. If the cymbal(s) available do not sound good for both,
     players may have to use more than one cymbal (space permitting).

     4-227. When possible, avoid using one of a pair of crash cymbals for suspended cymbal playing unless the
     cymbal is properly mounted. This means that the cymbal still has its strap and is suspended by the strap on a
     boom stand. The earliest uses of suspended cymbals were mounted in this way, or with a hook stand that
     used the strap as the attachment to the stand (see figure 4-21).

Playing Areas

     4-228. Suspended cymbals offer an array of possible sounds. To explore the cymbal’s sound palette players
     should experiment with using different playing areas on the cymbal. A general rule is that the closer to the
     edge that the cymbal is struck that lower partials predominate, and rhythmic clarity is reduced.

     4-229. Striking the cymbal in the bell area gives a high, piercing “ping” sound. There is a lack of resonance,
     and the higher overtones predominate, but this area provides the sharpest rhythmic definition. Striking the
     cymbal in the ride area provides the best combination of high and low overtones. It is the best area for general
     rhythmic definition while still achieving a characteristic cymbal sound. Striking the edge of the cymbal
     provides a predominance of low overtones and the greatest resonance. It is the best area for sustained legato
     notes and rolls (see figure 4-21).

Playing Technique

     4-230. Except for special effects, single strokes are used for playing everything including rolls. Articulated
     rhythms played in the ride area are performed with technique similar to snare drum or drum set playing. For
     longer single notes, use a glancing strike near the edge of the cymbal to activate more of the cymbal.

     4-231. Play rolls with single strokes near the edge of the cymbal to acquire a smooth, even, unarticulated
     sound. Position the mallets on opposite sides of the cymbal (approximately the 9 and 3 positions of a clock
     face) to keep the cymbal flat while playing rolls. Priming a roll is not necessary unless the cymbal is slow to
     respond because of its size or thickness. The same principles of rolling on the bass drum apply to rolling on
     suspended cymbals. The roll speed will generally be slower than expected. Louder dynamics do not
     necessarily require faster hand speed. Roll in phase with the vibrations of the cymbal at each dynamic level
     listening for an even, smooth, continuous tone (see figure 4-22).

Implement Selection

     4-232. Almost any striking implement will work on suspended cymbal, and unless specified, selection is
     determined by the performer. With a little experimentation, many interesting and imaginative sounds can be
     produced on suspended cymbal. The prime concern should be musical taste while preventing damage to the
     cymbal.

     4-233. The commonly preferred mallet for suspended cymbal is a medium hardness yarn-covered mallet.
     This provides some degree of cushion to the attack while giving some heft to single notes and works for most
     rolls. If a softer (pp) attack to sound beyond the front of the stage is desired, use a slightly harder mallet than
     normal. This gives some edge to the note and allows the player to play it closer to the edge of the cymbal.

     4-234. Soft yarn mallets produce a rounder sound with a loss of articulation. Heavier weight soft yarn-mallets
     may be substituted for playing rolls. When timpani mallets are called for in a part, yarn mallets are usually
     substituted. This provides a softer attack while avoiding damage to the felt covering on timpani mallets.

     4-235. Snare drum sticks provide a faster response and a more rhythmically distinct attack than yarn mallets.
     They are generally used for crashes that require a sharp attack (e.g., short staccato notes), or faster rhythmic
     passages that require clear articulation. Depending on the volume, wooden sticks will provide the clarity
     needed for most situations. If more clarity is required, metal or nylon-tipped sticks provide crystal clear
     articulation that will cut through the ensemble texture.

     muffling.

MAINTENANCE

     4-239. There is very little maintenance required for cymbals. The primary concerns will be to ensure that the
     cymbals are not damaged (e.g., cracked, dented, bent), and that the hand straps are properly maintained for
     the use of hand cymbals. For strap maintenance and replacement follow the steps in paragraph 3-165 and
     figure 3-29.

     4-240. The steps for periodic cymbal maintenance are:
          • Check for cracked or bent cymbals.
          • Check leather straps for wear or damage by untying the cymbal knot and checking contact points
                that rub against the cymbal hole.
          • Check the holes of suspended cymbals for keying or damage from metal-on-metal contact from
                cymbal stands.
          • Check the post protector and cymbal felts on each cymbal stand for damage.
          • Ensure that cymbal felts allow the cymbal to remain within the length of the plastic post protector.

     4-241. The accessory percussion instruments provide the largest variety of percussive timbres in symphonic

     accurate performance. This section is not all encompassing but rather provides a brief description and general
     playing considerations for the most common accessory instruments used in the concert band percussion

     4-243. Jingles are mounted in slot openings of the frame with steel pins. When choosing a tambourine, a
     player must decide what type and how many jingles are required for the music. Generally, the more jingle
     sound required, the more jingles the tambourine should have. Tambourines are available with one or two

     4-244. Jingles come in a variety of materials and are described by their manufacturers in terms related to
     pitch (e.g., high, medium, low) and sustain (e.g., long, medium, short). Tambourines may be equipped with
     one jingle material or a combination of two or more varying materials. The four common metal types used
     for jingles are:

          • Silver jingles (referred to as German Silver) – are cupped, smooth or hammered, and generally
                nickel-plated disks. They are used for the liveliest and highest pitched jingle sound with long sustain.

          • Copper jingles (referred to as Beryllium Copper) – are cupped, smooth or hammered disks. They
                provide a medium to low pitched jingle sound with medium to long sustain.

          • Brass jingles – are cupped, smooth or hammered disks. They provide medium pitched jingle sound
                with medium sustain.

          • Bronze jingles (referred to as Phosphor Bronze) – are cupped, smooth or hammered disks. They
                provide the lowest pitch jingle sound with long sustain.

     4-245. Heads on professional quality tambourines are traditionally made of calf skin. Natural skin heads are
     secured to the shell of the tambourine by glue, tacks, or a combination of the two. This decreases the weight
     of the instrument and makes the instrument easier to control.

     4-246. Weather and climate can greatly affect natural skin heads. In dry climates with low relative humidity
     heads will dry out and become very tight. This may cause the shell of the tambourine to warp and increases
     the chance of the head breaking while playing. In climates with high humidity or damp weather, heads will
     absorb the moisture in the air and become loose. This also increases the chances of the head breaking while
     playing.

     4-247. There are several methods to address the tension of natural skin heads. For drier climates players may
     dampen the head with water to loosen the tension. When applying moisture to a dry head, avoid completely
     saturating the head as it may loosen the head too quickly.

     4-248. For climates with higher humidity players may use a hair dryer or a heating pad to tighten the head.
     If using a hair dryer, avoid prolonged direct exposure to any single area of the head to prevent damage or
     discoloration. For prolonged dehumidifying simply leave the tambourine on a heating pad.

     4-249. For consistency, modern manufacturers have begun adding synthetic (i.e., plastic) heads to concert
     tambourines. This provides players with the traditional tone and response of natural skin with consistent head
     tension. The overwhelming advantage to these synthetic heads is their immunity to weather changes and the
     corresponding maintenance required.

     4-250. Muffling is rarely necessary. To reduce the amount of head sound, strips of moleskin may be added
     to the underside of the head. To stop the jingles from sounding, turn the tambourine to a 45-degree angle or
     flat (i.e., parallel) to the ground.

     4-251. Headless tambourines are preferred for commercial music styles due to their increased projection and
     difference in playing techniques.

     4-255. Personal preference will determine which hand is used to hold the tambourine. Most players hold the

Rolls

     4-275. The two primary types of tambourine rolls are shake rolls and friction rolls (e.g., thumb and finger
     rolls). The goal for both types of rolls is to create a sustained sound rather than a rhythm.

Shake Rolls

     4-276. Shake rolls are often used for louder dynamics during long sustained rolls and can be performed in
     several ways. To start, hold the tambourine vertically and twist the wrist back and forth (e.g., as if turning a
     doorknob) in an even, controlled manner. The speed of the wrist rotation will determine the sustain of the
     roll. Start slowly to learn the feel of the motion. As the speed increases the sound will shift from a rhythm to
     a sustained roll (resulting in a “shimmer” or “sizzle” sound). A second method is to hold the tambourine
     vertically and produce the back-and-forth (i.e., left to right) motion from the elbow.

     4-277. Many players use a combination of the two methods. To start, begin with a wrist rotation and slowly
     morph into the elbow motion. During the transition, there will a “sweet spot” where the player will be using
     a combination of the wrist and elbow motions. Experimentation is key, and each player will find the best
     method for their body mechanics. The most important thing to remember is to keep the grip relaxed to avoid
     unnecessary tension in the hand and forearm.

     4-278. Crescendos can be achieved by starting the roll with the hand below waist level, next to the leg. As
     the volume increases, simply raise the tambourine up to the normal playing height. For a decrescendo reverse
     the movement, starting at the normal playing height and lowering the tambourine as the dynamic decreases.

     4-279. A general rule for shake rolls is that there should be clear articulation when starting and stopping rolls.
     This can be achieved by striking the tambourine to start and release the roll. When releasing the roll, strike
     the tambourine while simultaneously tilting the tambourine at a 45-degree angle to completely stop jingle
     movement. Be aware of the balance between the articulation of the roll and the actual roll. Avoid accenting
     the start or stop of the roll unless dictated by dynamic or articulation marking. The goal is to have a defined
     roll that fulfills the musical requirements.

Friction Rolls

     4-280. Thumb and finger rolls are often used for softer dynamics and shorter rolls. Whether using the thumb
     or finger, this technique involves creating friction against the head to engage the jingles. Friction against the
     head can be aided in many ways by modifying the head. Players may apply beeswax, surf wax, or string bass
     rosin. Several tambourine manufacturers produce “roll rings,” a thin curved strip of emery paper (i.e., fine
     grit sandpaper) with adhesive backing that adheres to the head. The method of modification will be
     determined by the individual player’s preference or section preference when sharing instruments. These
     modifications are applied in a one-inch band around the outside of the head (see figure 4-33).

     4-287. Commercial music tambourine is designed to reinforce the drummer’s cymbal pattern, help keep the

TRIANGLE

     4-290. Triangles are made of high-tempered carbonized steel, bronze, or an alloy of several metals bent in
     the shape of a triangle with an opening in one corner. The triangle is suspended by a string or mounting chord
     that is attached to a clasp (clip) held in the hand or mounted on a stand and struck with metal beaters.

     4-291. The objective is to produce as many overtones as possible. The sound should be a shimmer of
     overtones rather than a definite pitch. The tone should not sound like an alarm bell or dinner call unless
     intended. Triangles, because of the variety of overtones, project tone surprisingly well. It is not unusual to
     have a triangle sound too soft to the player and too loud to the audience. Performers are encouraged to
     experiment with triangles to discover their musical personalities.

     4-292. Triangle tone depends on the size, diameter, and vibrating qualities of its metallic composition. An
     assortment of triangle sizes and types should be available for the different musical effects required (see Table
     4-4):

GONG/TAM-TAM

     4-313. The gong and tam-tam are often confused for one another. Gongs are instruments tuned to specific
     pitches, while tam-tams are instruments of indefinite pitch that produce a spread of unrelated overtones. In
     Western music composers and manufacturers often use the terms “gong” and “tam-tam” interchangeably,
     which adds further confusion for percussionists. This publication will use the term “gong” to refer to both
     instruments as this has become common usage. Regardless of the term used, unless the music has specific
     pitches notated, percussionists will generally use an unpitched tam-tam.

     4-314. The gong produces a spreading tone of unrelated overtones. It is used for loud, thunderous, and
     climactic passages. Gong sizes range from 12 to 40 inches. Larger instruments have a lower fundamental
     pitch and slower response (see figure 4-44).

GENERAL RULES FOR PERFORMANCE

     4-315. The general rules for performance on the gong include careful consideration of implement selection,
     playing areas, stroke technique, and muffling.

Implement Selection

     4-316. Because of the instrument’s size, the player must use a mallet that is large and heavy enough to
     produce maximum tone from the instrument. Standard mallets have long metal or wooden handles, with
     heavy plastic, rubber, or metal disks that are covered in a variety of materials (e.g., felt, yarn, lamb’s wool).
     A single mallet is used for the majority of all gong parts. The most important thing to remember is to choose
     a mallet with enough mass to make the entire instrument vibrate at any dynamic level.

     4-317. When rolls are notated, the use of two mallets (e.g., a matched pair in shape, size, and weight) is
     recommended to achieve a consistent and balanced sound. For general-purpose rolls and softer dynamics,
     smaller mallets may be used. Smaller mallets have heads that are smaller in diameter, lighter, and allow for
     ease of control. Regardless of dynamic, the use of two matched mallets for performing rolls will produce a
     consistent and balanced sound.

     4-318. Composers call for several special effects on gong which require the use of non-traditional striking
     implements. Bass drum beaters produce lighter and softer notes. Drumsticks, triangle beaters, and brushes
     allow for articulated rhythmic passages. The “scrape” effect is produced by rubbing a triangle beater or coin
     across the face of the instrument (e.g., circular motion), or along the rim or edge (i.e., perimeter) of the
     instrument. A high, whining effect can be produced by drawing a string bass bow across the edge of the gong.
     Produce a haunted house “wailing” effect by rubbing a rubber mallet across the surface of the instrument. A
     low suspended cymbal roll effect or a high, light, delicate, small effect can be produced with yarn mallets.

Playing Areas

     4-319. To choose a playing area, the player must know which overtones (high or low) are predominant, the
     response needed for the attack, and the required duration of the note. The center produces a dull, dead attack
     on impact. A widely spreading tone follows with the lower overtones predominating. This is the slowest
     response area and is used when more attack and punch are required.

             Note. Striking the exact center of the gong with extreme force or velocity can cause the instrument
             to crack.

     4-320. Striking the gong off-center produces the best combination of high and low overtones, the greatest
     tonal duration, and the widest dynamic range. The distance off-center is relative to the size of the gong. This
     playing spot is used for general-purpose playing.

     4-321. Striking the gong at the edge produces the highest overtones. The tone is shallow and of relatively
     short duration. Response is slightly slower than when striking the gong off-center.

     4-322. The characteristic sound of the gong is an immediate decrescendo followed by a quick crescendo. If
     a faster attack is required, the gong must be primed.

     4-323. Priming the gong means striking the gong softly anywhere on its surface to start it vibrating for a
     faster response to the actual attack with a mallet. This can be achieved by lightly tapping the instrument with
     the fingers or mallet, before playing the actual attack. In deciding whether to prime the gong, the player
     should consider the musical function of the part and the interaction of the instrument with other instruments
     (e.g., is the gong sound and attack part of the entrance of the other instruments or is it in response to another
     instrument’s entrance?).

     4-324. The strokes used on the gong should always be straight-in single strokes. Rolls may be performed like
     suspended cymbal rolls to provide better control. Roll in phase to produce an even, smooth, and non-pulsating

     Muffling the edge lightly stops the upper overtones while allowing the low overtones to ring.

     4-328. Grabbing the edge of the gong with the hand or rubbing the mallet head across the gong can produce

WOODBLOCK

     4-330. The woodblock used in concert band is usually made from a solid rectangular block of wood.
     Woodblocks used for world percussion are usually made of plastic. There are two common types used in
     traditional symphonic music:

          • Double slotted woodblocks have ¼ inch wide slots on the two long sides that act as a resonating
                chamber.

          • Single slotted woodblocks have a single ¼ inch slot along one side of the block that acts as a
                resonating chamber.

     4-331. The tone produced is a penetrating, non-pitched, non-sustaining “tick” or “tock” sound. The block
     size, placement of the slotting, and density of the wood determine the resonance and pitch of the tone.

     4-332. Woodblocks are 6 to 8 inches in length, 2½ to 3 inches wide, and 1½ to 2½ inches deep. There should
     be at least two sizes available to produce the required sounds: large for low tones, and small for high tones
     (see figure 4-45).

Implement Selection

     4-334. Do not use extremely hard synthetic plastic xylophone or bell mallets, or any other implement that
     will damage the instrument.

     4-335. Implement selection will be determined by the desired sound needed. Common choices include:
          • Using the tip or bead of a drumstick produces a high and hollow “tick” sound, reminiscent of the
                sound of tap dancing. The shoulder or butt of the stick produces a lower, more resonant sound (i.e.,
                akin to the sound of horse hoofs).
          • Medium-hard rubber mallets produce the fullest and most resonant sound on woodblocks. Be careful
                not to strike too aggressively; these mallets can dent or possibly crack the woodblock.
          • Medium rubber mallets are suitable for softer, rounder sounding notes where sharp percussive
                attacks are not needed.
          • Softer mallets than described will not project through an ensemble.

Playing Areas

     4-336. The playing area for each woodblock encountered will be different. Whether using a double or single
     slotted woodblock, playing in the center or just off center (when viewed from above) produces a resonant
     tone. Playing on the edge over the tone slot will produce a lower and less resonant tone. Do not strike the
     edge aggressively; it will crack easily. The edge opposite the tone slots and the sides of the woodblock are
     nodal areas and sound dead. Experimentation is key to finding the best sounding playing area.

Playing Technique

     4-337. If desired, hold the woodblock in the palm of a cupped hand (e.g., between the fingers and pad beneath
     the thumb). Hold it at chest or eye level for easy rhythmic passages where only one mallet is needed. For
     rhythmically dense passages requiring two mallets, place the woodblock on a trap table that is padded with a
     towel or piece of foam.

TEMPLE BLOCKS

     4-338. Temple blocks can be individual blocks but usually come in sets of five (see figure 4-46). The tone
     produced is much like that of a woodblock but lower, mellower, and less penetrating. Traditionally they are
     carved from wood and are relatively hollow inside. Modern manufacturers produce plastic temple blocks for
     durability and consistency. They tend to emit a definite pitch but will blend harmonically because of their
     short sound decay. Choose a set of temple blocks that produces evenly spaced tones. They need not be tuned
     to a pentatonic scale or specific pitches, although most commercially available temple block sets approximate
     a pentatonic scale tuning.

     4-339. There are two types of commercially available temple block set-ups:
          • Straight-row blocks are set up from high to low in a straight line.
          • Double-tiered blocks are set up in two tiers with the two lowest notes on top, and the three highest
                notes on the bottom. This makes sight-reading difficult but makes certain sticking situations easier.
                Another version of the double-tiered setup has the three highest notes on top and the two lowest
                notes on the bottom.

Machine-Mounted Castanets

     4-345. Machine mounted castanets are spring-mounted in pairs on a wooden base that acts as a sound

Handle Castanets

     4-346. Handle castanets are mounted in pairs on wooden handles via string or cord and played as a set. Most

                castanets into the leg after the stroke. This is effective for general-purpose playing.

          • For softer dynamics (pp to mf) and greater articulation control, place a forefinger on the top castanet
                clapper while striking the leg.

SLAPSTICK

     4-358. A slapstick is a pair of boards held together by a hinge. Larger ones have handles on each board;
     smaller ones have a spring hinge to help the striking board return to its position (see figure 4-52). If no
     commercial slapstick is available, construct one from hardwood boards about two feet long and four to five

     length from the hinges.

     4-359. If using a spring hinged slapstick, it is normally played by a slapping wrist motion (e.g., like cracking
     a whip), causing a loud crack. If using a handled slapstick, it is played by hitting the boards together.

     determined by how fast the crank is turned.

     can be suspended for better resonance or laid out on a padded table. When an anvil is not available, this is
     sometimes an acceptable substitute.

     is bent (i.e., flexed) the pitch will rise or fall accordingly. It is used for glissando and microtonal sliding sound
     effects (see figure 4-54).
