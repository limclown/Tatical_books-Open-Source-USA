                                 Preface

TC 7-0.2, Training Meetings provides Army leaders the fundamental processes and procedures to plan and
conduct training meetings. Users of this TC must familiarize themselves with Army training doctrine as
described in ADP 7-0 and FM 7-0.

The principal audience for TC 7-0.2 is all members of the profession of arms. Commanders and staffs of
Army headquarters serving as joint task force or multinational headquarters must also refer to applicable joint
or multinational doctrine concerning the range of military operations and joint or multinational forces.
Trainers and educators throughout the Army also use this publication.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure that their decisions and actions comply with applicable United
States, international, and, in some cases, host-nation laws and regulations. Commanders at all levels ensure
that their Soldiers operate in accordance with the law of war and the rules of engagement. (See FM 6-27).

TC 7-0.2 uses joint terms where applicable. Selected joint and Army terms and definitions appear in both the
glossary and the text. For definitions shown in the text, the term is italicized, and the number of the proponent
publication follows the definition.

TC 7-0.2 applies to the Active Army, Army National Guard/Army National Guard of the United States and
United States Army Reserve unless otherwise stated.

                              Introduction

TC 7-0.2, Training Meetings, expands on the fundamental concepts of conducting training meetings,
primarily at the company level, but techniques and concepts that can be applied at multiple echelons. It
reflects training management as described in ADP 7-0 and FM 7-0. TC 7-0.2 guides leaders to develop
necessary techniques to conduct effective training meetings, and tools that assist in planning and coordinating
training events. Leaders are encouraged to expand, modify and develop their own techniques as required.
This publication applies to all Army leaders and the three components of the Total Army: the Regular Army,
the Army National Guard, and the Army Reserve.

TC 7-0.2 contains two chapters:

Chapter 1. Training Meeting Fundamentals discusses the purpose of training meetings and establishes three
primary goals executed in three phases, in the process of conducting training meetings and event planning. It
introduces the T-Week concept, which assists leaders by providing a methodological timeline to discuss event
planning milestones.

Chapter 2. Training Meeting Battle Rhythm outlines specific techniques and agenda recommendations by
phase. It describes participant roles and details each phase with techniques to assist in discussing training.

TC 7-0.2 contains three appendices:

Appendix A. Platoon Training Meetings provides platoon leaders with details on conducting effective
training meetings at the platoon level.

Appendix B. Battalion and Brigade Training Meetings briefly describes participants and recommended
agenda items.

Appendix C. T-Week and Pre-Executions checks is a detailed example of planning considerations from the
development of the unit training plan through event execution and AAR.

                                                 Chapter 1

                   Training Meeting Fundamentals

OVERVIEW

     1-1. Preparation is crucial for effective mission-focused training. Training meetings are vital as they review
     past training, plan future events, synchronize resources and allow commanders to issue and adjust training
     guidance, keeping the unit training plan on course. These meetings also ensure smooth communication and
     coordination among unit leaders at all echelons. For additional techniques, you can check out the training
     meeting how-to video available on the Army Training Network (ATN) https://atn.army.mil/unit-training-
     management-(utm)#TrainingMeeting.

             Note: Although this training circular focuses on the company level and below, training meetings
             held at the battalion and brigade are no less important and are equally vital to ensuring unit training
             proficiency. Training meetings at all echelons apply the basic ideas discussed in this TC.

PURPOSE OF TRAINING MEETINGS

     1-2. The training management cycle is a continuous cycle of planning, executing and assessing and begins
     when commanders issue their Annual Training Guidance (ATG). An important aspect of the cycle is the
     continuous use of feedback to refine the training plan. This feedback takes many forms including personal
     observation, AARs, and informal evaluations. Subordinates provide their bottom-up feedback when they
     evaluate the training proficiency needs of the unit and individual Soldiers. Leaders provide top-down
     feedback when they conduct training meetings. The agenda of a training meeting includes reviewing past
     training, identifying and planning necessary retraining, planning and preparing future training, and
     exchanging timely training information between leaders. The training meeting is the best forum for discussing
     training assessments and unit, leader, and task proficiencies on a regular basis.

     1-3. Company, troop, and battery training meetings are the center of gravity of unit training management.
     Regular Army (RA) and Reserve Component (RC) units serving on active duty conduct training meetings
     weekly.

     1-4. RC units conduct training meetings monthly, normally during drill weekends. They focus on the next
     30, 60, and 90 days and identify required facilities to support training events a year in advance. At battalion
     and higher RC echelons, if staff and training meetings are combined, there should be a clear separation of the
     two meetings. The training dates are locked 90 days out to ensure RC soldiers can coordinate with their
     employer.

     1-5. At battalion and brigade levels, training meetings focus on overall ATG progress and ensuring training
     resources are forecasted, scheduled, and coordinated in time with supporting installation agencies and
     available when training begins.

             Note: For all echelons and components, the goal is to complete the meeting in one to one and a
             half hours and should only focus on training.

     1-6. Establishing a unit training battle rhythm is crucial for maintaining an effective and cohesive training
     schedule. Leaders alternate training events with training meetings. After a unit executes tasks during a
     training event, the leader assesses the unit’s task performance and determines if the unit met the established
     training objectives for the event. Commanders use the unit’s training battle rhythm to integrate and
     synchronize training activities, meetings, briefings, conferences, and reports. Establishing a training battle
     rhythm helps sequence and formalize these activities to regulate the flow and sharing of training information
     across the command. This structured approach ensures that training is continuous, coordinated, and effective.
     See figure 1-1.

TRAINING MEETING PHASES

     1-8. With these objectives in mind, training meetings have three primary goals executed in three phases:

Phase I Assessment

     1-9. Review completed training. Leaders discuss lessons learned from recent events and the achievement of
     training objectives. Discussions focus on the effectiveness of planning and preparation, resource coordination
     and availability, task execution to include evaluation and assessment. Leaders also determine if the training
     objectives were met, and if not, why. See figure 1-2 for an example of an event training objective. This
     includes identifying requirements for retraining tasks not performed to standard and outlining a plan to
     retrain. Additionally, discuss any scheduled training not performed and the reasons why. This portion ends
     with an overview of training proficiency. The commander updates the unit’s mission essential tasks list
     (METL) and battle tasks assessments as required.

Phase II Coordination

     1-10. The focus is on detailed resource and leader coordination for the next training events scheduled (T-5
     to T-1). Update and confirm T-7 and T-6 draft training schedules. Each training event is discussed in detail,
     re-confirming the training objectives, ensuring every aspect of planning and preparation is synchronized. Of
     particular interest are the resources needed to train, to include all classes of supply, training areas, convoy
     clearances and all administrative requirements. During this discussion, leaders provide updates on the status
     of the training event planning and resource coordination, and any anticipated shortfalls and how these are
     mitigated. The units T-Week format for each event is critical to ensuring future training events occur without
     delay due to a lack of resourcing and planning. The commander reviews major events scheduled from the
     publication of the UTP to T-8.

Phase III Event Planning

     1-11. After publishing the UTP, and each time an event is identified and placed on the calendar, the
     commander builds a training objective for each event. Training objectives describe the specific tasks trained
     during the event, the commander’s intent and anticipated proficiency at the end of the training. See figure 1-
     2. At the company level and below, the 8-step training model offers a flexible simple checklist to ensure

PARTICIPANTS

     1-15. Leader participation is essential for a successful company training meeting. The commander determines
     the participants to attend. Paragraphs 1-16 through 1-24 list participants who should routinely attend company
     training meetings and their associated responsibilities.

COMPANY COMMANDER

     1-16. The commander facilitates the training meeting and ensures the meeting agenda is followed. Prior to
     the meeting, the commander updates the assessment of the company’s training proficiency and progress in
     meeting UTP training objectives. The commander reviews the assessment of the unit METs, reviews the
     company long-range training calendar, upcoming training schedules, and upcoming training event operations
     orders. The commander then provides guidance for upcoming events and adjusts the mission focus for future
     training events based on this review. Next, the commander confirms the status of resource requests and their
     requirements. The commander updates the training estimate—to include MET assessments, training risk
     status of external resources, and status of trainers—and specifies the individual Soldier tasks for hip-pocket
     training with advice from the company first sergeant.

EXECUTIVE OFFICER

     1-17. In the absence of the commander, the executive officer runs the training meeting. This officer also
     coordinates training for all the Soldiers in sections or attachments without platoon leaders or platoon
     sergeants. The executive officer reviews the current assessment of the unit METs in dialogue with the
     company commander. The executive officer provides the status of resources to support upcoming events and
     identifies resource issues affecting upcoming training. Additionally, the executive officer identifies and
     consolidates new resource requirements based on adjusted collective tasks to train for upcoming events.

FIRST SERGEANT

     1-18. The first sergeant is the senior enlisted advisor to the commander on all issues that affect not only
     individual training but also collective training. The first sergeant reports on individual tasks progress as well
     as on the status of training warrior tasks and battle drills. The first sergeant provides guidance and advice on
     training plans and reviews pre-execution checks discussed during the training meeting. Additionally, the first
     sergeant actively participates in the development of training plans for platoons (battle tasks and identification

PLATOON LEADER

     1-19. Based on training conducted, platoon leaders brief their evaluations of battle tasks. They also brief on

PLATOON SERGEANT

     1-20. Platoon sergeants provide their evaluation of section- or crew-level and individual tasks based on
     training conducted. They observe and receive feedback from squad leaders on the status of individual training
     conducted and support the company’s METs. Platoon sergeants brief the status of specific pre-execution
     checks—including the status of trainers, resources, tasks to train, or site reconnaissance—for upcoming
     events. Additionally, platoon sergeants provide input to any changes—such as battle tasks, warrior tasks,
     battle drills, and resource requirements for upcoming events.

SUPPORTING STAFF NONCOMMISSIONED OFFICER

     1-21. Other key staff NCOs attend training meetings and advise the commander on the status of specialized
     training. For example, the battalion master gunner works with the company first sergeant to track individual
     and crew-served weapons qualifications and helps leaders with gunnery qualification requirements. Other
     key battalion or brigade NCOs identify and advise company leaders on opportunities to integrate specialized
     training into upcoming events. Additionally, they advise leaders on specialized resource requirements as
     needed.

SUPPORTING MAINTENANCE PERSONNEL

     1-22. Supporting maintenance personnel coordinate the maintenance efforts of the company and work with
     the commander and executive officer to ensure timely support is provided. Supporting maintenance personnel
     provide input on the status of maintenance training in the company, recommend maintenance-related training,
     and inform the commander of scheduled services and inspections that may impact training. Additionally,
     they may advise the commander on maintenance training requirements based on an evaluation of training
     previously conducted.

SUPPLY SERGEANT

     1-23. Supply sergeants provide input to the commander on supply-related issues, inspections, and
     inventories. Supply sergeants also work with the executive officer and first sergeant to coordinate necessary
     support from outside sources. They assist the executive officer and first sergeant in identifying, coordinating,
     and resourcing logistic support requirements—internal and external—for future training events.

ATTACHED AND OTHER SUPPORT LEADERS

     1-24. Other leaders attend training meetings to coordinate their training efforts with those of the company.
     For example, the leader of an attached target acquisition radar section may attend the meeting. Habitual
     attachments, such as medics or fire support personnel should also attend. These leaders provide their
     evaluation of tasks and battle drills previously trained. They confirm the tasks to train and the status of
     resource requirements for upcoming events. Commanders fully integrate and support the training of all
     habitually associated units. RC companies may have personnel associated with the RA attend training
     meetings. These personnel bring valuable experiences and the latest training techniques from RA units.

                                      Chapter 2

              Training Meetings Battle Rhythm

OVERVIEW

2-1. Training meetings have a set battle rhythm and routine. They occur at the same time and place each
week and follow a set agenda. Company training meetings include updating company METs or battle tasks
and coordinating training. Lastly, training meetings include a review of pre-execution checks as well as a
review of plans for future training.

SCHEDULING TRAINING MEETINGS

2-2. Generally, leaders conduct training meetings on the same day and time each week and keep them as a
recurring entry on the unit training schedule. This regularity provides a degree of battle rhythm in training,
consistency, and predictability. As necessary, leaders can conduct training meetings using collaborative
electronic means, like a scheduled meeting via Army 365 resources.

2-3. Leaders consider several factors before selecting a time to     conduct  training  meetings.  Main
considerations include—

     • Enabling leaders to attend.
     • Minimizing training disruptions.
     • Allowing subordinate leaders time to prepare.
     • Local policy.
     • Making it a routine inclusion on the unit training schedule.

2-4. RC company commanders encounter more challenges when scheduling training meetings. They have
three alternatives:

     • Conduct the meeting during a regularly scheduled drill period.
     • Conduct the meeting during an additional training assembly.
     • Conduct the training meeting during a “for points only” or non-paid assembly.

2-5. The agenda is keyed to the T-Week concept (see a detailed discussion in appendix A). There are three
phases to company training meetings. The first is used to assess previous training. The second is used to
coordinate upcoming events (weeks T-5 through T-1 then update and confirm T-7 and T-6). Also, conduct a
review of the major milestones included from the beginning of the UTP to T-8. The final phase is used to
plan training for future training events. The agenda maintains a focus for all to see, understand, and follow.

Phase I: Review Last Week’s Training

     2-9. In the first goal, commanders begin the meeting by discussing the training week just completed. The
     feedback input by the platoon leaders and platoon sergeants is critical to assessing METs, leader task, and
     individual task proficiencies of the unit. Company leaders discuss the agenda items listed in paragraphs 2-10
     through 2-13. Commanders then ensure the assessments are recorded.

Platoon Evaluation

     2-10. Platoon leaders and platoon sergeants evaluate tasks and other collective task proficiencies as well as
     Soldier proficiency since the last training meeting. The sources of the platoon evaluation may be formal or
     informal and based on the task T&EO and other forms of feedback. A platoon evaluation includes—

          • Collective tasks, battle tasks, leader tasks, Soldier tasks, warrior tasks, and battle drills.
          • The proficiency rating—fully trained (T), practiced (P), and untrained (U)—of all training

                conducted by the platoon assessments from T&EOs (also GO and NO-GO results from the
                performance steps, as necessary and individual tasks trained).

Training Shortfalls

     2-11. As each platoon completes task evaluations, leaders address training shortfalls. A training shortfall
     occurs when a unit plans training but does not conduct it. Platoon leaders explain to the commander the
     reasons for not executing training and the plans to retrain the tasks missed. A training shortfall also occurs
     when a unit fails to meet training objectives. If a unit fails to meet objectives, it retrains on those tasks until
     it earns a fully trained (T) or practiced (P) proficiency rating as indicated by the training objective.
     Commanders record training shortfalls that include—

          • Training planned but not conducted (including discussion of tasks not trained).
          • The reason for not executing training.
          • A retraining plan, if needed.

     2-12. Completed T&EOs may reveal training that is incomplete or not conducted to the Army standard.
     Leaders conduct retraining at the first opportunity, ideally during the same training period. However, when
     this cannot happen, leaders may need to adjust subsequent training events (adding or subtracting tasks to
     train) to retrain on those tasks on which the unit did not meet the standard. The commander then adjusts the
     UTP for future events.

     2-14. Once subordinates provide their evaluations, the commander and first sergeant provide their input to

PHASE II: COORDINATION

Short-Range Training

     2-16. Coordination for training (weeks T-5 through T-1) requires a review of command guidance and pre-
     execution checks.

Review of Training Guidance

     2-17. During a review of training guidance, the unit reviews FRAGORDs or any other new or updated
     guidance that impacts training (weeks T-6 through T-1). Commanders convey the purpose of the training and
     the desired end state, but exercising command and control, they leave the how to achieve results to
     subordinates. This latitude allows subordinates to use their critical and creative thinking to support the
     commander’s guidance and vision. Additionally, leaders understand and assess the impact of this command
     guidance on the current training plan, discussing this impact and its associated risks.

     2-18. Leaders discuss any adjustments to the current training plan to clearly understand the impact on the
     commander’s end state for upcoming training. Once leaders understand the mission focus for upcoming
     events, the company leadership reviews key aspects of the pre-execution checks for these events.

Pre-execution Checks

     2-19. Pre-execution checks are procedures, usually using checklists, employed to ensure all planning and
     prerequisite training (collective, Soldier, and leader) has been conducted prior to the execution of training.
     These are not precombat checks. A critical part of the training meeting is the discussion of pre-execution

           T-Week Concept and Pre-execution Checks

PURPOSE

     C-1. The T-Week concept is a backward-planning technique used to project critical actions and activities
     (pre-execution checks) when planning and preparing training events. Using ‘T-week’ as the week of
     execution and working backward to today (for example, if the unit is eight (8) weeks from conducting a
     training event), today would be referred to as ‘T-8’ (T minus eight), etc. Each week before the execution of
     training requires planners to identify and coordinate all necessary actions and resources carefully. These may
     include coordinating training areas, publishing OPORDs, requisitioning Class V, requesting convoy
     clearances, etc.

     C-2. The degree of difficulty in planning training events varies. For example, preparations for conducting a
     class are significantly simpler than planning an FTX. Complex, multiechelon training events require more
     time to plan, resource, and coordinate.

             Note: Reserve component units should aggregate activities based on their monthly schedule and
             published UTP

OVERVIEW

     C-3. Planners use the T-Week concept as a guide to assist in planning training activities that should occur
     in anticipation of each event. Each T-Week has an associated series of training activities that help guide
     planners and advise the commander of the actions the unit must accomplish to execute an effective training
     event. During training meetings, T-weeks provide a common framework of reference when discussing
     actions taken when planning each training event.

     C-4. The anchor point for the T-Week concept is the week training is executed. This is designated as T
     Week. A minus sign (-) indicates the number of weeks prior to a particular training event execution. For
     example, T-5 is five weeks before the training event occurs. A plus sign (+) indicates the number of weeks
     following the event, for example, T+1 is one week after the training event. Units modify the T-Week concept
     to mirror their own local and command training requirements. See table 1-1 on page 2 as it breaks down
     hypothetical major T-Week activities.

     C-5. The rest of this appendix breaks down major T-Week activities by week. Although this appendix
     contains extensive descriptions, they do not account for an individual unit or installation’s particular resource
     requirements for planning and coordination. Additionally, each major training event falls into its own T week
     construct as various points on the long-range, mid-range, and short-range planning horizons.

     counter tasks, and variables that provide the necessary physical, mental, and ethical stressors. The training
     environment provides a generalized representation of potential enemies and projected operational
     environment conditions that challenge unit task execution. For example, when this information is published
     in a WARNORD for a mission rehearsal exercise where an operational environment is known, trainers apply
     known information from the theater of operations to make training more realistic.

TRAINING SUPPORT

     C-27. Training support includes selecting training environments, developing a base tactical scenario, and
     publishing a WARNORD. Once the tasks and the training environment are determined for an event, the
     training support enablers are selected. Training support consists of training Aids, devices, simulations and
     simulators (TADSS), facilities (mission training complexes and ranges), and services (personnel running the
     ranges).

Select Training Environments

     C-28. Collective training events take place in three types of training environments: live, virtual, and
     constructive. Planners consider employing each environment independently (the easiest to plan and prepare)
     or a combination of two or more environments to meet the objective. If using more than one training
     environment, then leaders use either a blended training environment (BTE) or an integrated training
     environment (ITE.) Planners choose the environment based on the environment that best enables the unit to
     accomplish its training objectives in the training time allotted.

     C-29. Just as a leader understands an operational environment in combat, a leader understands the training
     environment for a training event. Leaders and trainers take the initiative, quickly develop partnerships with
     the right people—personnel at the Directorate of Plans, Training, Mobilization and Security; range control,
     and the mission training complex—and familiarize themselves with training capabilities. Subsequently, they
     take subordinate leaders on a terrain walk of those facilities and capabilities.

Develop a Base Tactical Scenario

     C-30. A primary driver to the training event is the development of a tactical scenario. The tactical scenario
     provides a realistic backdrop to training when it properly replicates an operational environment. Focused on
     the training objectives to achieve during the event, a tactical scenario simulates the situations that cause the
     unit and Soldiers to act and react to specific situations, whereby the training objectives (collective tasks) can
     be evaluated and assessed. After determining the OPFOR requirements, develop a basic concept for scripting
     and role-playing that supports the tactical scenario. The creation of the master scenario event list (MSEL)
     will aid in controlling the event to achieve the desired training objectives. All the information gathered and
     developed during weeks T-11 and T-10 inform the commander of the personnel, logistics, and other resource
     requirements to support the performance of the training event.

     C-32. Pre-execution checks are informal checks that units complete to coordinate prior to conducting training

     needed to use resources efficiently.

COMBINED ARMS TRAINING STRATEGY

     C-36. The CATS provides proponent-recommended resources necessary to conduct training. It provides units

WEEK T-6: LOCK IN TRAINING AND PUBLISH SCHEDULES

PRODUCTS

COMPLETED PLAN

     C-54. The plan covers both the friendly force and OPFOR. The plan drives the training to meet the training
     objectives. The week T-5 plan identifies both the training audience and the OPFOR to ensure the staff
     synchronizes and focuses on all training aids. If training involves multiple training support enablers, then the

     complex to ensure data are uploaded into the simulation.

COMPLETED MASTER SCENARIO EVENTS LIST

     C-55. Units develop a master scenario event list (known as MSEL) at week T-10 to develop the training
     scenario and set the conditions for the unit to display proficiency in the tasks evaluated during the training.
     The MSEL drives OPFOR actions that stimulate a friendly force reaction. A MSEL scenario is developed
     chronologically to stimulate friendly force actions from the beginning of the exercise to the end. The MSEL
     also provides stimulus for evaluators to observe and evaluate the selected training objectives.

EXERCISE CONTROL PLAN

     C-56. Developing an exercise control plan helps synchronize and manage the training event. A small-unit
     training event may have a simple graphic with timelines and control measures. A large-scale event may
     include a complex scenario, white cell personnel and equipment, evaluators, and rules of engagement. In
     addition to the scenario, a higher headquarters develops an OPORD to drive the training event.

EVALUATOR PLAN

     C-57. The evaluator plan addresses (by name) the evaluators and the evaluation plan. It outlines how the
     evaluators (by name) are supported with supplies (and from whom) and details the evaluators’ duties. The
     evaluator plan itemizes the evaluator packet contents. For example, the evaluator packet might contain unit
     SOPs, T&EOs, doctrine, pyrotechnics (Class V) on hand, and a Multiple Integrated Laser Engagement
     System (known as MILES) sets. Evaluators read and make themselves familiar with both the friendly force
     and OPFOR plans as well as attend key unit events (such as backbriefs, rehearsals, and precombat checks).

     C-58. Evaluators ensure all systems work properly prior to the training event to ensure accurate outcomes.
     Evaluators familiarize themselves with the training support systems used for the event. Effective evaluators
     conduct workarounds if live or simulated conditions cannot portray the realism necessary to achieve the
     training objectives. As with the leaders, evaluators ensure the unit conducts the training event safely. They
     address unsafe acts immediately.

EVALUATION PLAN

     C-59. The evaluators may be internal or external to a unit. Evaluators have a copy of the collective and
     individual tasks’ T&EOs and are familiar with the scenario to ensure proper evaluation. Upon completion,
     evaluators give evaluation packets to the commander for the commander’s assessment.

     allow. Ideally, leaders train selected tasks in 15 to 30 minutes. Initial individual training or collective task
     training ordinarily requires more time and resources. Effective leaders use their initiative to ensure their
     subordinates are well trained and their time is not wasted.

RETRAIN

     C-83. Leaders allocate sufficient time to retrain tasks during or after training events. Not all tasks will be
     performed to standard on the first or even second attempt. Commanders do not allow an organization to end
     training believing that a substandard performance was acceptable. Therefore, leaders allocate and schedule
     time and other resources for unit retraining of key collective and individual tasks in their training plans.
     Retraining allows participants to implement corrective action. Ideally, units complete retraining at the earliest
     opportunity, if not immediately after they attempt the task. In some cases, a restart of an event may be
     necessary before moving to the next training event.

WEEK T+1: RECOVER, CONDUCT FINAL AFTER ACTION
REVIEW, AND ASSESS TRAINING

     C-84. In week T+1, commanders recover, conduct final AARs, and assess training. Recovery is part of every
     training event. Leaders plan for recovery to ensure all the resources and personnel are available to return to
     full operation. Evaluators and leaders conduct a final AAR of the training event. The final step in a training
     event is to assess the training, specifically the collective and individual task proficiencies demonstrated or
     attained.

RECOVERY

     C-85. Recovery is critical to every training event. Leaders use recovery to ensure the resources and personnel
     return to standard. The recovery process is part of training. Once recovery is complete, it signifies the end of
     the training event. Captured AAR comments reflect the effectiveness of the recovery and modification to the
     SOP. Ideally, a unit performs the following sample recovery activities after a training event:

          • Account for personnel health and welfare.
          • Perform post operations preventative maintenance checks and services.
          • Ensure sensitive item accountability.
          • Ensure accountability of organizational and individual equipment.
          • Ensure that Class IV, Class V, TADSS, and other support items are maintained, accounted for, and

                turned in.
          • Close out training areas and ranges.
          • Conduct AARs.

RECOVERY AND POST OPERATIONS CHECKS

     C-86. Post operations checks are those tasks a unit accomplishes at the conclusion of training. An effective
     unit SOP contains these checks. The checks vary depending on the type of training. For example, an FTX
     requires more extensive post operations checks than garrison-type training. Sample post operations checks
     include the following:

          • Soldier accountability.
          • Sensitive item accountability (such as weapons or communications security).
          • Report closure of unit to higher headquarters.
          • Ammunition and equipment turn in (such as TADSS).
          • Maintenance of vehicles, weapons, and communications including the following:

                 Equipment cleaned.
                 Thorough preventative maintenance checks and services after-operations checks completed.
                 Required services performed.
          • Training assessments:
                 Leaders record results of training in leader books.
                 AARs completed.
                 AAR initiated, if appropriate.
          • Soldier recovery.
          • Chain of command inspections of Soldiers and equipment.

FINAL AFTER ACTION REVIEW

     C-87. The final AAR takes place as soon as possible following the event. (Leaders conduct informal AARs
     as needed during the event). This ensures that events are still fresh in the minds of all the participants,
     capturing the data as accurately as possible. OPFOR personnel and evaluators all provide their input to inform
     the commander’s assessments. Using multiple recorders ensures a unit captures all lessons. Sound
     assessments of those lessons facilitate later success. A final AAR begins with a general review of the UTP.
     Leaders discuss if event planning and preparation were sufficient and if the commander’s training objectives
     and intent were met. They also determine if administrative and tactical support was sufficient to conduct the
     training. OPFORs and evaluators record all the lessons learned and preserve them for retraining and future
     use.

TASK ASSESSMENT

     C-88. Commanders assess training event training objectives. The commander considers personal
     observations as well as observations, T&EOs, insights, and lessons from AARs and unit evaluations to inform
     both MET proficiency for the assigned mission and for unit status reporting purposes.

STANDARD OPERATING PROCEDURE REVIEW

     C-89. Leaders conduct an SOP review after the event to implement recommended changes gleaned from the
     observations, insights, and lessons. Quickly implementing the right changes allows the unit to begin
     improving performance sooner.
