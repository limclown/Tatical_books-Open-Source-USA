                                                 Chapter 1

                                 Introduction

    TC 3-20.31-044 provides a structured method for training weapon system operators, master
    gunners, and leaders to utilize firing tables during the training, execution, and recovery of direct
    fire operations. With continuously improving automated ballistic fire control software, weapon
    systems, algorithms, methodologies, and fire control data computing, expertise with hard-copy
    data is becoming exponentially more important. In the event of failures during direct fire
    operations, analog computations must be performed to verify hazard zones and impacts.

    This publication is designed to assist all personnel operating direct fire weapon systems in their
    understanding of ballistics, projectile flight characteristics, the establishment of battlesight
    ranges, and how to accurately determine the location of rounds out of impact. It describes the
    history of ballistic data collection, the reduction of that data into readable data, and how it is
    applied in training and austere environments.

HISTORY

     1-1. It is important to know the history of firing tables to understand their significance in modern-day ballistic
     study. As ballistics and attributes of our weapon systems, ammunition, and capabilities evolve, knowing why
     those changes occurred and how to apply the lessons learned is paramount.

WORLD WAR I

     1-2. The development of firing tables in the U.S. Army began during World War I, when ballistics tests were
     conducted, including computations of firing tables. Rapid demand for ballistic data led the Army's Chief of
     Ordnance to establish the Ballistics Branch on April 6, 1918. Firing tables enabled gunners to manually
     calculate aiming solutions, such as elevation (EL), azimuth, and fuse settings to engage targets under various
     conditions. Known as tabular firing tables (TFT), these products were developed by the Ballistics Branch
     through trajectory analysis and experiments to improve weapon system range and accuracy.

     1-3. The Ballistics Branch was tasked with preparing accurate TFT and evolving the quickly aging targeting
     systems of World War I by conducting mathematical analysis of trajectories and experimenting with concepts
     designed to increase the range and accuracy of weapon systems.

     1-4. As firing table development began during World War I, Congress moved ordnance testing facilities from
     Sandy Hook, NJ, to Aberdeen, MD, due to range limitations. The transition started in late 1917, and the first
     test round was fired at Aberdeen Proving Ground on January 2, 1918. Nine divisions were eventually
     established at the new proving ground, comprising the proof department. Ballistics work was primarily
     assigned to three sections: range firing, development, and instrument. The range firing section prepared all
     firing tables, performed mathematical analyses of ballistic problems, and conducted experiments designed to
     increase projectile range and accuracy. The organization of the proof department remained unchanged until
     July 1922.

     1-5. In 1925 after the post-World War I personnel reduction, the mathematical unit of the Ballistic Section,
     Gun Testing Division, and Proof Department prepared the ballistic and firing tables. By 1935, the first major
     reorganization incorporated the withdrawal of the Ballistic Section from the Gun Testing Division and
     established as the Research Division. The new research division of 30 people was organized into six sections,
     of which two were involved in the development of firing and bombing tables. The exterior ballistics section
     studied trajectories and flight characteristics of projectiles and bombs. The computing section prepared the
     firing and bombing tables for standard ammunition and bombs, computed fundamental ballistic tables, and
     prepared ballistic data to be used in improving fire control equipment. The results of the exterior ballistics
     section's work were used as the basis for computing firing and bombing tables and for designing new
     projectiles. In December of 1938, the research division was renamed the Ballistic Research Laboratory
     (BRL).

Chapter 1

THE COMPUTER AGE

     1-6. Between 1930 and 1931, the burden of firing table computation was reduced by the development of the
     Bush Differential Analyzer. The machine solved differential equations by means of mechanical integration
     with speed and accuracy. However, it soon became overpowered by the demand to accomplish more volume
     and scope of work than it was designed to do, leading the BRL to arrange access to larger analyzers of the
     same type. Simultaneously, considerable progress was made in substituting electrical components for
     mechanical, allowing newer analyzers to obtain greater speed of operation and accuracy.

     1-7. By 1937, advancements in electrical calculation devices revolutionized ballistic computation. Early
     comparisons between automated firing table calculations and traditional methods demonstrated significant
     improvements in accuracy and efficiency, leading to the adoption of punch-card machines for ballistic work
     by 1941. Subsequent developments, such as IBM's relay calculator, further enhanced computational speed,
     performing addition and subtraction twice as fast and multiplication three times faster than earlier systems.

     1-8. World War II created an immense demand for firing tables, requiring around-the-clock computation.
     Large rooms of workers, primarily women, performed millions of numerical integration calculations using
     hand-crank calculators to compute trajectories for firing tables. Despite advances in computing technology,
     the need for greater speed, accuracy, and operational flexibility grew as the war progressed and demand
     increased.

     1-9. Two computers, the electronic numerical integrator and computer (also called ENIAC) and the
     Electronic Discrete Variable Computer (also called EDVAC), were designed during World War II to address
     the growing need for firing table computation but were completed after the war. The electronic numerical
     integrator and computer's development in 1947 marked the start of the computer age, driven by the need to
     efficiently compute firing table trajectories. Follow-on systems, such as the Ordnance Discrete Variable
     Automatic Computer (also called ORDVAC) and Ballistic Research Laboratory electronic scientific
     computer (also called BRLESC), further advanced trajectory calculations. The BRL electronic scientific
     computer remained in use into the late 1970s, but improvements in commercial computer technology
     eventually surpassed the requirements for firing table development, eliminating the need for in-house
     systems.

MODERN ERA

     1-10. After World War II, firing and bombing tables were divided between the U.S. Army and U.S. Air Force,
     with bombing tables relocated to Eglin Air Force Base and firing tables remaining at Aberdeen Proving
     Ground. Despite expectations of reduced emphasis, the Cold War, Korean Conflict, and Vietnam War
     sustained the need for firing tables and ballistic work. Advancements in computing technology accelerated
     development, but improvements in trajectory models for simulating projectile flight remained essential.

     1-11. In the 1960s, the trajectory model was upgraded from a basic point mass simulation to the modified
     point mass (also called MPM) model, developed in 1966 to better represent spin-stabilized artillery shells by
     incorporating yaw of repose. Over time, the modified point mass model has been modified to simulate direct
     fire rounds, base-burn shells, and rocket-assisted projectiles. Widely recognized, it serves as the basis for the
     North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) standard in ballistic fire control.

     1-12. The development of the modified point mass trajectory model marked a breakthrough in firing table
     methodology, reducing the number of test rounds needed while maintaining accuracy. Interior ballistic
     modeling addressed muzzle velocity (MV) issues from propellant temperature and projectile weight
     variations, while improvements in test design and aerodynamic coefficient development further enhanced
     efficiency. By the 1970s, advancements in computing technology enabled field use of devices for training
     and combat, producing more accurate firing tables at lower costs.

     1-13. Early field computers were unsophisticated by today's standards but represented significant
     technological advancements for their time. The field artillery digital automatic computer (also called
     FADAC) integrated point mass equations of motion and used polynomial fits to calculate drift. Similarly,
     tank and helicopter ballistic computers applied fitting techniques to incorporate firing table data into fire
     control systems. These devices functioned as advanced calculators, processing polynomial fits to support
     ballistic computations.

                                                                                                                               Introduction

1-14. Advances in miniaturized components allowed for smaller devices and increased computation speed,
driven by improvements in processing technology and more efficient hardware designs. As computational
speeds reached acceptable levels and the devices became small enough with sufficient memory and storage
capability, field computers could solve the equations of motion that simulate projectile flight without relying
on polynomial fitting techniques. This process provided more accurate gunnery solutions.

1-15. By this time, the Firing Tables Branch was providing databases and computational methodologies for
field computers such as tactical fire (also called TACFIRE) and the battery computer system (also called
BCS) for artillery applications. Similar efforts supported fire control computers for tanks, helicopters, and
mortars. In the early 1990s, the Firing Tables Branch (now the Firing Tables and Ballistics [FTaB] Division)
partnered with Norway's Forsvarets Forshningsinsitutt (FFI), to plan the next generation of ballistic
processing for field artillery technical fire control systems.

1-16. Advancements in computing speed, memory size, and programming flexibility made a standard,
layered set of software modules for ballistic processing appealing to NATO AC/225 Panel IV, Sub-Panel 2
(now Land Capabilities Group 3 on Fire Support, Sub-Group 2). FTaB, co-leading with Norway's Forsvarets
Forskningsinsitutt (FFI), developed the NATO armament ballistic kernel (NABK), designed for shareability
and reusability. Fifteen NATO nations are implementing country-specific versions of NABK for artillery fire
control systems, enabling trajectory and MV simulations and ammunition interchangeability during
multinational exercises. The first application of the ballistic kernel (BK) was its inclusion in the improved
mortar ballistic computer (IMBC) in May 1995, with subsequent IMBC BK versions supporting new
munitions and the Mortar Fire Control System. Lessons from IMBC BK development were pivotal to the
NABK effort.

1-17. Another recent effort within FTaB is the meteorological kernel, a weather forecasting model that
improves accuracy while reducing reliance on labor-intensive weather balloon collection. Accurate weather
data is critical for generating precise gunnery solutions. FTaB has collaborated with NATO and academia to
develop and test meteorological kernel prototypes, including live-fire exercises in Denmark (2003), Turkey's
high desert (2006), and planned testing in southern France to evaluate its performance across diverse terrains.

1-18. Through involvement in international forums such as NATO, The Technical Cooperation Program, and
the Joint Ballistic Working Group, FTaB has become a global training hub for firing table data reduction,

Corps units under quasi-combat conditions, building a database for fire delivery techniques. Tests in the
1990s confirmed that weather data inaccuracies are the largest source of delivery errors.

and the Joint Technical Coordinating Group for Munitions Effectiveness.

FACILITIES

     1-25. Aberdeen Proving Ground’s aerodynamics range was the world’s first large-scale, fully instrumented
     ballistic range producing data on the aerodynamic characteristics of missiles in free flight. For more than 40
     years, its data has been integral to the development of supersonic aerodynamic designs. The facility’s unique
     technology became the foundation of similar installations worldwide.

     1-26. The Transonic Range of BRL is part of the Free Flight Aerodynamics Branch of the Exterior BRL.
     Designed in 1944, primary construction was completed in 1947, and instrumentation was completed for
     full-scale operation in the summer of 1950. Projectiles traveling through this enclosed spark photography
     range are not restrained or affected by any forces other than those normally encountered in flight.

Aerodynamics Range

     1-27. During the 1930s, research into advanced ballistic measurement techniques began at Aberdeen Proving
     Ground. Before the German Army entered Poland, exceptional data on the flight of projectiles was already
     being provided. The crisis of World War II gave incentive to incorporate this pioneering research into the
     design and construction of the aerodynamics range at the BRL. The facility was unique for its time and
     established the capability to study, in detail, the aerodynamics of bodies in supersonic free flight.

     1-28. The range’s sequential, high-speed photographic instrumentation has recorded the flight of projectiles,
     missiles, and aircraft important to national defense. Personnel working in this original installation have
     contributed to technical disciplines ranging from photography and high-speed circuit design to the theory of
     flight. The range is recognized as the prototype for similar installations in the United States and abroad.

Transonic Free Flight Range

     1-29. The purposes of this range are threefold:
          • To provide data for determining the aerodynamic coefficients of projectiles up to 8 inches in
                diameter or wingspan.
          • To provide an accurate instrument with which to investigate the causes of dispersion of projectiles.
          • To provide data on flow characteristics from the subsonic through the transonic and on into the
                higher regions of velocity.

                                                                                                                                     Introduction

     1-30. The Transonic Range has many predecessors and contemporaries as a free-flight range. The first such
     range is believed to have been built at the National Physical Laboratory in England.

     1-31. The Transonic Range is 1,173 feet in length. The part of the building nearest the gun position is
     reinforced concrete while the remainder is sheet metal on a steel frame. The first 750 feet of the range is
     24x24 feet in a cross section and is fully instrumented. The remaining 423 feet has never been instrumented
     but does facilitate the extension of roll histories using yaw card techniques. The metal sections of the building
     are insulated and have an aluminum covering. The instrumented section has radiant heating.

     1-32. The tested projectile is usually launched from a gun. Projectiles pass through the opening in the large
     concrete blast shield and then into the enclosed range. Projectiles travel through an array of 25 spark
     photographic stations, arranged in five groups of five stations each. These stations each capture a tiny chapter
     in the motion history of projectiles as they pass through the range.

     1-33. To study causes of dispersion of projectiles, researchers must understand the following:
          • The motion of the projectile in the launcher.
          • Any reaction between the launcher and the projectile before and during emergence.
          • The reaction of the projectile in the muzzle region or while it is affected by muzzle gases.
          • A timed history of projectile motion until some target or measure of dispersion is reached at the
                conclusion of its recorded flight.

     1-34. The capabilities of this facility allow for the testing of full-scale projectiles up to 8 inches in diameter.
     The collection of data that gives a history of the motion of those projectiles and permits study of their
     aerodynamic properties in free flight, and the recording of flow patterns about the projectiles for aerodynamic
     considerations. A 1,000-yard target is incorporated with the range facilities to make the study of dispersion
     possible through a close study of initial conditions and results on the target.

     1-35. The third purpose of the range, to provide data on detailed flow characteristics, is partially satisfied by
     regular spark records, however, another piece of specialized instrumentation called a mosaic has been
     developed to further this purpose. As the name indicates, mosaics consist of a series of large 11 x 14-inch
     photographic plates laid out together on a tabletop, placed just below the calculated trajectory. A spark source,
     located above these plates, is triggered in the same way as the standard stations and the large mosaic picture
     is obtained. This picture shows flow detail in such a size as to be easily studied for small effects. When
     trajectories can be closely estimated, the distance from spark to plates in this setup can be shortened to as
     little as 5 feet, allowing the use of a fast and less intense spark that results in sharper detail than the usual
     shadow stations.

Preparation for and Firing of a Program

     1-36. After model shops receive approved designs for projectiles, the models are manufactured and taken to
     the physical measurements laboratory for measurements. The moments of inertia, both transverse and axial,
     are determined on a torsion pendulum and compared with known test masses. The periods are measured by
     means of electronic timing. The timing signal is produced and recorded through a photocell, optical-counter
     combination.

     1-37. While the manufacture and measurements are being completed, the means of launching must be
     determined. To date, all launchings except for some rockets have been made from breech-loading guns. Some
     projectiles, because of external configuration, desired initial angle of launch, maximum pressure limits,
     required spin, and protection of surface finish may require special, individual launching devices.

     1-38. The expected trajectory or flight path of a test projectile is calculated by standard methods and the
     optimum spark height is selected to best fit this path. Each spark unit contains a delay system which must be
     adjusted to the expected velocity, which allows the projectile to proceed to a point opposite the center of the
     reflective screen before the spark is discharged.

     1-39. After all preparations have been made, a systematic firing procedure is carried out. The cameras are
     loaded with photographic plates and the high-speed motion picture cameras with film. All personnel are
     cleared from the possible danger areas. The projectile and propellant are loaded into the gun. From the
     instrument shelter, a facility separated about 1,000 feet from the range itself, the round number and station
     designation are exposed on the plates, the cameras are opened, and a bias control is operated which allows

DATA REDUCTION AND COMPUTATION

     1-40. One of the free flight range technique’s basic problems is data reduction. Data reduction is the process
     of selecting, focusing, simplifying, abstracting, and transforming data to reduce the amount of capacity
     required to store it. For firing tables, only some aerodynamic, met, physical characteristics, and time-of-flight
     data is required from the amount collected during free flight testing.

     1-41. Achieving successful data reduction is difficult primarily because free flight ranges only work with the
     actual motion of a projectile in flight; aerodynamic forces and moments during must be inferred from that
     motion. Due to the importance of making large amounts of data palatable to the average reader, ranges like
     the free flight aerodynamics and Transonic Range are limited in how they can output their data; a large bulk
     of the experimentation time is spent analyzing and simplifying the data. For this reason, great effort is
     constantly being made to refine the process and make as much use of large-scale computing machines as
     possible.

     1-42. The simplified data used in firing tables is employed to compute a ballistic coefficient or to produce
     final values for each listed ammunition. A ballistic coefficient is a numerical representation of how well a
     projectile retains its initial velocity over any distance. Once the ballistic coefficient is determined, a value for
     design MV and in-flight weight of the round is obtained. This information is then coupled with the
     aerodynamic and physical characteristics data to compute the firing tables.

USING CONVERSION FACTORS

     3-8. Unit conversion is a simple process that involves multiplication or division by a numerical factor and
     rounding. Use the following steps to construct a unit conversion problem so that the initial unit of
     measurement cancels and the desired unit remains:

          • Identify the starting unit (meters, yards, feet, for example).
          • Identify the desired unit.
          • Identify the appropriate unit conversion factor(s) (see chapter 2, figure 2-4) and use exact conversion

                factors whenever available.
          • Cancel units and perform the calculation (multiply, divide, for example) and double check by

                repeating the calculation.
          • Evaluate the result to ensure the answer makes sense.

     3-9. There are best practices to keep in mind when converting units of measurement. First, do not attempt to
     round any of the quantities/numbers during execution of the calculation. If necessary, only round the final
     calculated solution. Many unit conversion problems require only a single unit conversion factor; however,
     multiple conversion factors may be required to solve a problem. Ensure the correct conversion factor is used
     to solve your problem. If an incorrect (or approximate) conversion factor is used, a correct solution is not

     8-9. Unit standard operating procedures (also called SOPs) determine battlesight ranges based on the
     commander’s analysis of METT-TC (I) which stands for mission, enemy, terrain and weather, troops and
     support available, time available, civil considerations, and informational considerations. Master gunners are
     responsible for maximizing lethality under any conditions; properly establishing battle carry ammunition and
     battlesight range enables units to maintain overmatch when equipment does not operate as intended.

     8-10. Firing tables are required to establish effective battle carry ammunition and battlesight ranges. Given a
     commander’s assessment and the master gunner’s ability to take advantage of the relatively flat trajectories
     of armor-defeating ammunition, table data is used to determine the best option for a high probability of
     first-round strikes on target.

     8-11. Master gunners take the information from the commander and determine battle carry ammunition and
     battlesight range based on two main factors: height of the anticipated target and MAX ORD of the
     ammunition type. The MAX ORD and range to MAX ORD for ammunition provides necessary information
     to determine a battlesight range.

     8-29. There are several firing occasions where the use of Table B’s first three columns is necessary. Some

                QE for the ammunition type).

          • Determining the general location of an ROI.
          • Employing indirect fire applications using the main gun.
          • Designing and constructing ranges.

     8-30. Examples are provided for determining distance X and maximum range based on QE. These examples
     use Table B.

Round Out of Impact—Sabot Fired with Coax Indexed

     8-31. A tank fires an ROI causing a cease-fire freeze. When the crew is asked what was fired and how, they
     say they fired sabot with the master blaster after a misfire. The master gunner must analyze what led to the
     misfire and why the projectile left the SDZ.

     8-32. Upon analysis, it is discovered that the prior engagement consisted of a tank and troop presentation.
     The crew successfully engaged the tank target with sabot indexed and proceeded to index coax to service the
     troops. The most common cause of ROI, having high-explosive antitank (HEAT) indexed when firing sabot,
     is ruled out as there were no HEAT engagements immediately prior to firing the ROI. All HEAT rounds
     allocated to the run were still in the ready ammunition compartment.

     8-33. Ruling out HEAT index, the master gunner evaluates the actions taken immediately prior to the ROI.
     Having serviced troops last, the coaxial-ready ammunition box is inspected. No rounds remain after the prior
     engagement. When the crew is confronted, the gunner does not remember running out of coax ammunition.
     At this point, the master gunner identifies that coax is still selected on the gunner’s primary sight lower panel
     and the gun tube SE is far higher than it would be if MAIN was selected. The correct range to target was
     input before firing.

     8-34. In this example, the master gunner determines that after expending all 7.62-mm coax ammunition, the
     gunner did not index main gun before proceeding to the next engagement, which presented a single stationary
     tank. When the target lifted, the gunner indexed sabot while coax was still selected and attempted to fire at a
     1,720-meter tank target. The fire control system induced the required SE for 7.62-mm M80 ammunition to
     hit the target. Because the main gun was not selected and the coaxial was out of ammunition, the gunner
     attempted to fire, but no ignition occurred. The gunner then announced MISFIRE using the “master blaster”
     to manually fire the main gun. The fired main gun round missed the target and landed out of impact due to
     excessive SE. Now, the traveled distance of the projectile must be determined.

     8-35. Figure 8-41 illustrates the 1,720-meter tank target at 90-meters EL, which is 53.25₥ above the firing
     position. Because coax was indexed and 7.62-mm M80 ammunition was used, the firing table for M80 is
     used to determine the SE.

  SECTION II – TERMS

aeroballistics
          The science of ballistics combined with that of aerodynamics, dealing primarily with the flight path of
          projectiles, rockets, guided missiles, and others, through the atmosphere.

aerodynamic characteristics
          The branch of mechanics that deals with the motion of air and other gases and with the effects of such
          motion on objects in such a media.

axis of the bore
          The line passing through the center of the bore.

azimuth
          For fire control systems, azimuth is the rotation of a weapon about the pintle or turret ring (side to side)
          as opposed to elevation, which is up and down. Azimuth also indicates direction in degrees or mils. It
          can be measured using a compass, optic, or sensor to determine the angular relationship between two
          or more points.

ballistic drift
          The lateral departure of spin-stabilized ammunition from the gun-target line. Only spin-stabilized
          rounds are affected.

ballistic trajectory
          The path of a projectile when influenced only by external forces such as gravity and atmospheric
          friction.

ballistics
          The science or study of the motion, propulsion, flight, and impact of projectiles. It is also the art or
          science of designing projectiles for maximum flight or terminal effects performance.

caliber
          The measurement of the diameter of the bore of the gun. This measurement is also used as a relational
          distance, for example “the last 5 calibers of the gun.”

commencement of rifling
          The point in a rifled gun tube at which the lands have attained full size.

distance x
          The maximum distance a projectile (to include guided missiles and rockets) will travel when fired or
          launched at a given quadrant elevation with a given charge or propulsion system.

exterior/external ballistics
          Encompasses the period from when the projectile has left the muzzle and any sabot pedal discard has
          occurred until impact with the target. It includes the projectile muzzle velocity, projectile dynamics
          and stability, the predicted flight path, time of flight, and the angle, velocity, and location of impact. It
          is the study of projectiles in flight.

                                                                                                                                       References

fire control
          The science of offsetting the direction of weapon fire from the line of sight to the target to hit the
          target.

jump
          Vertical jump is the angle, measured in ₥, between the line of departure and the line of elevation.

kinetic energy
          Energy due to mass and velocity. It can be calculated by taking half the mass and multiplying it by the
          velocity squared (1/2 mv²).

line of departure
          The center of axis as the projectile leaves the muzzle.

line of elevation
          The center of the axis of the gun bore. Also known as the axis of the bore, the line of elevation
          describes the exact bore centerline from the chamber region, to the muzzle, and onward indefinitely.

line of sight
          The straight line between the gun sight and the target.

Mach number
          The speed (velocity) of the projectile in relation to the speed of sound (343m/s).

maximum ordinate
          The maximum height the projectile will travel above the gun line of sight on its path to the point of
          impact.

muzzle velocity
          The speed (velocity) of the projectile approximately 2 feet beyond the muzzle of the gun (after shot
          exit).

obscuration
          The effects of firing which hinder observation.

prediction angle
          The angle between the line of sight (the line from the weapon station to target at the instant the weapon
          fires) and the weapon line (the extension of the weapon axis).

superelevation/tangent elevation
          The gun angle between the line of sight and axis of the bore measured in ₥.

surface danger zone
          The ground and airspace designated within the training complex, to include associated safety areas, for
          vertical and lateral containment of projectiles, fragments, debris, and components resulting from the
          firing, launching, or detonation of weapon systems to include explosives and demolitions.

time of flight
          The time taken for a projectile to reach a given range after firing.

yaw
          The rotation of the nose of a projectile away from the line of flight in the Y-axis.
