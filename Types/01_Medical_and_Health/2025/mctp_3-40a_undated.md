                     CHAPTER 1.

  FUNDAMENTALS OF HEALTH SERVICE SUPPORT

Health service support (HSS) medical forces provide the warfighter on-demand healthcare
capabilities; ensure a healthy, fit, medically ready force; counter health threats to the deployed
force; provide critical care; and manage combat casualties.

All HSS directly supports the national military strategy of forward presence and power projection.
It strengthens the warfighting commander by providing essential in theater care and rapid medical
evacuation (MEDEVAC) of casualties across the continuum of care to include medical treatment
facilities (MTFs) in the continental United States (CONUS).

Health service support personnel often work with North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO)
Allied Medical Publications, and Allied Joint Publications; American, British, Canadian, Australian,
and New Zealand Armies’ Program standards; and other coalition forces. The reference section of
this publication lists numerous medical standards, agreements, and methods for interoperability that
guide HSS personnel actions when working with US allies.

The Marine Corps’ organic HSS assets provide prevention and protection, first responder, and
forward resuscitative capabilities of the continuum of health care, as well as a surgical platoon or
company. Within first responder and forward resuscitative capabilities, Marine Corps HSS units
provide emergency fresh, whole-blood transfusion (EFWBT), damage control resuscitation, damage
control surgery, prolonged casualty care, and patient holding. Enabling capabilities organic to some
HSS units include ancillary services, en route care, operational planning, logistical sustainment, and
unit-level command and control.

MISSION

The HSS mission is to minimize the effects that wounds, injuries, and disease have on unit
effectiveness, readiness, and morale. The mission is accomplished with an aggressive and
proactive preventive medicine (PVNTMED) program that safeguards personnel against potential
health risks and an HSS system that provides appropriate care from the point of injury or illness to
definitive care.

MCTP 3-40A, Health Service Support

   PRINCIPLES

    The HSS principles guide planning, organizing, managing, and executing HSS. Seldom will all
    principles exert equal influence in a given situation, and HSS is most effective when it can
    identify the priority principles and apply them in support of the commander’s mission. See
    Joint Publication (JP) 4-02, Joint Health Services, for more information on HSS principles. Each
    Service component must have an HSS system that encompasses—

       • Conformity. The medical plan must integrate and comply with the commander’s plan.
       • Proximity. The medical plan must provide HSS as close to combat operations as the tactical

         situation permits.
       • Flexibility. The medical plan must shift HSS resources to meet changing requirements.
       • Mobility. The medical plan must anticipate rapid movement requirements in supporting

          combat forces during operations.
       • Continuity. The medical plan must provide optimum, uninterrupted care and treatment to the

         wounded, injured, and sick.
       • Coordination. The medical plan must ensure that HSS resources, typically in limited supply,

          are efficiently and effectively employed.

   A HEALTHY AND FIT FORCE

    Health service support promotes wellness and quality of life to strengthen the human component
    of military forces against disease and injury. A warfighting commander with a physically and
    mentally healthy force can deploy them anywhere in the world assures warfighting commanders
    physical and mental readiness. Wellness requires continuous attention before, during, and after
    deployment to sustain readiness and warfighting capabilities. Every effort should be made to
    incorporate all available medical specialties. Organic medical staff are employed to the greatest
    extent possible to align with force protection and conserve combat-power initiatives.

   THREAT TO THE FORCE

    The focus of HSS efforts remains on the health of the force and threats to it. The extent of an enemy
    threat lies in its intent and capability to inflict casualties. Other threats to a force’s health are disease
    and nonbattle injuries (DNBIs). Although the level of threat to a forces’ health depends on the
    enemy, a complex set of environmental, physiological, and operational factors when combined can
    result in DNBIs. Failure to counter threats jeopardizes mission accomplishment.

ROLES OF CARE

Health services support within an area of operations is organized by medical capabilities into roles
of care that extend from forward to the rear area of responsibility. Each role of medical care is
designed to provide the required mobility and capability to meet the supported operational unit’s
basic health care needs, phased treatment, casualty holding, and preparation for evacuation of the
sick, wounded, or injured.

MCTP 3-40A, Health Service Support

    Fleet Marine Forces’ (FMF) HSS is flexible to meet mission demands despite enemy forces,
    terrain, and other tactical situations. Furthermore, at each stage of the role of care hierarchy,
    treatment capabilities and capacities increase. The five interacting factors that limit an HSS
    system are—

       • Urgency of the patients’ needs.
       • Unit mobility.
       • Tactical or operational situation.
       • HSS personnel assigned, equipment capabilities, and supplies available
       • Workload of each HSS unit relative to its treatment capacity.

    Point of Principal Treatment
    The HSS system provides care beginning at the point where medical care becomes necessary and
    ends at the appropriate treatment facility. Casualties are evacuated through the HSS system until
    they arrive at a facility with the capability, time, and bed capacity to begin definitive intervention.
    This intervention continues until the Service member is returned to duty or prepared for further
    evacuation. This is known as the point of principal treatment. The principal treatment site is
    determined according to the patient’s diagnosis and the HSS unit’s capabilities and workload.

    Role 1 Care
    Role 1 care is the first medical attention that military personnel receive and is referred to as
    unit-level medical care. This role of care includes immediate lifesaving measures, DNBI
    prevention and care, combat and operational stress preventive measures, and patient location
    and acquisition (i.e., collection).

    First Responder. The first responder provides unit-level medical care. This is composed of
    personnel from a unit and its organic battalion aid stations (BASs) or squadron aid stations.
    In the case of organizations without organic medical elements, unit-level care is provided by
    medical elements at the regimental, group, or support squadron level or by other designated
    medical elements. Tactical combat casualty care (TCCC) has three phases, occurs during a combat
    mission, and is the military counterpart to prehospital emergency medical treatment.

    Prehospital TCCC in the military is most commonly provided by enlisted personnel and includes
    first responder, trained as combat lifesaver (CLS), unit Corpsmen, independent duty Corpsmen,
    or treatment by other medical personnel. Tactical combat casualty care focuses on the most likely
    threats, injuries, and conditions encountered in combat and on a strictly limited range of
    interventions directed at the most serious wounds. The three phases of TCCC are: care under fire,
    tactical field care, and tactical evacuation.

       • Care under fire. The first phase is care under fire, or in the case of a non-combat environment,
         care under threat. In this phase, the scene is not safe, and the priority is to establish safety
         with fire superiority or by controlling threats.

       • Tactical field care. The second phase is tactical field care, which is provided when the
          responder and casualty are no longer under a direct threat from effective enemy fire. This
          allows time and relative safety for a more deliberate approach to casualty assessment
          and treatment.

       • Tactical evacuation. Tactical evacuation starts with a systemic assessment and treatment, in a
         secure location, and ends with the evacuation of casualties to a higher role of care.

Combat Lifesaver. The CLS is a non-medical military personnel selected by their unit for
additional training beyond basic first aid procedures. At least two Marines are trained to be a CLS
for each Corpsman on the unit’s table of organization. A CLS individual’s primary duties are to
provide enhanced first aid for injuries, based on training, before the Corpsman arrives and to assist
the Corpsman if needed. The commander delegates the management of the CLS training program
to the operations section (i.e., S-3).

Unit Hospital Corpsman. The company or squadron Corpsman represents the first point where a
sick, injured, or wounded Marine receives care from trained medical personnel. In case of an
emergency, before a Hospital Corpsman (HM) arrives, a primary assessment identifies life-threats
and intervention requirements (i.e., lifesaving measures) to be performed by fellow Marines
trained as a CLS, first responder self/buddy aid before an HM progresses to the secondary survey.

Usually, the unit HM provides primary (if responding first) and secondary assessments, then
emergency or lifesaving measures (e.g., establishing and maintaining an airway, control of
bleeding, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, treatment for shock, fracture stabilization). The HM’s
duties involve applying basic medical skills, using medical equipment and supplies, and initiating
requests for assistance and evacuation.

The Aid Station. The aid station provides role 1 care for the Marine Corps. Treatment at the aid
station is managed by more highly skilled personnel: a physician, physician assistant, and an
independent duty Corpsman. Care provided at an aid station is based on a more comprehensive
evaluation and treatment plan than offered by a first responder (i.e., HM or CLS), which could
include restoring airways, administering intravenous fluids, antibiotics, and EFWBTs, and
applying splints and bandages. These medical management elements prepare patients to return to
duty or evacuation to the appropriate role of care.

Forward Resuscitative Care. Forward resuscitative care prioritizes non-surgical interventions, to
include damage control resuscitation and efforts to save an individual’s life, limb, or eyesight.
This intervention can reduce the rates of morbidity and mortality stemming from trauma and
hemorrhage. It provides a mobile capability within theater and as close to the battlefield as is
tactically possible. This capability requires operational and logistical support when employed
(e.g., EFWBT). Its location and accessibility are critical when evacuating casualties as is
established communication with the next appropriate role of care.

Force level HSS is provided by HSS units and elements of the Marine logistics group (MLG).
Long-term, in-theater health care support is difficult because of limited resources. External
resources, such as casualty receiving treatment ships (CRTSs), can augment force-level support.

Role 2 Care
Role 2 care provides advanced trauma management, surgical capabilities, and emergency medical
treatment that amplifies resuscitation actions started in role 1. If necessary, additional emergency
measures are instituted (e.g., damage control surgery), but they do not go beyond the measures
dictated by immediate necessities.

    Surgical Platoon. Surgical platoon support includes collecting, clearing, and evacuating casualties
    from supported elements and medical units for resuscitative and surgical procedures. The surgical
    platoon provides advanced trauma life support capabilities and a connection among first responders,
    Corpsmen, and surgical companies. Also, the surgical platoon can be used to reinforce role 1
    capabilities in the absence of the forward resuscitative surgery system (FRSS).

    Surgical Company. A surgical company can provide direct or general HSS including surgical care,
    damage control resuscitation, stabilization, collecting and evacuating care, some en route care,
    radiology, laboratory, holding care capability, and dental. For planning considerations, a surgical
    company can support a regimental size force. Surgical companies provide the highest level of
    medical care organic to the Marine Corps.

    The Forward Resuscitative Surgery System. The FRSS is a highly mobile, rapidly deployable,
    trauma surgical unit that provides emergency surgical interventions to stabilize casualties who
    might otherwise die or lose a limb or eyesight before receiving appropriate treatment. The FRSS
    has a small logistic footprint that supports early introduction into the operating area, rapid
    movement, formation, deployment, and redeployment in forward areas.

    Casualty Receiving and Treatment Ships. Large-deck amphibious ships are designated for use as
    CRTSs. This capability is distinguished by a team of medical officers and technicians with advanced
    facilities and resources. The CRTS have general surgery, basic laboratory, pharmacy, X-ray, dental,
    and holding ward capabilities, and these ships provide physicians and operating space to perform
    emergency procedures. Patients who need a more comprehensive scope of treatment are evacuated
    to the role of care their condition requires.

    Role 3 Care
    Role 3 care provides treatment in an MTF or veterinary facility (i.e., working animals) that is staffed
    and equipped to provide resuscitation, surgery (some definitive surgery), and post-operative
    treatment. This level expands the support provided at role 2 care. Patients who are unable to tolerate
    or may not survive movement over long distances receive surgical care in a hospital as close to the
    supported unit as the tactical situation allows. This role includes provisions for evacuating patients
    from supported units, proper staffing and equipment for an MTF to provide care for all categories
    of patients, and area support to units without organic medical assets. Mobile role 3 assets should be
    located where it best provides the most expedient support to combat forces. Role 3 care includes
    the following:

    Theater Hospitalization and Surgical-Clinical Specialties. The theater hospital ensures quality
    health care to combat forces. Theater hospitalization facilities are staffed and equipped to provide
    resuscitation, surgery (some definitive surgery), and postoperative treatment. This role of care is
    the first step toward restoration of functional health, as compared to the stabilization actions
    provided at role 2 care. The theater hospital should be employed in relation to the point of injury,
    taking into account the time-distance relationship and ability of the patient evacuation system.

Hospital Ships, Navy Expeditionary Medical Facilities. Hospital ships, Navy expeditionary medical
facilities (EMFs) overseas hospitals, other Services’ MTFs, and host-nation support (HNS)
agreements provide theater-level HSS. Moving patients to and between theater facilities is
coordinated through the United States Transportation Command patient movement requirements
center (TPMRC).

Role 4 Care
Role 4 care is provided in US base hospitals and well-equipped overseas facilities. Mobilization
requires expanding military hospital capacities in the National Disaster Medical System (including
Department of Veterans Affairs and civilian hospital beds) to meet the potential increase in demand
as in-theater patients are evacuated. These base hospitals represent the most definitive medical care
available within the medical care system.

Definitive Health Care (Comprehensive Medical and Surgical). Definitive health care relies on all
aspects of the military health system, to include comprehensive HSS throughout the Department
of Defense (DoD), and it must be comparable to civilian standards. This role of care provides
Service members and their dependents with professional clinical care that targets health, fitness,
and optimal physical and emotional well-being. It requires evacuation and hospitalization
strategies that can maintain the capability to provide a fit and healthy force, prevent casualties,
and provide care and management to casualties in theater and in CONUS.

Continental United States (Full Convalescent, Restorative, Rehabilitative Care). Limited resources
can impede long-term, in-theater health care efforts. Convalescent, restorative, and rehabilitative
care are usually provided in CONUS. Convalescent care is provided in military hospitals, other
Federal hospitals, and select civilian facilities that might be activated under the National Disaster
Medical System. The Global Patient Movement Request Center coordinates patient movement to
and between CONUS MTFs.

TRIAGE

Triage is the system that sorts and prioritizes casualties based on injury, tactical situation, mission,
and available resources. It is conducted at each role of medical care. Triage is the most effective
means for establishing order and is a method for providing the greatest benefit to the largest number
of patients. Triage allocates resources within the limitations of time, distance, and capability. It is a
constant process as casualties move within and through the roles of medical care. The decision to
withhold medical care from any casualty is difficult to make but may be necessary.

Although decisions of this nature have been uncommon in recent conflicts, military medical
personnel should be prepared to make these decisions in large-scale combat operations or where
patient movement, or logistics are constrained. Nonetheless, the goal to provide the best care to
as many casualties as possible must guide these difficult decisions. Deciding how resources
should be used is first, based on mission and immediate tactical situation, and then by medical
needs, irrespective of a casualty’s nationality or combatant status. To maximize limited
resources, triage or sorting of casualties is required. Triage requires individuals to use clinical

MCTP 3-40A, Health Service Support

    The ultimate goals of military medicine are to sustain life, limb, and eyesight and return as many
    Service members as possible to duty. The triage categories include immediate, delayed, minimal,
    and expectant.

    Immediate
    The immediate category requires individuals to receive attention within minutes to two hours
    upon arrival to avoid death or major disability. The procedures in this category are focused on
    patients who have a good chance to survive with immediate intervention. Examples of injuries in
    this category include—

       • Uncontrolled hemorrhage.
       • Airway obstruction or potential compromise.
       • Tension pneumothorax.
       • Torso, neck, or pelvis injuries with shock.
       • Head injury requiring emergent decompression.
       • Threatened loss of limb.
       • Retrobulbar hematoma.
       • Multiple extremity amputations.
       • Sepsis without source control.
       • Hollow viscous injury with delay in evacuation to surgical capability.

    Delayed
    Injuries in the delayed category include patients who are wounded and require surgery but whose
    general condition permits delaying treatment without endangering a patient’s life, limb, or
    eyesight. Sustaining treatment is required (e.g., fluid resuscitation, fracture stabilization, and
    antibiotics, bladder catheterization, gastric decompression, pain relief). Delayed category injury
    examples include—

       • Blunt or penetrating torso injuries without signs of shock.
       • Fractures.
       • Soft-tissue injuries without significant bleeding.
       • Facial fractures without airway compromise.
       • Globe (i.e., eye) injuries.
       • Survivable burns without immediate threat to life (airway, respiratory) or limb.

    Minimal
    Casualties in the minimal category have relatively minor injuries and can effectively care for
    themselves or be rendered minimal medical care. These casualties might require manpower
    resources to assist with movement and care. When a mass casualty incident occurs close to an MTF,
    it is likely that these casualties are the first to arrive, circumventing the casualty evacuation
    (CASEVAC) chain. Such casualties can inundate the treatment facility, leading to early commitment

and ineffective use of resources. To be effective stewards of resources, it is imperative to secure and
strictly control access to the MTF immediately upon notification of a mass-casualty event. Examples
of injuries within this category include—

  • Minor lacerations.
  • Abrasions.
  • Small bone fractures.
  • Minor burns.

Expectant
Casualties in the expectant category have injuries requiring treatment that could overwhelm medical
resources at the expense of treatable patients. The expectant casualty should not be abandoned but
should be separated from view of other casualties when feasible, receive comfort measures, and be
intermittently reassessed and re-triaged if appropriate. These casualties require a staff that can
monitor and provide comfort measures. Expectant category injury examples include—

  • Any casualty arriving without vital signs or signs of life.
  • Transcranial gunshot wound with coma.
  • Open pelvic injuries with uncontrolled bleeding and shock due to fluid loss.
  • Life-threatening burns that cannot be treated with available resources.
  • High cervical spinal cord injuries affecting spontaneous respiration.
  • Patients requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation or ventilator support if ventilators are

     not available.
  • Destructive cardiac injuries.
  • Chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear (CBRN) casualties with ionizing radiation

     exposures with an absorbed dose of eight grays (eight joules per kilogram).

   THE HAGUE AND GENEVA CONVENTIONS

    Conducting armed hostilities on land is regulated by the law of war, which is both written
    and unwritten. The law of land warfare is derived from two principal sources: customs and
    lawmaking treaties, such as The Hague and Geneva Conventions. Under the US Constitution,
    the rights and duties stated in these conventions are part of the Department of Defense
    Directive (DoDD) 2311.01, DoD Law of War Program, and violating any convention is a
    serious offense. Under these conventions, the signatories established the principle of
    disinterested aid to victims of war, including those who, through wounds, capture, or shipwreck,
    are no longer enemies but are merely suffering and defenseless human beings. Additional
    protocols to the Geneva Conventions establish standards of conduct for medical and religious
    personnel assigned to aid victims. The United States follows the Geneva Conventions of 1949
    and directs its military forces to abide by its articles; however, asymmetrical theaters, especially
    non-state actors, might not abide by the Convention accepted by nation states. Refer to the
    following sources for international and domestic law principles and the status and protections
    for medical personnel under both Conventions:

       • Marine Corps Tactical Publication (MCTP) 11-10B, The Commander’s Handbook on
         the Law of Naval Operations.

       • MCTP 11-10C, The Commander’s Handbook on the Law of Land Warfare.
       • Department of the Army Pamphlet 27-1, Treaties Governing Land Warfare.

environment to help a commander plan and make decisions. Medical intelligence involves more than
gathering information on potential diseases and environmental, physiological, or other operational

                     CHAPTER 3.
                     OPERATIONS

The Marine Corps organization for combat is based on its unique assigned force structure. Health
service support is common to every MAGTF, and definitive HSS planning is an integral part of
any operation. The inherent flexibility and broad spectrum of potential MAGTF missions requires
flexibility in HSS execution. The size, type, and configuration of HSS capabilities needed are
determined by mission, enemy, terrain and weather, troops and support available-time available.

Evolving Marine Corps operations require enhanced force mobility; therefore, HSS personnel
must evolve to meet these changes to be as capable and mobile as the forces they support.
Therefore, HSS elements must provide enhanced medical and evacuation capabilities to meet the
needs of a Marine Corps operation. These capabilities are concentrated in the MAGTF LCE and
HSS battlefield facilities to establish, displace, and rapidly relocate as required.

In garrison and during routine deployments, Marine Corps units are not staffed with the full
operational complement of HSS personnel. When increased medical and dental staffing levels are
required, the medical personnel are sourced from medical or dental battalions within the MLG.

MARINE AIR-GROUND TASK FORCE

The MAGTF is the Marine Corps’ principal warfighting organization for missions throughout the
competition continuum. Each MAGTF, regardless of its size or mission, has the same basic
structure with four core elements: a command element, GCE, ACE, and LCE with HSS in each.

MARINE CORPS FORCES

Although there are Marine Corps component commands for all combatant commanders (CCDRs)
and the Special Operations Command, three organizations (i.e., Marine Forces Command, Marine
Forces Pacific, and Marine Forces Reserve) have special medical staffs and could have an
operational medical planning staff. The medical staffs advise the commander on matters relating
to the health of the force, theater-MEDLOG, intratheater and intertheater patient movement,
OEHS activities, sanitation, safety, disease surveillance, medical intelligence, health threats,
medical personnel issues, as well as current and future HSS operational planning. Additional
duties include serving as the liaison for the CCDRs and other component surgeons and monitoring
HSS aspects of the time-phased force and deployment data flow.

Assault Echelon

   CAPABILITIES EXTERNAL TO THE MAGTF

    Health service support in the Marine Corps does not extend past role 2 care. Role 3 care and
    above is external to the MAGTF. Examples of higher levels of care and additional naval role 2
    capabilities include hospital ships, CRTS, fleet surgical teams (FSTs), forward-deployable
    preventive medicine units (FDPMUs), outside the continental United States MTFs, and joint
    theater hospitalization capabilities.

    Hospital Ships
    Hospital ships (T-AHs) are floating surgical hospitals. Their mission is to provide acute medical
    care in support of combat operations at sea. Support can be provided to ATFs, joint task forces,
    and combined forces. The T-AH is designed to receive patients primarily by helicopter, and it has
    limited capacity for receiving patients by surface craft.

    Casualty Receiving and Treatment Ships
    The CRTS have the largest medical capability of any amphibious ship in the ATF. A fully manned
    and equipped CRTS medical capability includes four to six operating rooms, a 15-bed intensive
    care unit, a quiet room, 45 ward beds, and six isolation and overflow beds. Dental spaces include a
    general dental operating room. Navy medical department personnel augment CRTS to achieve full
    casualty treatment capability. The ATF amphibious assault ships that can be used as CRTSs are the
    multipurpose landing helicopter dock (LHD) ship and the general purpose landing helicopter
    assault (LHA) ship. The CATF’s Annex Q (Planning Guidance, Medical Services) designates
    platforms to serve as CRTSs. For the medical support capabilities these vessels provide, and their
    potential roles as CRTSs, see Allied Tactical Publication ATP 08.1, Amphibious Operations
    Interoperability Matrix.

Fleet Surgical Teams
The FSTs are medical augmentation teams assigned to the amphibious squadron commander.
A FST provides surgical capability to the LHAs and LHDs for deployment and inter-deployment
surgical requirements. They are attached to amphibious readiness groups when deployed with a
MEU. The OIC and medical regulation and control officer are part of the amphibious squadron
staff, while the remaining team members are assigned to ships as needed throughout the
amphibious ready group, integrating with the respective ship’s medical department. The FST
provides the surgical capability to the CRTS. The OIC of the fleet surgical team is the CATF
surgeon, who is the senior medical authority afloat for the amphibious readiness group and the
principal medical advisor to the commander or CATF.

Forward Deployable Preventive Medicine Unit
The FDPMU is a rapidly deployable, task-organized, specialized preventive medicine platform
that meets the operational commander’s force health protection (FHP) needs. It includes former
forward-deployed laboratory capabilities and many expanded capabilities, including CBRN
agent identification.

The FDPMU’s mission is to expand FHP capabilities by identifying and evaluating
environmental hazards, assessing the risk of adverse health outcomes, and monitoring the
well-being of the deployed force. The FDPMU advises the operational commander on significant
risks, recommended countermeasures, and other interventions that would affect FHP. The five
major functional areas of FDPMU are PVNTMED, chemical, microbiology, disease vector, and
occupational and environmental health site assessment (OEHSA).

Theater Hospitalization Capability
This capability delivers health support to medically sustain forces in a geographic combatant
commander’s area of responsibility or in a joint operating area. These capabilities involve hospitals
purposefully positioned to provide in-theater support and deploy as modules or multiple individual
capabilities that provide incrementally increasing medical services. The theater hospitalization
capability offers essential care to either return patients to duty (within the theater-patient movement
policy) or stabilize them for evacuation to a definitive care facility outside the joint operating area.
In addition to skilled medical personnel, the hospitalization capability includes the facilities and
materiel (equipment and consumable supplies) necessary to render significant preventive and
curative health care. These robust services encompass primary inpatient and outpatient care;
emergent care; and enhanced medical, surgical, and ancillary capabilities.

Theater hospitalization capabilities can vary according to the regional infrastructure and the tempo
of an operation. A robust capability would contain the following services (which are not typically
provided at the lower roles): advanced burn management, blood bank services, MEDLOG,
optometry and ophthalmology, pediatrics, obstetrics, gynecology, internal medicine, cardiology,
pathology, infectious disease, intensive or critical care beds and nursing, medical nutrition
therapy, behavioral health, OEHS, physical and occupational therapies, dental, PVNTMED,
veterinary, and other medical specialties. Additionally, surgical capabilities exist for eye,
maxillofacial, neurosurgery and expanded radiology, pharmacy, and laboratory services.

                      LOGISTICS

Health service support logistics encompasses planning, procurement, initial issue, management,
resupply, and disposition of materiel required to support medical and dental elements organic
to FMF units. Requisitions for consumable Class VIII materiel follow the same channels as other
classes of supply through the medical logistics company. Guidance for planning and procuring
Class VIII (e.g., blood products, see Appendix B) is found in Navy/Marine Corps departmental
publication (NAVMC) 4000.2A, Marine Corps Class VIII Management Handbook, and
Department of Defense Instruction (DoDI) 6480.04, Armed Services Blood Program.

As with all classes of supply, careful consideration should be given to stock levels of Class VIII
materiel. Commanders should not be burdened with moving and maintaining excess supplies, nor
should support ever be delayed because of inadequate supplies or lack of resupply. When the
medical planner is developing and planning for appropriate levels of Class VIII support, the
following information is crucial to ensuring the HSS system can remain responsive to
the commander:

  • Concept of operation and scheme of maneuver.
  • Combat intensity.
  • Duration of the operation.
  • Casualty estimates.
  • Primary, alternate, contingent, emergent (PACE) communication plan.

ALLOWANCE AND SOURCE OF LOGISTICS

A MAGTF surgeon advises the MAGTF commander on medical or dental materiel support.
Allocation of supplies is documented in the table of equipment, the AMALs, ADALs, and the
30-day replenishment supply support requirement. The table of equipment, AMALs, and ADALs
are designed to support a specific number of days, of high intensity combat operations, for the
following size MAGTFs: MEF—60 days, MEB—30 days, MPF—30 days, and MEU—15 days
of supply. The AMAL and ADAL requirements to support a MEF are determined during mission
analysis in support of aligned units to that force. The AMALs and ADALs are allocated to support
specific requirements and can be reconfigured to be more expeditious for developing concepts.
The authorizing commander is responsible for funding AMALs and ADALs as well as assuring
access to program of record software such as Defense Medical Logistics Standard Support and
enabling DoD Activity Address Code in allocating Class VIII supplies.

Routine Resupply
Health service support personnel needing resupply should submit a requisition to their unit’s supply

Combat Resupply
During embarkation planning, HSS planners determine the number and type of AMALs and
ADALs required to support the operation. Additional consumable Class VIII materiel is positioned
for deployment with the LCE’s supply section. After the consumable AMALs and ADALs are

Patient Movement Items
Patient movement items (PMIs) are medical equipment and supplies required to support the
patient during evacuation (e.g., ventilators, oxygen, vital sign monitors, blankets). Handling and

logistic infrastructure to ensure PMIs are available and can be serviced. Plan for a PMI exchange

Command-approved PMIs. These items accompany a patient throughout the chain of evacuation
from originating MTF to destination MTF, whether it is an intra- or inter-theater transfer. Services
obtain and maintain initial quantities of PMIs in the appropriate medical assemblages.

                     CHAPTER 5.
             COMMAND AND CONTROL

The complexities of an operational environment, myriad medical functions and assets, and the
requirement to provide health care throughout the competition continuum to diverse populations
(i.e., United States, joint, interagency, intergovernmental, multinational, host nation, and civilian)
necessitates a medical command authority that uses scarce medical resources available to their full
potential and capability. The medical C2 capability is flexible, versatile, and capable of providing
medical support ashore and afloat to support current and future operations. The medical C2
capability integrates vertically and horizontally with the command and control unit commander to
enhance situational awareness. These C2 capabilities develop, process, and disseminate orders
and information to subordinate units and provide the means to receive and implement orders from
higher authorities.

COMMAND AND CONTROL ORGANIZATION

Health service support is embedded in each MAGTF element. However, available communications
assets vary and HSS shares these assets with other warfighting functions. In the Marine Corps, the
same radios that carry fire support or command information also carry medical information, and
commanders and HSS personnel use the same C2 systems. There is not a separate or dedicated
medical network in the tactical Marine Corps communications architecture.

Units responsible for providing detachments C2 support, for HSS, operate under the staff
cognizance of the G-6/S-6 of the supported unit. Marines assigned to these units, in concert with
the personnel assigned to the G-6/S-6 sections and HSS organizations, ensure that an effective
communications and information systems (CIS) network is planned, installed, operated, and
maintained to assure a comprehensive communication PACE plan.

COMMUNICATIONS AND INFORMATION SYSTEMS

The CIS network provides management and decision support tools for the HSS commander and
staff to collect, transport, process, disseminate, and protect voice, data, and information. A CIS
network includes tactical, single-channel high frequency, very high frequency, super-high
frequency, and ultrahigh frequency radios; tactical and commercial telephones; multichannel digital
systems; satellite communications; cryptographic equipment; and data systems for classified and
unclassified local area network or wide area network connectivity. All these systems are used by
assigned units and are allocated in accordance with the commander's priorities.

                     CHAPTER 6.
                       PLANNING

Comprehensive planning is essential in any operation. The course of action analysis, based on the
commander’s intent, should include casualty estimates and risk mitigation strategies for MAGTF
operational support. For planning purposes, Annexes K and Q should reflect intended
coordination by and between HSS and the G-6/S-6.

Health service support planning must be continuously directed toward accomplishing missions
and tasks and be flexible to accommodate changes. The HSS planning must include mitigating
factors to FHP threats and provide medical capability according to anticipated wounded in action,
DNBIs, and human remains within the HSS system. Commanders and their staffs develop an
efficient plan for a MAGTF operation, which depends on a combined and coordinated effort of all
involved. Because MAGTF operations are inherently complex, planning at every level must
provide as complete, functional, and flexible support as possible with available resources. Having
a clear understanding of staff planning is based on knowledge of characteristics, methods, and
objectives of the overall operational planning process.

MARINE CORPS PLANNING PROCESS

The Marine Corps Planning Process establishes procedures for—

  • Problem framing.
  • Developing courses of action (COAs) against the threat.
  • Wargaming COAs using the enemy’s most likely, dangerous, and greatest advantage

     (against friendly forces) COAs.
  • Comparison and decision on COA.
  • Developing an OPORD or OPLAN based on commander’s selection.
  • Transitioning an OPORD or OPLAN for execution to those tasked.

The Marine Corps Planning Process organizes these procedures into six steps. Using these steps,
the commander and supporting staff can organize their planning activities and transmit plans to
subordinates and assigned commands, thereby sharing a common understanding of the mission
and commander’s intent. See Marine Corps Warfighting Publication (MCWP) 5-10, Marine
Corps Planning Process, for more information on the Marine Corps Planning Process.

SEQUENCE OF COMMAND AND STAFF PLANNING FOR HEALTH SERVICE SUPPORT

Health service support planning begins with the commander’s planning guidance issuance to the
supporting staff. The commander’s guidance includes mission analysis, factors to consider, and
COAs to analyze. The MAGTF surgeon’s staff participates in COA development and wargaming
each COA. Also, they prepare a comprehensive estimate regarding HSS factors affecting mission
accomplishment in light of each proposed COA. The HSS estimate provides the commander with
a portion of the information considered when deciding a COA. After considering and comparing
each COA against established criteria and against each other, the commander selects a COA that
best accomplishes the mission. Health service support planning continues in detail after the
commander announces a decision and the CONOPS. Much of the material included in the HSS
plan has been developed before preparing the medical estimate. This material should be reviewed,
refined, and updated as necessary.

Medical Estimate
The medical estimate requires significant research along with concurrent, parallel, and detailed
planning. Effective planning requires thorough analysis and research to support the decision-making
process. The medical estimate—

  • Assesses medical capabilities and limitations.
  • Determines if medical capabilities are sufficient to support the proposed COAs.
  • Determines which COA is most desirable from a medical standpoint.
  • Determines what measures the commander and supporting staff must take to overcome

     limiting factors.

The actual format used is directed by the cognizant G-3/S-3 and is based on guidance from higher
headquarters. It is imperative that staff estimates and estimates of supportability are reviewed
throughout the planning process. Estimates include problem framing, COA development, COA
war game, and COA comparison and decision. When possible, refer to the CCDR casualty
estimates or use the Medical Planners’ Toolkit (MPTk). See MCWP 5-10 for information on the
planning process and sample staff estimate development.

Decisions and Recommendations
The MAGTF surgeon prepares an HSS estimate that provides analysis and identifies advantages
and disadvantages, from a medical view, on each proposed COA. The MAGTF surgeon
recommends a COA based on this analysis.

Health Service Support Concept of Operations
The commander considers the results of wargaming, examines staff estimates, and determines a
desired COA. At this point, the MAGTF surgeon develops an HSS plan and a CONOPS to support
the commander’s decision. This plan contains information and instructions outlining HSS support
requirements for the MAGTF operation.

TRANSITION: PHASES OF EXPEDITIONARY OPERATIONS

Health service support planning for expeditionary operations can be divided into the following
five phases:

  • Predeployment.
  • Deployment.
  • Entry.
  • Enabling and decisive actions.
  • Redeployment.

Predeployment
Pre-deployment actions include organizing the deployment to ensure that forces arrive in the
objective area in a logical sequence, at the right time, and with the correct equipment and
sustainment to support CONOPS. Regardless of the deployment mode, pre-deployment
preparation and deployment are complex undertakings that require accomplishing numerous
tasks. In crisis response situations, the time available to accomplish these tasks is limited. Some
HSS pre-deployment considerations are—

  • Conducting pre-deployment health screening to ensure personnel are medically ready.
  • Staffing and training HSS components supporting the CONOPS.
  • Maintaining HSS equipment and supplies in sufficient condition and quantity for the mission.
  • Ensuring responsive resupply mechanisms are in place.
  • Establishing liaison with MAGTF, joint, and multinational components.
  • Procuring and administering required immunizations and medicines for endemic diseases.
  • Identifying HNS, nongovernmental organizations, and international organizations.
  • Providing unit training in personal hygiene and field sanitation.
  • Ensuring pre-deployment health threat briefs and health-screening questionnaires

     are completed.
  • Assisting medical units in completing pre-deployment requirements, specifically

     immunizations, and making preparations for embarkation of PVNTMED equipment.
  • Making sure HSS personnel organic to the MAGTF embark with their units.
  • Verifying mobile loading of BAS, surgical platoon, and FRSS equipment and supplies

     is completed.
  • Ensuring HSS personnel and Class VIII materiel are adequately allocated to the extent

     possible among ATF ships for amphibious operations. However, this should not be
     accomplished at the expense of separating personnel from equipment they will be using
     ashore. It is important that personnel from HSSEs embark on vessels carrying their equipment
     and supplies.
  • Verifying all medical personnel, equipment, and supplies are registered on the Joint Operation
     Planning Execution System time-phased force and deployment data.

    Deployment
    Deployment planning and execution are challenges for even the most experienced and skilled
    logisticians and HSS personnel. Transportation modes vary depending on the size, purpose, and
    duration of deployment. A MEF deployment uses various transportation modes to include—

       • Military or commercial ground transportation.
       • Amphibious or commercial ships.
       • Air mobility command or commercial charter airlift for personnel, supplies, and equipment.

    Entry
    Entry refers to the initial introduction of forces onto foreign soil where no prior presence exists.
    During this phase, expeditionary forces are often at greatest risk and the introduction of forces is
    often a complicated military evolution. The most difficult type of entry is forcible entry, which
    does not have the established medical capabilities to render treatment necessary for expected
    casualties. The HSSE must remain flexible and responsive during this period of an
    expeditionary operation.

    Amphibious Operations. An amphibious operation is, “a military operation launched from sea, by
    an amphibious task force to conduct landing force operations within the littorals” (DoD Dictionary).
    The five phases of an amphibious operation are planning, embarkation, rehearsal, movement, and
    action. Sound planning provides a basis for successful execution. The HSS personnel should—

       • Test the adequacy of HSS plans.
       • Test regulating networks and medical C2 networks.
       • Check individual readiness.
       • Ensure all HSS personnel are familiar with the OPLAN and Annex Q.

    Maritime Pre-Positioning Force Operations. An MPF operation is, “the rapid deployment and
    assembly of a Marine expeditionary force in a secure area using a combination of intratheater
    airlift, and forward-deployed maritime pre-positioning ships” (DoD Dictionary). A maritime
    pre-positioning ship (MPS) operation is a strategic deployment option that is global in nature,

naval in character, and suitable for employment in various circumstances. The essential strategic
contribution of MPF operations is the mobility and flexibility that allows the rapid concentration
of forces in a designated area.

Amphibious and MPF operations have complementary activities. Amphibious operations provide
the means for forcible entry, while MPF operations permit rapid deployment into permissive
areas, where force introduction is essentially unopposed and is expected to remain so throughout

In an MPF operation, a MAGTF and Navy support element personnel are airlifted into a

  • Providing first-responder care upon landing.
  • Obtaining host-nation and inter-Service support.
  • Coordinating transportation of personnel, equipment, and supplies.
  • Inventorying equipment and supplies for serviceability.
  • Identifying resupply sources.

Air Contingency Force Operations. Air contingency force operations are task-organized,
air-deployable forces. An air contingency MAGTF has lead elements ready to deploy within
hours of notification; these elements deploy to a secure area for subsequent employment. Air
contingency forces can be used as part of the fly-in echelon of an MPF, as reinforcement for an
amphibious force, or as the lead MEF element. Organic HSS and its equipment are part of the
air contingency force.

Enabling and Decisive Actions
Enabling actions refer to those preparatory actions taken by the expeditionary force after entry
and mission establishment. Follow-on forces typically follow initial forces, which have
undertaken enabling actions for the eventual decisive actions to take place. Enabling actions can
include seizing a port, airfield, or other lodgment to facilitate the secure introduction of follow-on
forces. In cases of disaster or disruption, enabling actions usually involve the initial restoration of
order or stability. In open warfare situations, enabling actions can involve operations that halt or
delay an enemy advance, attack certain enemy military capabilities, or to capture key terrain
necessary for conducting decisive operations.

Decisive actions are actions units take to create favorable conditions for accomplishing the
mission. In disasters, decisive actions include relief operations. In disruptions, they often include
peacemaking and peacekeeping until local government control can be re-established. In conflict,
they usually involve the military defeating the enemy’s fighting forces. These enabling and
decisive actions require HSS planning and support to varying degrees based on mission,
conditions, and forces applied.

    Redeployment
    Expeditions are temporary by definition, and expeditionary operations involve a departure of the
    expeditionary force or a transition to a permanent presence. Redeployment could be required
    when the mission changes; there are additional missions within the theater or in another theater;
    or when forces return to the base of origin. The MAGTF’s organic sustainment and its ability to
    quickly reorganize and reconstitute without having to first return to its home base or wait for a
    theater logistic infrastructure to be established make it unique. Redeployment requires additional
    HSS planning and resupply, so the medical capabilities are available for follow-on missions.

    Sustainment. The MAGTF’s versatility stems from its naval, expeditionary character, which
    enables it to respond quickly to contingencies and shift rapidly between missions in littoral
    regions around the world. A MAGTF has this capability even when there is no logistic
    infrastructure ashore and before resupply channels from CONUS have been established. The
    MAGTF’s organic sustainment capability allows units to conduct independent operations for
    60 days without resupply. Since the MAGTF is a lighter force, and it can operate with a small
    footprint within an austere environment, it is more easily sustained, places less demand on lift
    capabilities, and simplifies problems associated with redeployment.

    Reconstitution. A MAGTF’s endurance, is based on its accompanying sustainment and its ability
    to maneuver at sea, which makes it ideally suited for reconstitution and redeployment out of
    theater within the limits of accompanying supplies. Commanders must ensure that requisitions for
    replenishment are submitted commensurate with anticipated lead times for delivery.
    Reconstitution in theater requires resupply to rebuild baseline levels of sustainment.

   HEALTH SERVICE SUPPORT PLANNING CONSIDERATIONS

    Evaluations of data from World War II, Korea, Vietnam, Operation DESERT STORM,
    Operation IRAQI FREEDOM, and Operation ENDURING FREEDOM have led to development
    of the following guidelines concerning combat HSS:

       • Commanders at all levels are responsible for providing adequate and proper health care
         to individuals.

       • Medical support can be effectively executed only if they are based upon realistic
         HSS capabilities.

       • Health care for patients must be continuous from the onset of injury or disease through the
         roles of care to the facility that can provide final treatment and disposition.

       • Casualty triage must be conducted at the point of injury and at each medical facility in the
         chain of evacuation; patients are triaged at a receiving MTF and moved rearward only to the
         point patients can receive definitive care (based on their condition).

       • Marine Corps HSS units must be prepared to move quickly.
       • Health service support units must disperse, to protect assets, personnel, and to render the best

         medical service possible.
       • Flexible plans ensure the mission can be accomplished despite changes in the

         tactical situation.

               PREVENTIVE MEDICINE

Disease, nonbattle injuries, and occupational environmental health (OEH) hazards can have
a rapid and widespread effect on a military organization’s effectiveness. Preventive medicine
efforts can dramatically reduce the incidence of these hazards during military operations.
Proactive PVNTMED measures should be incorporated into all levels of care. Real-time disease
surveillance data and results from OEH monitoring should be used to brief commanders on
potential medical and environmental threats and on the effectiveness of PVNTMED measures.
An HSSE’s critical function is preventing DNBI. For more information on PVNTMED
measures refer to Navy Medical (NAVMED) P-5010-9, Manual of Naval Preventive Medicine.

PREDEPLOYMENT

Specific PVNTMED tasks and efforts units should conduct before deployment are as follows:

  • Collect and disseminate entomology, environmental, occupational, and epidemiological
     information, on the theater of operations, to the supported commander, HSS units, and
     MAGTF elements that might be affected for planning and potential action as changes occur.

  • Conduct pre-deployment OEHSA, including preliminary health hazard site survey during
     pre-deployment site survey, to identify potential OEH threats to deployed personnel.

  • Recommend personnel augmentation requirements to support the OPLAN.
  • Recommend immunizations and other PVNTMED measures to counter medical threats

     within theater and in-transit staging points.
  • Train individuals (i.e., via unit training) in personal hygiene, personal protective measures,

     protection equipment, and field sanitation practices.
  • Assist medical units in completing pre-deployment requirements (e.g., immunization,

     PVNTMED threat briefings, permethrin) and in preparing to embark PVNTMED equipment.
  • Conduct health threat briefings when threats are identified and when countermeasures

     are required.
  • Conduct food and water vulnerability assessments.

                     CHAPTER 8.
                PATIENT MOVEMENT

FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES

The primary mission of the DoD patient movement system is to transport US military casualties
to the appropriate level of care within the combat zone or out of theater as required. Timely
patient movement serves an important role in HSS and supports the joint task force’s CONOPS.
Patient movement is a system that provides a continuum of care and coordinates the movement
of patients from the site of injury or onset of disease, through successive levels of care, to meet
the needs of the patient. Each Service component has an organic patient movement capability
and is responsible for patient evacuation from point of injury to initial treatment at a health care
facility. See MCRP 3-40A.7, Patient Movement, for more information.

Casualty evacuation is, “the unregulated movement of casualties aboard any vehicle”
(DoD Dictionary). Medical evacuation traditionally refers to Army, Navy, Marine Corps,
and Coast Guard patient movement using dedicated or pre-designated tactical or logistic aircraft,
boats, ships, and other watercraft temporarily equipped and staffed with medical personnel for
en route care; however, the Army has historically employed dedicated air and ground evacuation
assets. Aeromedical evacuation specifically refers to Air Force fixed-wing movement of
regulated casualties using organic and contracted mobility airframes with aeromedical
evacuation aircrews trained explicitly for the mission. In addition, the civil reserve air fleet can
be used for aeromedical evacuation missions.

Dedicated medical evacuation assets are configured for patient movement, externally marked
with a red cross, and specifically reserved to support the MEDEVAC mission. Dedicated
MEDEVAC provides a specific level of en route care capability to prevent degradation of patient
conditions during transport. All dedicated MEDEVAC assets are authorized protection under the
Geneva Conventions and ground ambulances are the only dedicated MEDEVAC assets in the
Marine Corps.

Designated CASEVAC assets are non-medical patient movement assets, not externally marked with
a red cross, but configured and allocated for patient movement. Similar to dedicated MEDEVAC,
designated CASEVAC provides a specific level of en route care capability to prevent degradation of
patient conditions during transport and are not afforded protection under the Geneva Conventions.

More information on the HSS system and specific tactics, techniques, and procedures for naval
expeditionary force medical regulating for joint operations is provided in MCRP 3-40A.7. It also
addresses Navy and Marine Corps patient movement capabilities available to an operational
commander and gives patient movement recommendations for theater operations, mission
planning, and training.

   EN ROUTE CARE CAPABILITY

    En route care ensures the continuation of care during patient movement within the HSS
    continuum of care without clinically compromising the patient’s condition. Patient movement
    involves transitory medical care, patient holding, and staging capabilities during transport from
    the site of injury or onset of disease to a level of care to meet the patient’s needs. Each Service
    component has an organic patient movement capability for evacuation from point of injury to
    initial treatment at an MTF.

    Both CASEVAC and MEDEVAC are accomplished through litter carries, manual carries, ground
    transportation, and limited air (i.e., fixed- or rotary-wing) transport. The Army generally employs
    dedicated patient evacuation assets, such as ground and air ambulances. The Navy typically relies
    on lifts of opportunity. The Marine Corps has some dedicated ground evacuation and relies on
    opportune lift for air transport.

   DECISION MAKING

    The level of care provided to sick and wounded in any area of combat is greatly influenced by
    prevailing tactical situations. Conditions are seldom static and success in combat must remain
    the primary goal of combat, combat support, and combat service support units. This calls for a
    dynamic casualty management and decision-making process that must be applied at all levels
    within the patient movement system.

    Casualty Sorting (Triage)
    Triage is an effective casualty priority sorting process and is essential to any successful patient
    movement system. Rapid evaluations are made to identify which patients need immediate
    resuscitation and which patients can tolerate delay in treatment. Deciding which patients should be
    moved after initial treatment to a higher-level of care is equally important. Additionally, reverse
    triage (identifying terminal patients) is necessary to free up resources, preserving critical supplies for
    patients who have treatable injuries.

    Medical Management
    Under combat conditions, the flow of sick and wounded patients puts varying pressures on the
    capabilities of medical facilities. Medical personnel responsible for decisions on moving individual
    casualties work closely with logistics officers charged with implementing patient evacuation.

   MEDICAL EVACUATION ASSETS

    The Marine Corps’ assigned mission dictates the number and types of aircraft required to
    accomplish all tasks. Assault support tasks are typically the priority. All Marine Corps tilt-rotor,

    number of aircraft in the Marine Corps inventory precludes, in most cases, assigning dedicated
    CASEVAC and MEDEVAC aircraft. Allocating aircraft to perform the CASEVAC mission would
    be the MAGTAF commander’s decision. Assets are designated to perform CASEVAC missions
    through implied mission tasking or by assuming a standby posture.

            DENTAL SERVICE SUPPORT

The dental battalion, within the MLG, is organized and equipped to provide a range of dental
support to the MEF. In garrison, the dental battalion provides comprehensive care for the
operating forces to assist the MTF in maintaining optimal dental readiness and health for
deployable units. While deployed, dental service support detachments return dental casualties to
duty, and sustaining dental readiness for operational forces. Based on mission requirements,
dental service support uses flexible, lightweight, and expeditionary dental equipment and task-
organized dental detachments to support deployed unit dental care requirements.

TENETS

Dental service support offers substantial operational dental support with particular attention to
prevention, return to duty, scalability, and forward care.

Prevention
Taking preventive measures to reduce disease and injury is the most resource-efficient means
individuals can use to maintain good dental health. Dental emergencies are reduced when
individuals receive appropriate treatment and continue good individual oral hygiene.

Return to Duty
The primary objective of operational dentistry is to rapidly treat dental needs and return personnel
to their unit without degrading mission performance.

Scalable Dental Support
Dental battalion has command and control of all dental personnel in garrison and deploys scalable
task-organized components providing dental support to any size MAGTF.

Enhanced Forward Care
Enhanced forward care reduces time and resources required to evacuate an individual for dental
care. Dental section detachments provide support as far forward as required. Each ADAL contains
a subassembly dental unit that has two portable, dental lightweight load-carrying equipment
backpacks to provide emergency dental care. Refer to Appendix A for information on ADAL.

The field dental care system maintains dental readiness for deployed forces by preventing and
treating dental disease and injury. Dental support is based on a task-organized and flexible
structure that responds to rapidly changing conditions. This task-organized support is based on—

  • Size of unit supported.
  • Deployment duration.
  • Mission type (e.g., stabilization activities, humanitarian assistance, armed conflict).
  • Other dental support requirements.

Consistent with the tactical situation, the spectrum of dental care provided ranges from expedient
emergency care to the full range of essential nonemergency care.

Dental Platoon-Ground Unit
The dental platoon-ground unit consists of six dental officers, nine Corpsmen, and six ADALs.
This unit is the dental component of the LCE within a MEB. Typically, assigned to a surgical
company, the dental platoon-ground unit can effectively function attached to another unit organic
to the LCE. The LCE exercises operational control (OPCON) over the dental platoon-ground unit,
and it is responsible for logistics and security support.

Dental Platoon-Air Unit
The dental platoon-air unit consists of four wing detachments. Each wing detachment has one
dental officer, two Corpsmen, and one ADAL. This unit is attached to the MWSS and provides
dental support to personnel assigned to the ACE. The ACE exercises OPCON over the dental
platoon-air and is responsible for logistics and security support.

One dental platoon-ground unit combined with one dental platoon-air unit supports a MEB-level
force and provides emergency and essential non-emergency dental support to approximately 8,000
to 12,000 personnel using ADALs, with enough supplies to support a defined stream of patients
for one month (estimated at 1,700 dental casualties).

Dental Section
The smallest dental support module is a dental section, which consists of one dental officer, one
Corpsman, and one ADAL. The dental section is the dental component of the LCE within a
MAGTF. The LCE exercises OPCON over the dental section and is responsible for all logistics
and security support. The dental section provides emergency and essential non-emergency dental
support to approximately 2,200 personnel using the ADAL to support a defined stream of patients
for one month (estimated at 175 dental casualties).

   PREVENTIVE DENTISTRY

    Oral health, as evident in the absence of dental disease, is the ultimate goal in dentistry. Dental
    disease increases under deployment conditions: stress, exhaustion, inadequate nutrition, and poor
    oral hygiene measures. Dental healthcare providers use a range of treatment and preventive
    programs to eliminate oral conditions that decrease performance and increase absence from duty.
    Before deployment, deploying personnel are provided with priority appointments to treat urgent
    dental conditions. Dental prophylaxis and individual oral hygiene instruction are provided to
    ensure every Marine has the knowledge and skills to maintain oral health. During deployment,
    dental service support provides ongoing dental treatment and preventive dentistry programs to
    identify and treat personnel who might be at risk for dental disease.

Field Oral Hygiene Instruction
Deployable personnel should receive additional oral hygiene instruction specific to a field
environment. Field oral hygiene information includes the following:

  • Importance of oral hygiene to combat fitness.
  • Use of fluoridated toothpaste.
  • Alternative dental-hygiene methods when there are no garrison-type facilities.
  • Procedures on how to seek dental services while deployed.

Infection Control
Infection control is a critical requirement in a field environment. The demands for proper infection
control are often greater than in garrison due to undesirable environmental conditions. Specific
guidance found in DHA-PM 6025.13, Volume 2, Patient Safety-Infection Prevention & Control,
can be adapted to field environment.

Infectious and Medical Waste Management
Proper handling and disposal of waste is required to protect the force, environment, and to fulfill
agreements with the host nation. The unit generating waste ensures proper collection and disposal.
Assistance is available through the supporting engineer unit, preventive medicine team, or local
MTF. Medical waste, such as blood, blood products, and surgical waste, are produced during
dental procedures. Medical waste can be sterilized and disposed of along with general waste.
Fluid wastes collected through oral evacuation apparatus should be disposed of along with human
wastes. Contaminated needles and other sharp items requiring special handling should be stored in
clearly marked, puncture-proof containers with a tight-fitting lid until disposal. Large quantities of
medical waste should be collected in impervious containers if available, or double plastic bags as
an alternative. Containers must be clearly marked with a universal biological hazard symbol or
labeled consistent with the unit standing operating procedure.

Hazardous Waste
Dental service support typically generates small quantities of hazardous waste in a field
environment and should be disposed of in a manner that minimizes environmental impact.
Engineer personnel and PVNTMED can advise dental service support on proper waste disposal.

ALTERNATE DENTAL SERVICE SUPPORT ROLES

In addition to providing dental services to deployed units, dental service support can provide
services to support an OPLAN. Dental care is provided to operating forces and other designated
beneficiaries (e.g., civilian, indigenous population, detainee, enemy prisoner of war) in theater.
Priority for treatment is based on command directives, patient’s dental condition, available
resources, negotiated agreements, and applicable laws and conventions.

Dental civic action projects are generally associated with host-nation assistance, but they can
support more conventional conflicts, such as a civil affairs operation.

                    CHAPTER 10.
             CHEMICAL, BIOLOGICAL,
   RADIOLOGICAL, AND NUCLEAR DEFENSE

Military personnel must be prepared to operate in a contaminated environment. Additionally, HSS
personnel must be prepared to provide patient care in a CBRN environment. Decontaminating
patients and transportation assets causes evacuation delays, making first aid and patient care even
more critical. Because staffing HSS units is based on conventional warfare requirements, these
units are challenged to provide effective HSS in CBRN environments. All CBRN actions cause
high casualty rates, materiel losses, obstacles to maneuver, and contamination. Mission-oriented
protective posture (referred to as MOPP) levels 3 and 4 result in body heat buildup, reduced
mobility, and degraded senses (e.g., sight, touch, hearing), ultimately reducing unit effectiveness.

Contamination is a major problem in providing HSS in a CBRN environment. To decrease
potential for CBRN contamination, commanders can use alarm and detection equipment, unit
dispersion, overhead shelters, shielding materials, protective covers, collective protection shelters,
and chemical agent resistant coatings. On a contaminated battlefield, the focus is on keeping
Service members mission capable. Effective and efficient triage, emergency treatment,
decontamination, and contamination control save lives, assures prompt evacuation, and increases
the return-to-duty rate. See MCRP 3-40A.6, Multi-Service Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures
for Health Service Support in a Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear Environment.

PREPARATIONS BEFORE A CHEMICAL, BIOLOGICAL, RADIOLOGICAL, AND NUCLEAR ATTACK

Before a CBRN attack, HSS personnel must train to survive the attack, function in the
environment, and to effectively care for CBRN casualties. Health service support personnel must
keep their immunizations current, use available preventive treatment against suspected agents,
pretreat for suspected agents, and have antidotes and essential Class VIII supplies readily
available for potential chemical or biological agents.

The best defense for HSS personnel is protecting themselves, their patients, Class VIII supplies,
and equipment by following procedures to avoid contamination. Also, HSS personnel must ensure
that stored Class VIII supplies and equipment are in protected areas or storage containers with
covers secured. One method to protect supplies and equipment is to keep them in their shipping
containers until needed. When time permits, upon receiving warnings that a CBRN attack is
imminent or downwind hazard exists, HSS personnel should seek protected areas such as
basements, culverts, and ravines for themselves and their patients.

   SHELTER DURING A CHEMICAL, BIOLOGICAL, RADIOLOGICAL, AND NUCLEAR ATTACK

    During a CBRN attack HSS personnel and their patients should remain in the best available
    protected areas and take positions within a shelter, away from windows and other openings, and
    move out of these positions only when notified it is safe to do so. When there is no higher authority,
    HSS personnel should use caution in their movements and assume all casualties entering a medical
    unit after a CBRN attack are contaminated unless certified as non-contaminated.

   RESPONSE AFTER A CHEMICAL, BIOLOGICAL, RADIOLOGICAL, AND NUCLEAR ATTACK

    After a CBRN attack, HSS personnel must survey their equipment to determine the extent of damage
    and their ability to continue the mission. All patients receiving treatment must be checked for CBRN
    contamination. Also, HSS personnel should expect disorientation in patients and unit personnel.
    Unless life-, limb-, or eyesight-threatening conditions exist, patients are decontaminated before
    treatment to reduce hazards to HSS personnel. Patients requiring treatment before decontamination
    are treated in the HSS decontamination station area. See MCRP 3.40A.1 Multi-Service Tactics,
    Techniques, and Procedures for Treatment of Chemical Warfare Agent Casualties and Conventional
    Military Chemical Injuries, for more information on how to medically treat patients.

             NOTE: Cardiac arrest in a CBRN environment should immediately be a black tag.
             Massive hemorrhage and respiratory distress are conditions that might require
             treatment in the decontamination area.

    When evaluating patients, HSS personnel must be vigilant and consider the following in their
    evaluation of patients:

       • Nuclear-induced injuries are immediately observable and typically include thermal burns or
         blast injuries.

       • Radiation-induced injuries might not be observable until a few hours or days after the attack.
       • Chemically induced injuries are immediately observable when individuals are exposed to

         the agent; however, blister-agent patients can show signs of exposure immediately or up to
         14 days later.
       • Biologically induced injuries or illnesses might not become apparent for hours to days
         after exposure.

       • Well-constructed fighting positions with overhead cover and expedient shelters (e.g., reinforced

  • Armored vehicles provide protection against nuclear weapon blast and radiation effects.
     Casualties generated in a nuclear attack will likely suffer multiple injuries (e.g., combinations
     of blast, thermal, and radiation injuries) that complicate HSS. The three categories of nuclear
     radiation casualties are—
      Irradiated Casualties. The irradiated casualty has been exposed to ionizing radiation but is
        not contaminated. These casualties are not radioactive and pose no radiation threat to
        medical care providers. Casualties who have suffered exposure to initial nuclear radiation
        are also in this category.
      Externally Contaminated Casualties. The externally contaminated casualty has radioactive
        dust and debris on clothing, skin, or hair. This presents a problem for the MTF similar to
        the lice-infested patient arriving at a peacetime MTF, in that it can present a threat to HSS
        personnel. The externally contaminated casualty is decontaminated at the earliest
        opportunity. Lifesaving care is always rendered before decontamination.
      Internally Contaminated Casualties. The internally contaminated casualty has ingested or
        inhaled radioactive materials, or radioactive material has entered the body through an open
        wound. The radioactive material continues to irradiate the casualty internally until radioactive
        decay and biological elimination remove the radioactive isotope. Attending HSS personnel
        are shielded, to some degree, by the patient’s body. Inhaling, ingesting, or injecting
        radioactive material sufficient to present a threat to medical care providers is highly unlikely.

RADIOLOGICAL ENVIRONMENT

Decontaminating patients in a radiological environment is performed in a similar manner to that
of chemical decontamination. Typically, radiation-induced injuries are observed within a few
hours or days after the attack. The initial management of a casualty contaminated by radiological
agents is to perform all immediate life-, limb-, or eyesight-saving actions without regard to
contamination. See MCRP 3-40A.2, Multi-Service Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures for
Treatment of Nuclear and Radiological Casualties, for more information.

BIOLOGICAL ENVIRONMENT

A biological attack can be difficult to recognize because, it often does not have an immediate
effect on exposed personnel. Health service support personnel must monitor for biological warfare
indicators, to include the following:

  • An increase in disease incidences or fatality rates.
  • A sudden presentation of an exotic disease.
  • Other sequential epidemiological events, particularly when presented in lines

     of communications.

Passive defensive measures (e.g., immunizations, good personal hygiene, physical conditioning,
insect repellents, protective masks, good sanitation practices) lessen the effects of most
biological intrusions.

    Handling chemically contaminated patients is challenging for HSS units. The vapor hazard
    associated with contaminated patients can require HSS personnel to remain at mission-oriented
    protective posture level four for a time; therefore, HSS personnel must locate clean areas for
    surgical companies to provide treatment to patients. The surgical company should operate in a
    contaminated environment only until HSS personnel have the time and means to move the
    company to a clean area.

   MEDICAL EVACUATION

    In a CBRN environment, unit commanders must consider what evacuation assets are committed
    to the contaminated area. If operating in a contaminated area, most medical evacuation assets
    must operate there; however, efforts should be made to keep some ambulances from
    becoming contaminated.

    There are three basic modes to evacuate casualties: personnel, ground vehicles, and aircraft.
    Commanders must recognize the constraints CBRN warfare places on CASEVAC during
    operations and then plan and train to overcome these deficiencies.

   PERSONNEL CONSIDERATIONS

    Medical treatment requirements increase when operating in a CBRN contaminated environment.
    As a result, replacing HSS personnel might be needed. Following a CBRN attack, plans must
    include efforts to conserve available HSS personnel and resources. Generally, HSS personnel
    provide emergency medical treatment, advanced trauma management, and more definitive
    treatment as time and resources permit. To provide definitive care, personnel must be able to wear
    long sleeves. Non-HSS personnel conduct search and rescue activities for injured or wounded
    individuals; they can also provide immediate first aid and decontamination.

                    CHAPTER 11.
         COMBAT CASUALTY REPORTING

The G-1/S-1 section is responsible for submitting prompt, accurate, and complete casualty
reports to higher headquarters. The Defense Casualty Information Processing System is a
single-standard system supporting uniform procedures, accounting, and accurate reporting of
casualties; ensuring support to family members; benefits tracking; coordinating mortuary affairs;
and returning personal effects and human remains. During combat operations, casualty tracking
information can be obtained from the role 3 level of care using the US Transportation Command
Regulating and Command and Control Evacuation System (TRAC2ES). This web-based system
facilitates responsive communications essential to patient movement and is used to maintain and
protect patient privacy and troop strength information. It replaced the defense medical regulating
information system, Automated Patient Evacuation System, and theater Army medical
management information system medical regulating module. Also, TRAC2ES provides global
support throughout the operational medical continuum: fixed and deployable MTFs, global
movement, and TPMRCs. Additionally, TRAC2ES links the global patient movement
requirements center and the TPMRCs. Reporting personnel must be familiar with casualty
reporting procedures. Marine Corps Order (MCO) 3040.4, Marine Corps Casualty Assistance
Program, and other local directives in the 3040 series addresses casualty reporting. The system
described in these directives is one in which personnel losses, regardless of cause, are reported
through the chain of command to a central location.

TACTICAL COMBAT CASUALTY CARE CARD

Hospital Corpsmen at the unit level are usually the first to record written information about a
casualty using the Department of Defense Form (DD Form 1380), a tactical combat casualty care
card depicted in Figure 11-1 and Figure 11-2. It is a casualty tag printed in a set that provides a
hardened original copy for attaching to the casualty, whether wounded or deceased. The HM, who
provided initial treatment to the casualty retains a carbon copy. The HM must render initial
casualty treatment promptly and deliver copies of TCCC cards to the unit commander or their
representative. Unit commanders need these documents for casualty reporting responsibilities
through their chain of command, MCO 3040.4 requires.

When initial casualty treatment is rendered at point of injury, a BAS, or other level of care, a
TCCC card is initiated, and a copy is forwarded through the facility’s chain of command.
Regarding whether a casualty is received with or without a TCCC card, the receiving facility
compiles a casualty list and, then they forward the list through their chain of command. A casualty
list is prepared at least once a day, and it should show each casualty received, held, evacuated, or
returned to duty during the reporting time.

                    CHAPTER 12.
                       TRAINING

The primary goal for military training is to achieve and maintain the highest level of preparation
possible, thereby, developing confidence among personnel and ensuring a high state of
readiness. Training enhances combat readiness, and it is equally important for HSS personnel.
This is particularly true given the reduced peacetime staffing of HSS units and difficulty of
obtaining realistic hands-on trauma training. The end result is, when mobilizing, some medical
personnel assigned to deploying HSS units are augmentees with varying field medical skill-set
and experience. Medical department personnel must, to the extent possible, be involved in each
phase of training and every exercise before deployment.

HEALTH SERVICE SUPPORT GOALS

Staffing and training constraints require efforts be directed toward developing a nucleus of
personnel highly skilled in techniques of establishing and effectively operating HSS units in the
field. This nucleus, when mobilized, becomes the core of each field medical unit and is tasked
with receiving augmentees and shaping them into effective health delivery teams. It is not possible
to lay out a training program that applies to every HSSD due to geographic, climatic, and mission
variations. Commanders of HSS units and other key medical department personnel must structure
training based on available personnel, achievable goals, and limiting factors.

Increase Individual Readiness
To the maximum degree funds and scheduling permits, efforts to increase and maintain readiness
should extend beyond currently assigned personnel and incorporate augmentees into training
evolutions. Surgeons and other key staff should encourage commanders to involve identified
augmentation personnel in exercises when possible. In addition to enhancing individual training,
this practice aids in fostering unit identity and cohesiveness amongst its members.

Standardized Methods and Procedures
Methods and procedures must be standardized before mobilization. Augmentees must be familiar
with and able to depend on well-developed standing operating procedures, including desktop
procedures, and procedural manuals.

TRAINING COURSES

The following training courses are essential in developing effective, efficient, and successful HSS
personnel. Course quota management and control are under the cognizance of Bureau of Medicine
and Surgery.

MCTP 3-40A, Health Service Support

    Field Medical Service Technician Course
    This course provides the fundamental knowledge and skills of TCCC to include care under fire,
    tactical field care, preparing for tactical evacuation, EFWBT, and prolonged casualty care.
    Emphasis is on identifying, treating, and evacuating combat trauma casualties in accordance with
    standards set forth by DHA and Joint Trauma System. Also included are military skills necessary to
    function and survive in a combat environment. Training includes the establishment and functions of
    a unit aid station, field sanitation and preventive medicine, medicine in a field environment, and
    CBRN defense medical requirements. Additionally, an understanding of general military subjects,
    such as individual and small-unit tactics, military drill, weapons familiarization (i.e., field stripping,
    cleaning, assembling and firing certain weapons), and physical training and conditioning. Upon
    graduation, students are awarded the Navy enlisted classification of field medial service technician.
    This course is required for all enlisted medical personnel before assignment in their first Marine
    Corps command. The course is available at either of two Field Medical Training Battalions located
    at Camp Lejeune, North Carolina and Camp Pendleton, California. Students receive a course quota
    as part of the detailing process.

    Field Medical Service Officer Course
    This course trains medical department officers in skills essential for duty with the Marine Corps
    operating forces. The emphasis is on resource management to treat casualties in a combat
    environment. It includes competency in medical planning, BAS management, PVNTMED, and
    health care aspects during Marine Corps operations. Also, individuals taking this course gain an
    understanding in Marine Corps leadership and culture, HSS, administrative management,
    weapons familiarization to include cleaning, assembling and firing the Service pistol, and physical
    training or conditioning. The course is available at either of two Field Medical Training Battalions
    located at Camp Lejeune, North Carolina and Camp Pendleton, California.

    Combat Casualty Care Course
    Before reporting to a Marine Corps unit, each medical department officer who is a clinical
    provider (i.e., Medical Corps, Dental Corps, and Nurse Corps) can attend an instruction course in
    advanced combat life support. In combat support operations, non-physician health care providers
    can be required to assist medical officers in presurgical preparation and wound management.
    Training is available through the Defense Medical Readiness Training Institute, Fort Sam
    Houston, Texas and Naval Operational Medical Institute in Pensacola, Florida.

    Tactical Combat Casualty Care Courses
    The standardized TCCC curriculum developed by DHA replaces all Service first aid, self-aid,
    buddy care, and field trauma training. The curriculum, published by DHA for each of the four
    role-based training tiers, represents the minimum learning objectives for certification and
    ensures training standardization across Services. Additional topics could be added to meet
    Service, mission, platform, or unit-specific requirements. See DoDI 1322.24, Medical Readiness
    Training, and Chief of Naval Operation Instruction (OPNAVINST) 1500.86, Tactical Combat
    Casualty Care, Training for more information on TCCC courses.

    Sick Call Screener Course
    Sick Call Screener Course consists of didactic classroom lectures, practical hands-on training in
    physical examination, physical diagnosis, and supervised clinical practicum. It may be necessary to
    complete this course over time, but training must not exceed 60 calendar days.

Joint EnRoute Care Course
The Joint Enroute Care Course provides concise, realistic, relevant, and current en route trauma
transport team training to joint and coalition forces flight medics, registered nurses, physician
assistants, and physicians conducting aeromedical operations, in rotary wing platforms, to
optimize en route patient outcomes. The Joint Enroute Care Course is a two-phase course:

  • Phase I. Mandatory distance learning modules covering aeromedical and aviation
     specific content.

  • Phase II. A 12-day residence course with additional didactic content, extensive hands-on
     practical exercises, and culminating with a comprehensive graded written and practical
     examinations on equipment and critical care transport simulation.

Flight Medic Course
The Flight Medic Course (also referred to as FMC) provides selected medical enlisted personnel
with knowledge and skills required to conduct CASEVAC, en route care, and MEDEVAC missions.
Training includes patient assessment, basic and advanced lifesaving interventions, in-flight
emergency procedures, and critical care transportation skills to stabilize and treat patients,
independent of a medical officer, from rotary or tilt-rotor aircraft. Course includes skills and
knowledge necessary to care for a patient up to 24 hours under a full spectrum, in the competition
continuum environment, independent of a medical officer. Graduates will have the capability to
identify and employ various aircraft systems, the “B Medical Kit”, conduct patient turnover, and
perform radio communications.

Plans, Operations, and Medical Intelligence Course
The Plans, Operations, and Medical Intelligence Course is designed as a three-week resident
training program that addresses strategies, concepts, and tools necessary for an officer and enlisted
staff to develop into a plans, operations, and medical intelligence assignment. Topics include—

  • Tactical, operational, and strategic levels of planning.
  • Doctrinal publications, exercise planning, medical intelligence, and federal response system,
  • Pertinent threat and capability briefs, and geographic and geo-political background briefs.
  • Joint HSS assets.
  • Armed Forces Blood Program.
  • Logistics.
  • Chain of command issues.
  • Communication methods.
  • Future initiatives.

Throughout the course, the class participates in a capstone project that culminates in the
development and presentation of Annex Q (Medical Services) of an operations order, that is based
upon a fictitious scenario mimicking real world events. The target audience is junior medical
service corps officers in the health care administration, physician's assistant, environmental
health, or industrial hygiene fields and senior enlisted personnel desiring to enter the plans,
operations, and medical intelligence field.

    Medical Regulators Course
    This course provides students with knowledge and skills required to function as medical
    regulating and patient evacuation team members. This course provides concepts and principles on
    medical regulating, patient movement, operations, HSS assets, planning, and communications
    unique to Navy and Marine Corps, as well as the joint operating arena.

    Joint Medical Operations Course (Basic and Advanced)
    The Joint Medical Operations Course (Basic) is a five-day course focused on joint and combined
    operational medical planning from point of injury or illness to the appropriate level of care during
    military operations. This course familiarizes students with policy guidance and information
    relevant to Joint Staff publications. The Joint Medical Operations Course (Advanced) is a five-day
    training course consisting of planning activities associated with joint military operations by
    CCDRs and their subordinate joint force commanders in response to the competition continuum.
    Additionally, an in-depth discussion is conducted on the comprehensive system providing uniform
    policies and procedures for reporting on the Armed Forces ability to fight and meet the national
    military strategy.

    Joint Medical Planning Tool
    Joint Medical Planning Tool (JMPT) is a five-day course that provides hands-on training on the
    JMPT and MPTk. Through a series of exercises utilizing the crawl, walk, run method, students
    use the JMPT and MPTk to develop a model demonstrating the flow of patients from point of
    injury through definitive care, planning implementation, and logistics strategies for mission
    success. The JMPT is where casualty estimates come from, which is done at a higher-headquarters
    level, not the unit level. This highly complex process requires medical subject matter expert
    support and higher-headquarters approval for official casualty estimates to be determined and
    published (usually classified).

    Amphibious Warfare Staff Planning Course
    The Amphibious Warfare Staff Planning Course provides Navy and Marine Corps officers and
    senior enlisted with the knowledge and skills required to plan amphibious operations. This
    training focuses on tasks performed by officers and senior enlisted assigned to primary and special
    staff sections of the amphibious ready group (also referred to as ARG) and MEU.

    Expeditionary Warfare School
    The Expeditionary Warfare School is a 41-week resident, or distant learning non-resident school,
    that provides career-level professional military education and training to company grade Marine
    officers, and selected officers from other Services and countries. Upon graduation, students are
    prepared to lead, in billets of increased responsibility, within the complex and distributed Naval
    expeditionary environment.

    Mountain Medicine and Cold-Weather Medicine
    Prepares medical personnel assigned to the FMF, or those who may augment it, with advanced
    technical and field skills needed to support units in complex, compartmentalized, mountainous
    terrain at high to very high altitudes, and in cold-weather environments. This course is designed to
    bring students to a high standard of technical and medical proficiency in skills peculiar to these
    environments. The course covers mountain movement; bivouac routine; medical planning and
    leadership; prevention, diagnosis, and how to treat high-altitude and cold-weather illness and
    injury; and techniques to transport casualties through this challenging environment.

COMMANDER’S RESPONSIBILITIES

Commanders are responsible for ensuring all assigned personnel receive appropriate training,
in military and technical subject areas, emergency medical training of nonmedical personnel,
and continued training of medical department personnel in field medical practices and related
military subjects. Commanders develop, conduct, and evaluate individual and collective HSS
training based on training standards published, by Training and Education Command, in the
NAVMC 3500.84B, Health Services Support Training and Readiness Manual. If standards are
not published, commanders ensure training is based on doctrinal, tactical, and technical
publications approved for Navy and Marine Corps use.

MEDICAL DEPARTMENT OFFICERS AND SENIOR ENLISTED

The division and wing surgeons maintain staff supervision over all medical training programs,
including exercise planning and execution, for medical department personnel assigned to their
respective subordinate commands. Within the MLG, the group surgeon and dental officer, with
the G-3, exercises staff cognizance over all general medical training programs. Medical and dental
battalion commanders are responsible for all battalion training programs, including internal
planning and execution. The MLG HSSO exercises staff cognizance of MLG planning and
exercises; all medical department officers and senior enlisted conduct and supervise HM’s training.

TYPES OF TRAINING

Individual Training
Individual training can be conducted in a formal school or at the member’s command. This
training prepares individuals to perform specific tasks related to general military duties or
assigned specialties. Medical department personnel who regularly perform their occupational
specialty develop increased confidence and proficiency. Individual training can include first aid,
cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and formal programs such as the Plans Operations Medical
Intelligence course; Joint Medical Planners Course; and Medical Regulating Course.

Unit Training
Unit training prepares an individual to function as a cohesive element within a unit. Such elements
can include teams, sections, platoons, companies, or MTF. Unit training for the FMF is typically
conducted afloat or in a field environment and involves the entire organization, including assigned
HSSEs. Unit training works best when conducted in a simulated combat environment. These
exercises involve setting up facilities in the field and improve skill-sets by performing hands-on
treatment on simulated casualties. Other field units, such as infantry battalions, regiments, combat
support, and combat service support organizations conduct field training exercises involving HSS
personnel organic to those units. Each operational unit is required to have two Marines trained as
combat lifesavers for each Corpsman on the unit tables of organization.

                    APPENDIX B.
                  BLOOD SUPPORT

The Joint Blood Program requires deploying medical forces that use blood and blood products for
their mission to write standing operating procedures to contact and integrate into the Armed Services
Blood Distribution System.

Armed Services Whole Blood Processing Laboratories (ASWBPL) are Air Force-managed,
tri-Service staffed, central repository for liquid and frozen blood required in peacetime,
contingencies, and war. An ASWBPL releases blood to unified commands upon approval by the
Armed Services Blood Program Office. Theater MTFs may not go directly to the ASWBPL for
blood. The ASWBPL is responsible for—

  • Retyping blood for A, B, and O blood types and Rhesus factor only.
  • Packing, icing, and preparing blood for shipment to theater.
  • Maintaining a peacetime inventory of 250 units of liquid blood to use as a rapid

     response requirement.

See MCO 6530.2, Department of the Navy Blood Program, for more information.

An HM can move blood forward for transfusion, if there is a proper transport container to use, and
the cold chain is preserved. Only group ‘O’ red blood cells (i.e., type-O), Rhesus factor positive or
negative are used.

The surgical platoon can store 120 units of type-O packed red blood cells (RBCs) in a field blood
refrigerator. Emergency collection capability exists if stored supply is exhausted, and no blood
bank testing is available.

The LHA or LHD ships can carry 400 frozen RBC units and 25 fresh frozen plasma (FFP) units
for contingency operations, and 950 frozen RBCs and 50 FFP for mobilization. The EMF can hold
90 units of liquid RBC units. Specific requirements are established for each mission. Emergency
collection capability exists if stored supply is exhausted.

Hospital ships can carry 2,375 frozen RBCs and 100 FFP units for contingency operations,
and 2,850 frozen RBC units and 120 FFP for mobilization.

    be stored at room temperature and used within 24 hours of collection (and then destroyed if not
    used), or it can be refrigerated within eight hours of collection. The most important safety
    consideration in transfusing whole blood is to ensure that the donor’s red blood cells and blood
    type is compatible with the recipient to avoid negative reactions. Organizations must have an
    effective blood donor screening process to implement the EFWBT process.

                        Section II. Terms and Definitions

aeromedical evacuation
        The movement of patients under medical supervision to and between medical treatment facilities by
        air transportation. Also called AE. (DoD Dictionary)

aviation combat element
        The core element of a Marine air- ground task force (MAGTF) that is task-organized to conduct aviation
        operations. The aviation combat element (ACE) provides all or a portion of the six functions of Marine
        aviation necessary to accomplish the MAGTF’s mission. These functions are antiair warfare, offensive
        air support, assault support, electronic warfare, air reconnaissance, and control of aircraft and missiles.
        The ACE is usually composed of an aviation unit headquarters and various other aviation units or their
        detachments. It can vary in size from a small aviation detachment of specifically required aircraft to one
        or more Marine aircraft wings. In a joint or multinational environment, the ACE may contain other
        Service or multinational forces assigned or attached to the MAGTF. The ACE itself is not a formal
        command. Also called ACE. See also Marine air-ground task force (USMC Dictionary)

casualty
        Any person who is lost to the organization by having been declared dead, duty status–whereabouts
        unknown excused absence whereabouts unknown, missing, ill, or injured. (DoD Dictionary)

casualty collection
        The assembly of casualties at collection and treatment sites. It includes protection from further injury
        while awaiting evacuation to the next level of care. Planning for casualty collection points must include
        site selection and staffing. (USMC Dictionary)

casualty evacuation
        (See DoD Dictionary for core definition. Marine Corps amplification follows.) The movement of the
        sick,wounded, or injured that begins at the point of injury or the onset of disease and includes movement
        both to and between medical treatment facilities. Also called CASEVAC. (USMC Dictionary)

casualty receiving and treatment ship
        In amphibious operations, a ship designated to receive, provide treatment for, and transfer casualties.
        Also called CRTS. (DoD Dictionary)

casualty treatment
        Treatment that includes triage and all levels of care from self-aid or buddy-aid through resuscitative care.
        (USMC Dictionary)

chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear defense
        Actions taken to counter chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear hazards; reduce their risks; and
        prepare for or respond to and recover from chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear incidents. Also
        called CBRN defense. (DoD Dictionary)

combat operations center
        The primary operational agency required to control the tactical operations of a command that employs
        ground and aviation combat, combat support, and logistics combat elements or portions thereof.
        The combat operations center continually monitors, records, and supervises operations in the name

health maintenance
        Those tasks to ensure that a unit and its personnel are medically ready for combat operations.
