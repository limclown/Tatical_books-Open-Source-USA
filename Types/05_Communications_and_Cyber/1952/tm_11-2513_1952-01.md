27. Scope of Maintenance

a reference to the paragraphs covering the specific maintenance

   b. Operator’s weekly preventive maintenance checks and serv-

   c. Organizational monthly preventive maintenance checks and

   e. Troubleshooting (para 32).

28. Preventive Maintenance

   Preventive maintenance is the systematic care, servicing, and
inspection of equipment to prevent the occurrence of trouble, to
reduce downtime, and to assure that the equipment is serviceable.

   a. Systematic Care. The procedures given in paragraphs 29.1
through 29.3, 30 through 30.3, and 31 cover routine systematic
care and cleaning essential to proper upkeep and operation of
the equipment.

   b. Preventive Maintenance Checks and Services. The preventive
maintenance checks and services charts (para 29.2, 29.8, 30.2,
and 30.8) outline functions to be performed at specifiic intervals.
These checks and services are to maintain Army electronic equip-
ment in a combat-serviceable condition; that is, in good general
(physical) condition and in good operating condition. To assist
operations in maintaining combat serviceability, the charts in-
dicate what to check, how to check, and what the normal con-
ditions are; the References column lists the illustrations, para-
graphs, or manuals that contain detailed repair or replacement
procedures. If the defect cannot be remedied by performing the
corrective actions listed, higher category maintenance or repair is
required. Records and reports of these checks and_ services
must be made in accordance with the requirements set forth in
TM 388-750.

29. Preventive Maintenance Checks and Services Periods

   Preventive maintenance checks and services of the equipment
are required daily, weekly, monthly, and quarterly.

   a. Paragraph 29.2 specifies the checks and services that must
be accomplished daily (or at least once each week if the equip-
ment is maintained in a standby condition).

    b. Paragraphs 29.3, 30.2, and 30.3 specify additional checks
and services that must be performed on a weekly, monthly, and
quarterly basis, respectively.

29.1. Cleaning                                         surfaces

   Inspect the exterior of the test set. The exterior
should be clean and free of dirt, grease, and fungus.

    a. Remove loose dirt with a clean soft cloth.

   Warning: Prolonged breathing of cleaning compound is dan-

31. Touchup Painting

   Clean rust and corrosion from metal surfaces by lightly sand-
ing them with fine sandpaper. Brush two thin coats of paint on
the bare metal to protect it from further corrosion. Refer to the
applicable cleaning and refinishing practices specified in TB SIG
364 and TM 9-213.

32. Scope

   The troubleshooting and repair work that can be performed at
the organizational maintenance level (operators and repairmen)
is limited necessarily in scope by the tools, test equipment, and
replaceable parts issued. With the exception of replacing operated
fuses and faulty indicator lamps and the electrical adjustment of
255-type relays, troubleshooting is merely a general identification
of the trouble and a recommendation that the trouble be repaired
by qualified repair personnel.

33. Visual Inspection

   a. Failure of the equipment to operate properly will usually
be caused by one or more of the following faults.

(1) Improperly connected power cords.

(2) Worn, broken, or disconnected cords or plugs.

(3) Operated fuse.

(4) Relay out of adjustment or contacts burned because of

           overloads.                              |

(5) Wiring broken because of excessive vibration.

   b. When the equipment fails and the cause is not apparent
immediately, check as many of the above items as is practicable
before starting a detailed examination of the component parts of
the system. If possible, obtain information from the operator of
the equipment regarding the performance at the time trouble
occurred.

3. Purpose and Use

    a. Test Sets I-193—A, I-198-B, and I-193-C (figs. 1 and 2) are
portable test sets used to test and adjust 255-type polar relays.
These include relays 255A, D-163119A (similar to 255A except
that the relay has been moistureproofed), and D-—164816 (figs. 6
through 9). Circuits for testing these polar relays for bias and
for sensitivity of operate and nonoperate current values are in-
cluded in each unit. The electrical operating characteristics of
the relay under test are determined by deflections of the test set
meter needle. In addition, the test set contains a circuit that pro-
cuces and sends a continuous series of impulses, called dots, for
testing external telegraph circuits.

    Note. Test Sets I-193—A, I-193-—B, and I-193-C are similar in external
appearance and identical in operation. A specific model of the test set will
be referred to in the text by its supplement letter—for example, Test Set
I-193—A. When all models of the unit are under discussion, however, the test
set will be referred to as Test Set I-193—(*).

    b. Test Set I-193-—(*) operates from a power source of 115/130
volts de (direct current). This may be battery from associated
telegraph equipment or power provided by a rectifier or power
unit. An oscillator circuit converts the de necessary for operation
to 10- or 20-cps (cycles per second) ac (alternating current) for
testing and line-up purposes.

with a hinged cover. Handles for carrying the unit are located on

blocks for testing polar relays, and the meter are located on the
front panel of the equipment (fig. 3). A kit containing tools for
maintenance and adjustment of 255-type polar relays, a case con-
taining spare fuses and lamps, and operating instructions for the
test set are mounted inside the cover of the case.

is located on the right-hand side of the front panel (fig. 4). On
the left-hand side, a similar compartment mounts the two polar
relays used in the test set and has space for carrying a spare relay.
These two compartments are fastened with slide-type latches.

8. Uncrating, Unpacking, and Checking

   a. General. Test Set I-193-—(*) is packed for either oversea or
domestic shipment, and the shipping container is secured with
metal straps. When new equipment is received, select a location
where it may be unpacked without exposure to dust, dirt, and ex-
cessive moisture. Be careful when uncrating and unpacking the
equipment. Avoid thrusting tools into the interior of the shipping
container. When unpacking, do not damage packing materials
and containers any more than is necessary. These materials may
be used when repacking the unit for storage or for shipment to
depot repair shops. Store the interior packaging materials and
containers carefully inside the wooden shipping container.

    b. Step-By-Step Instructions for Uncrating and Unpacking
Equipment.

        (1) Cut the metal straps with a suitable cutting tool.

        (2) Remove the top of the shipping container with a tri-saw
               box opener. If a box opener is not available, a standard
              nail puller may be used.

        (3) Cut the barrier seal so that the barrier will be damaged
              as little as possible. Lift the boxed equipment from the
               shipping container. Cut the seals on the outer fiberboard
               box and lift the packaged unit from this container.

        (4) Carefully cut the moisture-vaporproof barrier and re-
               move the equipment from the interior box.

        (5) Thoroughly check the equipment against the shipping
               documents to see if any equipment is missing. Thor-
              oughly inspect the equipment for possible damage dur-
               ing shipment.

18. Sensitivity Test of Relay

   The pole pieces of the relay under test can be at equal distances
from the armature (par. 17) and the relay can still be out of
adjustment, thus affecting its sensitivity. The sensitivity test of
a relay is made to determine whether the clearance from each
pole piece to the relay armature is correct and, at the same time,
to check on contact adjustment. When testing for sensitivity,
operate and nonoperate current values are applied to the relay
windings in both the marking and spacing directions. Operate
current is the lowest value of current that will cause the relay
armature to operate. Nonoperate current is the highest value of
current at which the relay armature will not operate. To test
a relay for sensitivity, proceed as follows:

   a. General. Place the relay in the correct connecting block and
operate switch K4 to the position corresponding to the connecting
block used (par. 17a). Operate switch K6 to DOTS HIGH and

All other switches are at normal.

21. General

   When operating Test Set I-193—(*) in regions where extreme
cold, heat, humidity, moisture, and sand conditions prevail, spe-
cial precautions must be taken to keep the equipment in operating
condition at all times. Paragraphs 22 through 24 give instructions
for minimizing the effect of these unusual operating conditions.

22. Operation in Arctic Climates

   Since Test Set I-193-—(*) is intended for indoor use, it will be
exposed to extreme cold only when in storage or transport. When
possible, house and use the equipment in a heated shelter. If the
test set has been exposed to the cold it will begin to sweat and
will continue to do so until it reaches room temperature. This
condition also arises when the equipment warms up during the
day after exposure during a cold night. When the test set has
reached room temperature, dry it thoroughly before attempting
to operate the unit. Perform preventive maintenance operations
(par. 29) more frequently to keep the unit as moisture-free as
possible.

23. Operation in Desert Climates

   The main problem when operating the equipment in desert
climates is to prevent dirt, dust, and sand from filtering into the
equipment. If possible, house and use the equipment in a dust-
proof shelter. Since such a building is seldom available, however,

AGO 3086B                            21


---

make the shelter as dust-free as possible by hanging wet sacking
over windows and doors, covering inside walls with heavy paper,
and preventing the side walls of tents from flapping in the wind
by securing them with sand. Be sure that the relay compartments
are latched at all times except when a relay is being replaced.
Close and latch the lid of the test set case when the unit is not

24. Operation in Tropical Climates

   When operated in tropical climates, the test set should be used
in a building or shelter. The high relative humidity of such
climates causes condensation of moisture on the equipment when-
ever the temperature of the equipment becomes lower than the
ambient air. Inspect and clean the equipment frequently to keep
it free from rust, corrosion, and fungus growth. Keep the equip-
ment as dry as possible. Place a lighted electric bulb or a small
bag of desiccant in the compartment containing the test set relays
to help prevent moisture from entering the relay windings.

27. Definition of Preventive Maintenance

   Preventive maintenance is work performed on equipment,
usually when it is not in use, to keep it in such good working order
that break-downs and needless interruptions in service will be
kept to a minimum. Preventive maintenance differs from trouble
shooting and repair since its object is to prevent certain troubles
from occurring. See AR 750-5 for information on organizational,
field, and base maintenance.

28. General Preventive Maintenance Techniques

   a. Use No. 0000 sandpaper to remove corrosion.

   b. Use a clean, dry, lint-free cloth or a dry brush for cleaning.
       (1) If necessary, except for electrical contacts, moisten or
              brush with solvent (SD) and then wipe the parts dry
              with a cloth.
       (2) Clean electrical contacts with a cloth moistened with
               carbon tetrachloride and wipe them dry with a dry cloth.
                 Caution: Repeated contact of carbon tetrachloride
               with the skin or prolonged breathing of the fumes is
              dangerous. Make sure adequate ventilation is provided.

   ce. If available, dry compressed air may be used at a line pressure
not exceeding 60 psi to remove dust from inaccessible places. Be
careful, however, or mechanical damage from the air blast may
result.

   d. For further information on preventive maintenance tech-
niques, refer to TB SIG 178.

a minimum. The list shows what, when, and how to check, and

    Section III. WEATHERPROOFING

30. Weatherproofing

   a. General. Signal Corps equipment, when operated under
severe climatic conditions such as prevail in tropical, arctic, and
desert regions, requires special treatment and maintenance. Fun-
gus growth, insects, dust, corrosion, salt spray, excessive moisture,
and extreme temperatures are harmful to most materials.

   b. Tropical Maintenance. A special moistureproofing and fungi-
proofing treatment has been devised which, if properly applied,
provides a reasonable degree of protection. This treatment is
explained fully in TB SIG 18 and TB SIG 72.

   c. Winter Maintenance. Special precautions necessary to pre-
vent poor performance or total operational failure of equipment .
in extremely low temperatures are explained fully in TB SIG 66
and TB SIG 219.

   d. Desert Maintenance. Special precautions necessary to pre-
vent equipment failure in areas subject to extremely high tem-
peratures, low humidity, and excessive sand and dust are explained
fully in TB SIG 75.

31. Rustproofing and Painting

    a. When painted metal finishes have been badly marred or

damaged, prevent rust and corrosion by touching up bared sur-

faces. Use No. 0000 sandpaper to clean the surface down to the

bare metal until it is bright and smooth. Apply matching paint

with a small brush.

    Caution: Do not use steel wool to remove nist Small particles

of metal may fall into the unit and cause internal electrical short-

ing and grounding of surfaces.

    b. If complete repainting is necessary, follow the directions for

refinishing given in paragraph 59.  .

   The trouble-shooting and repair work that can be performed at

is limited necessarily in scope by the tools, test equipment, and re-
placeable parts issued. With the exception of replacing operated

255-type relays, trouble-shooting is merely a general identification

33. Visual Inspection

   a. Failure of the equipment to operate properly will usually be
caused by one or more of the following faults:

      Section |. THEORY OF TEST SET 1-193-—(*)

36. General

    a. Test Set I-193-(*) is designed for testing and adjusting

electrically 255-type polarized relays used in telegraph equipment

and for sending a series of impulses, called dots, for use in lining

up external telegraph equipment. The test set operates from a d-c

power source of 115 to 130 volts. This power may be supplied by

telegraph equipment or by a rectifier or power unit, or any other

available source of dc, which is not used to supply power to any

other equipment.  eet

    b. The test set contains six basic circuits. An oscillating circuit

produces an ac at a frequency of approximately 10 or 20 cps which

is necessary for testing. A dots producing and sending circuit

transmits a series of dots to interrupt another circuit external to

the test set at a frequency of either 10 or 20 interruptions per sec-

ond. These interruptions are generally used for equipment line-up

purposes. A metering circuit indicates the electrical condition of

a relay under test by oscillation or deflection of the meter needle.

This circuit also is used to adjust the dots-producing circuit for

zero bias. A soak current circuit, used when testing the sensitivity

of a relay, applies a necessary magnetic condition to a relay before

operate and nonoperate current values are applied. The operate

and nonoperate circuits are used to apply operate and nonoperate

current, respectively, in both the marking and spacing directions

to a relay under test, Paragraphs 37 through 43 contain detailed

descriptions of the functioning of the circuits comprising the test

set.

45. Removal of Pluck-Out Parts

   The fuse, the blown fuse indicator lamp, and the relays can be
removed without unsoldering connections. These parts are called
pluck-out parts as they do not require complicated disconnection
procedures. No disassembly is necessary except the removal of
the test set from the case when replacing the indicator lamp.

   a. Indicator Light. To remove the lamp, loosen the locknut on
the under side of the panel which holds the lamp cap in place, and
remove the lamp cap. When the indicator lamp has been replaced,
secure the lamp cap in place by tightening the locknut.

   b. Fuse. To remove the fuse, turn the fuse cap in the direction
indicated by the arrow on the cap and pull out the cap and fuse.

   c. Relays. To remove the relays, open the compartment on the
left-hand side of the front panel and pull the relays out of their
connecting blocks. A replaced relay cannot be seated improperly
in the connecting block as the two mounting posts on the left, and
the holes through the chassis which accommodate these posts, are
larger than the two on the right.

46. Inspecting, Cleaning, and Testing Removed Parts

   a. Inspecting and Cleaning. Check the removed relays for me-
chanical adjustment and dirty or built-up contacts. If necessary,
clean the relay according to instructions in paragraph 49. Inspect
the indicator lamp for burned base and broken filament. Inspect
the fuse visually for continuity, loose ferrules, and charring.

    b. Testing. Adjust the relays mechanically according to direc-
tions in paragraph 57. Replace the test set relays with two 255A
relays that are known to be in good adjustment, and insert the re-
lays to be tested, one at a time, in connecting block CB2. Test for
electrical performance and adjust in accordance with instructions
in paragraphs 17 through 19.

20. Cleaning Jack Springs and Sleeve

   a. Cleaning Springs. Use a plug which has recently been cleaned
and from which all cleaning paste has been removed to clean the
crimps of the tip and ring springs which make contact with the
plug. Insert and withdraw the plug two or three times. While
inserting the plug into the jack, rotate the plug against the springs.
It is not practical to clean the tip and ring springs thoroughly. If
it is necessary to disassemble the spring assembly for any reason,
however, wipe the parts with a clean, dry cloth.

   b. Cleaning Sleeve.

       (1) Cleaning with jack sleeve burnisher. If a portable motor
              and a flexible shaft are available, place a clean burnisher
              in the handpiece of the flexible shaft. If a hand-drill is
              used, place the tool in the hand-drill chuck. When the
              tool is rotating, insert it into the jack opening with the
              axis of the tool perpendicular to the face of the jack.
              Application of the tool for a few seconds should clean the
              surface of the flare. In some cases, however, it may be
              necessary to apply the tool at a slight angle to clean spots
              that are particularly dirty.

       (2) Cleaning with cotton cloth.

         (a) Make a solution of solvent (SD) and 2 percent by vol-

                  ume of light oil, using 17 drops of oil for each ounce
                  of solvent (SD). The solution should be made in quan-
                  tities of 2 ounces or less, depending on the number of
                  jacks to be cleaned. Keep the container tightly corked
                 except when moistening the cloth.

           (b) Fold a piece of clean, lint-free cloth, about 5 inches
                  long and 114 inches wide, along its length so that the
                 strip will be about 34, inch wide. Insert one end of an
                  orange stick into the fold at one end of the cloth, and
                 wrap the cloth spirally around the orange stick so that
                  the folded edge is exposed. There should be about 3
                 turns of the cloth per inch of length. Hold the end of
                 the cloth with the fingers or fasten it by some suitable
                 means.

         (c) Moisten the end of the cloth with the solution. Insert

                 the wrapped orange stick into the sleeve of the jack
                  and rub back and forth, contacting all of the surfaces
                 of the sleeve. Be careful not to touch the springs of
                 the jack more than is necessary. Repeat this process
                 as required until the cloth is clean when it is removed
                 from the sleeve.

51. Trouble-Shooting Procedure

   The first step in servicing a defective test set is to sectionalize
the fault. Sectionalization means tracing the fault to a major com-
ponent or circuit. The second step is to localize the fault—that is,
to trace it to the defective part responsible for the abnormal con-
dition. Careful observation of the performance of the test set may
aid in sectionalizing the fault to a particular circuit. For exam-
ple, the meter indications under certain conditions of operation
may indicate whether various switches and the associated circuits
are functioning properly. For the most part, however, the major-
ity of faults in Test Set I-193-(*) must be localized by checking
voltage, resistance, and circuit continuity. The test outlined in a
through e below will help to isolate the source of trouble. To be
effective, these tests should be made in the order given.

   a. Visual Inspection. Carefully inspect the equipment to be sure
that there is no mechanical binding of switches which might dam-
age the equipment. As instructed in paragraph 47a, make a visual
inspection of the equipment for any sign of damage such as loose
or missing screws, nuts, and bolts, loose or poorly soldered con-
nections, dirty, pitted, or built-up contacts on switches, jacks, and
relays, damaged insulation, and exposed wiring which might be
the cause of shorts with other wiring, terminals, or ground.
Through such visual inspection, frequently it is possible to dis-
cover the trouble or to determine in which circuit or stage the
trouble exists. In addition, visual inspection is valuable in that
further damage to the equipment, resulting from improper main-
tenance methods, may be avoided and future failures forestalled.

   b. Check Of Power Supply. Check the power supply           with a
suitable voltmeter to be sure that it is within the required  115- to
130-volt de range.

c. Operational Test. The operational test (par. 54) is impor-

tant because frequently it indicates the general location of trouble.

In many instances, the information obtained will determine

the exact location of the fault. So that this information may be

utilized fully, all symptoms must be interpreted in relation to one

another.                        :

53. General Precautions

   Whenever Test Set I-193-—(*) is serviced, carefully observe the
following precautions:

   a. Disconnect the power supply when working around the
power supply cords or connectors. Contact with 130 volts de can
be fatal.

    b. Careless replacement of parts often makes new faults inevi-
table. Pay particular attention to the following points:

       (1) Before a part is unsoldered, note the position of the leads.
               If a part has a number of connections, tag each of the
              leads before removing the part.

        (2) Be careful not to damage other leads by pushing or pull-
               ing them out of the way.

        (3) Do not allow drops of solder to fall into the set. Excess
               solder may cause short circuits.

        (4) A carelessly soldered connection may create a new fault.
              It is very important to make well-soldered joints. A
              poorly soldered joint is one of the most difficult faults to
               find.

        (5) When a part is replaced, place it in the same position as
               the original part.

    Section III. REPAIRS

56. Replacement of Parts

   No special instructions are necessary for the replacement of
parts in Test Set I-193-(*). All parts can be reached easily for
replacement by removing the equipment from the case or, in the
case of relays, by opening the relay compartment on the left-hand
side of the front panel. When removing wiring from terminals,
either by unsoldering or by loosening screw type terminals, tag
each wire to facilitate correct wiring when a new part is installed.
The new part should be an exact duplicate of the original. When
replacing wiring, be sure that the new wiring is the same length
as the wire being replaced. If wiring with different color-coding
is used, make the change on the applicable wiring diagram (figs.
33 through 35). Refer to figures 29 through 31 for location of
parts.

57. Adjustment of Relay (Mechanical)

    A relay should be adjusted mechanically only when it is to be
completely reconditioned or when the relay cannot be made to
meet the electrical requirements (par. 19) after it has been in-
spected and cleaned (par. 49).

   a. Appearance of Armature. Back off the pole-piece screws as
far as they will go. Back off both contact screws several turns
with the adjusting key. Remove the armature by loosening the
screws in the supporting bracket in the armature heelpiece and
by moving the armature out through the slot in the winding spool.
Check the armature to see that it is straight and that the rivets
holding the armature contact springs to the body of the armature
are tight. Make sure that there are no irregular projections from
that portion of the armature surface which is held by the clamp-
ing piece. Check the condition of the flexible springs. They should
be flat, and the ends of the springs should be in contact for at least
25 percent of their width. If these requirements are not met,
replace the armature with a new one. When the armature has
been replaced, tighten the holding screws securely.

    b. Armature Clearance. Back off the contact screws several
turns with the adjusting key. Check the armature to see that it
is approximately equidistant from the contact screw supporting
brackets and that it is centered in the spool slot. If the armature ©
is not centered between the contact screw supporting brackets,
loosen the screws holding the armature heelpiece and realine the
armature. Tighten the screws. If the armature is not centered
horizontally in the spool slot, loosen the screws that hold the front

the right or left, as required, and tighten the screws. If the arma-
ture is not centered vertically in the spool slot, slightly loosen the

screws holding the armature to its heelpiece and move the arma-

ture up or down to bring the stationary screw contacts and the
armature contacts into proper alinement. Tighten the screws.

   c. Contact Alinement. Both the right- and left-hand screw con-
tacts should be entirely within the area of the armature contacts.
When this requirement is not met, loosen the armature heelpiece
screws and line up the contacts, at the same time being careful to
keep the armature centered in the slot. Tighten the heelpiece
screws. If the armature contacts cannot be alined in this manner,

or if the contact screws are not in line, loosen the screws which
hold the contact screw brackets to the base of the relay and move
the brackets until the contacts are in alinement. If the contacts

are alined by moving the contact screw brackets, set the brackets
so that the contact screws strike the armature contacts as near to
the center as possible. Tighten all screws firmly.

Check with the pole-piece screws turned in toward the armature.

If the pole-piece screws are not in alinement, loosen the top and

bottom screws which hold the pole-piece screw support and move

the pole-piece screws into alinement by hand. Tighten the screws

the terminal block. If the relay base is loose, tighten the screws
with a screw driver. Be sure that the assembly screws on the relay
base are tight. If the screws are loose, tighten them with a screw

driver.

58. Adjustment of Switches

   When the lever type switches used in Test Set I-193—(*) meet
the specified minimum requirements given in subparagraphs a
through e below, the switches are in satisfactory operating condi-
tion. If the requirements are not met, however, readjust for maxi-
mum requirements. When a switch is adjusted for one require-
ment only, all other requirements must be rechecked to be sure
that they are still met. Before proceeding with adjustments, in-
spect the switch for dirt or other foreign material between the
springs which might interfere with operation of the switch. If
such deposits are present, remove them with a clean toothpick
which has been dipped in carbon tetrachloride.

   a. Contact Alinement. Contacts should line up so that the point
of contact falls wholly within the boundary of the opposing con-
tact at all times during contact. If the contacts do not line up sat-
isfactorily, loosen the spring assembly screws slightly and shift
the springs until the contacts are in alinement. Tighten the assem-
bly screws securely and recheck the contact alinement. Check all
other requirements again to be sure that the requirements are still
met.

    b. Contact Separation and Spring Clearance. The separation
between any pair of contacts should be a minimum of .014 inch
on test, or a minimum of .016 inch when the spring is readjusted.
Similar separation requirements apply to adjacent springs de-
signed never to make contact and, under all conditions, between
any spring and the switch framework. If the spring is bowed or
bent and the requirements are not met, straighten the spring be-
fore proceeding with any adjustment. To straighten a spring, apply
the parallel jaw pliers to the spring just back of the bow or bend
and, while exerting pressure to the right or left, as required, draw
the pliers upward toward the spring contact. Repeat this opera-
tion until the spring is in satisfactory condition. Proper adjust-
ment also requires that the spring be in line with its line of move-
ment and approximately parallel with adjacent springs. Adjust
for contact separation by placing the pliers just below the contact
and adjusting the spring toward or away from the associated
spring, as required.

   ec. Contact Pressure. Closed contacts should have a minimum
pressure of 50 grams on test, or a minimum of 55 grams when the
spring is readjusted. If the springs are bowed or bent, straighten
them as directed in subparagraph b above before making adjust-
ments. To adjust a spring for contact pressure, place the pliers on
the spring just above the spring pile-up and gently rotate the
pliers to the right or left, as required. When adjusting contact
pressure, be careful not to disturb the adjustment of properly ad-
justed springs.

   d. Contact Follow. Normally open and closed contacts should
have a minimum contact follow of .008 inch on test, or a minimum
of .010 inch when the spring is readjusted. With the parallel jaw
pliers, adjust for contact follow by adjusting the position of the
stop spring or by reducing the tension of the plunger spring
slightly. The tension adjustment should not be great enough to
interfere with any of the previous adjustments.

   e. Spring Separators. The spring separators should not rub on
the springs through which they pass when the switch lever is oper-
ated. If a separator rubs on a spring through which it passes,
loosen the spring assembly screws slightly and shift the spring as
required. When satisfactory separator adjustment has been made,
check to see whether contact alinement has been retained and
tighten the spring assembly screws securely. Recheck the separa-
tors and alinement of contacts.

59. Refinishing

   a. General. Check the appearance and condition of the finish of

all metal surfaces. The finish should show no decided wear, and
no bare metal should be exposed because of shipping or scratches
on the surface. Refinish surfaces where the finish has been re-
moved completely or worn through. Retouch such surfaces if the
bare parts are restricted to small areas, but refinish the entire sur-
face if the bare or damaged areas are large. Discolored, spotted,
stained, or faded surfaces are permissible if the original finish is
intact.

    b. Refinishing procedures. After sanding or scratch brushing,
refinish affected surfaces to match the original finish. Apply one
coat of lacquer to parts having a bright metal finish. Apply one
coat of matching enamel or paint to painted surfaces. Do not apply
lacquer to contact parts of jacks, switches, or the potentiometer,
or to other surfaces which might affect the mechanical or elec-
trical functioning of the equipment.

    c. Preservative Applications. After the unit has been completely
refinished, moistureproof and fungiproof the equipment according
to instructions in paragraph 30D.

Section 1. SHIPMENT AND LIMITED STORAGE

60. Removing from Service

a. Disconnect the test set from the power source.

   b. If a ground lead is connected to the GND binding post, discon-
nect the lead.

   c. Set all the lever switches at midposition.

   d. Coil and tie the power cords, and place the loose cords in the
recesses at either end of the front panel under the carrying han-
dles.

é. Close and latch the relay compartment covers.

   f. Check the  relay maintenance tools to see that they are in
place, and snap  shut the flaps of the tool kit and the spare parts
kit.

61. Repacking for Shipment or Limited Storage

    Note. As far as possible, use the original packaging materials which were
saved at the time the equipment was unpacked.

   a. Place small bags of desiccant on the front panel of the equip-
ment and cushion the face of the unit with cells or pads of cor-
rugated fiberboard. Close and latch the lid of the case.

    b. Place the equipment in a corrugated fiberboard box and
cushion the case on all sides with pads or cells of corrugated fiber-
board. Place bags of desiccant in the fiberboard cells. Close the
box, seal it with paper tape, and place in a moisture-vaporproof
barrier. Heat-seal the barrier.

   c. Inclose the barrier-wrapped package in a second close-fitting

62. Methods of Demolition

   a. Smash. Use sledges,    axes,  handaxes,  pickaxes,  hammers,
crowbars, heavy tools.

b. Cut. Use axes, handaxes, machetes.

c. Burn. Use gasoline, kerosene, oil, flame throwers, incendiary

grenades.

d. Explode. Use firearms, grenades, TNT.

   e. Dispose. Burn in slit  trenches, fox  holes, other holes. Throw
into streams. Scatter.        immediately   available for destruction

   f. Other. Use anything
of this equipment.

63. Methods of Destruction

When ordered by your commander, destroy all equipment to

prevent its being used or salvaged by the enemy.

a. Smash (par. 62a) the switches, jacks, meter, potentiometer

control, control panel, connectors, plugs, terminal boards, relays,

retardation coil, filters, resistors, capacitors, fuses, and indicator

light.                                                    .

b. Cut (par. 62b) the wooden case, all power cords, and wiring.

   c. Burn (par. 62c) the wooden case, cords, wiring, resistors,
capacitors, Signal Corps forms, office records, and technical manu-

als.

d. Bury or scatter (par. 62e) the destroyed parts in slit trenches

or fox holes, or throw them into streams.

e. Destroy everything.
