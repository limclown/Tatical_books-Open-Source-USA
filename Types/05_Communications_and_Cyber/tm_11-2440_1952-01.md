_ various radiosonde circuits in or out as required during operation of

 the check set.

  15. General

  Special problems may be encountered in operating Radiosonde Baseline

  Check Set AN/GMM-1 in regions where extreme cold, heat, humidity,
  or sand conditions predominate. The following paragraphs contain instruc-
  tions for minimizing the effects of these unusual operating conditions.

  16. Operation in Arctic Climates

     a. Locate the equipment inside a heated inclosure, where there is no
  danger of a cold draft striking the psychrometer when the door is opened.

  If such a location is unavailable, place a blanket or some other barrier

  between the possible source of draft and the equipment.

      b. Equipment which has been exposed to the cold will start to sweat
  when brought into a warm room and will continue to do so until it reaches
  the temperature of the room. When the equipment reaches room tempera-

  ture, dry it thoroughly. A similar condition arises when equipment warms
  up during the day after being exposed to a sharp drop in temperature

  during the night.
      c. For further information on operation in low temperatures, refer to

  TB SIG 66.

  17. Operation in Tropical Climates

  Equipment operated in tropical climates often is installed in tents or huts.

* Ventilation is usually poor in such locations, and the high relative humidity

  causes condensation of moisture on the equipment whenever the tempera-
  ture of the equipment becomes lower than the ambient air. Any means of

  heating, such as large electric bulbs, will tend to minimize this condition.

  For detailed information on operation in tropical climates, refer to
  TB SIG 72.

  18. Operation in Desert Climates

     a. The main difficulty of operation in desert climates is the large amount

  of sand and dust in the air. Since an air-conditioned, dustproof shelter

  seldom is available, precautions should be taken to make the building in
  which the equipment is operated as dustproof as possible. Hang wet
  sacking over the windows and doors, cover the inside walls with heavy
  paper, and secure the side walls of tents with sand to prevent their flap-
  ping in the wind.

     b. Never tie the cables or other wiring connections to any part of tents.
  Wind storms, which may jerk the connections loose or break the lines,

 21. Meaning of Preventive Maintenance

    a. DEFINITION. Preventive maintenance is work performed on equip--
 ment, usually when it is not in use, to keep it in good condition so that
 break-downs and interruptions in service will be kept at a minimum. The
 object of preventive maintenance is to eliminate the need for trouble
 shooting and repair.

    b. IMPORTANCE. Since the failure or inefficient operation of a single

 part may cause the break-down of the entire equipment, the importance

 of preventive maintenance is obvious. Operators must maintain equipment
 in their charge in such condition that it will work at top efficiency at all
 times.

 22. Preventive Maintenance Techniques

    a. Use #0000 sandpaper to remove corrosion.

     b. Use a clean, dry, lint-free cloth or a dry brush for cleaning. Moisten
 the cloth or brush with solvent (SD); clean the equipment, except elec-

trical contacts, and then wipe the part dry with a fresh cloth. Clean the

 electrical contacts with a cloth moistened with carbon tetrachloride; then

 wipe them dry with a clean cloth.

    Caution: Repeated contact of carbon tetrachloride with the skin or
 prolonged breathing of the fumes is dangerous. Make sure adequate
 ventilation is provided.

    c. If dry compressed air is available, it may be used at a line pressure

 not exceeding 40 pounds per square inch to remove dust from inaccessible
 places in the equipment. If compressed air is used, be very careful not to

 damage the equipment.

    d. Do not tighten screws or nuts carelessly. Fittings tightened beyond
 the pressure for which they are designed will be damaged or broken.

37. Trouble-shooting Procedures

The first step in servicing a defective equipment is to sectionalize the fault.
Sectionalizing means tracing the fault to a major component or circuit.
The second step is to localize the fault. Localization means tracing the
fault to the defective part. Some faults can be located by smell, sight, or
hearing. The majority of faults, however, must be localized by checking
voltage and resistance.

39. General Precautions

When equipment is to be serviced, always observe the following pre-

cautions:
   a. If equipment is operated for test purposes with the control unit cover

or the fan motor cover removed, observe proper safety precautions; dan-
gerous voltages are exposed.

   b. Careless replacement of parts can result in the creation of new
faults. Observe the following precautions when replacing parts:

   (1) Before unsoldering a part, note the position of the leads connected
to it. If the part has a number of connections, tag each of the leads as it is
removed. Be careful not to damage other leads by pulling or pushing them
out of the way.

   (2) Do not allow drops of solder to fall into the set; they may cause
short circuits. A carelessly soldered connection may create a new fault.
Well-soldered joints are important, because a poorly soldered joint is
sometimes difficult to find.

   (3) Give particular attention to proper grounding when replacing
a part.
