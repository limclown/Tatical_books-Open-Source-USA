                                 Preface

ATP 3-53, The Psychological Operations Branch and Unit Activities, replaces FM 3-53, Military Information
Support Operations, as the authoritative reference for the United States (U.S.) Army Psychological
Operations (PO) Branch to influence foreign audiences across the range of military operations. The principles
in this revision are linked to higher-level Army and joint doctrine and provide the basis for subordinate Army
techniques publications and technical manuals to discuss detailed tactics, techniques, processes, and
procedures. ATP 3-53 rescinds GTA 33-01-004 and GTA 33-01-006.

The principal audiences for ATP 3-53 are PO Branch personnel, Psychological Operations-qualified
planners, Psychological Operations units, and supported commanders and their staffs, regardless of the
Service. This publication informs leaders of other government departments and agencies, U.S. ambassadors,
and country team members. In addition, operational Psychological Operations units and the institutional force
responsible for fundamental and advanced training and education of Army, sister Service, and joint force-
assigned personnel; foreign partners; and other government department personnel can use this publication as
their foundational publication for influence. Subordinate Psychological Operations proponent doctrine and
training publications expand on the content in this publication and provide the additional requisite tactics,
techniques, procedures, and processes to develop individual and unit training and education.

ATP 3-53 (in conjunction with Army tactics, techniques, and procedures and combined arms training strategies)
provides Army leaders with practical references necessary to understand the role and core competencies of the
career management field 37 (Psychological Operations). It also allows leaders to understand the fundamental
processes, approaches, and activities that Psychological Operations units execute at the unclassified level.

ATP 3-53 serves as an authoritative reference for force structure, materiel, training and education requirements,
and other resources. This publication describes some of the pertinent legal parameters for the planning and
approval of operations and activities. ATP 3-53 also addresses the execution of operations in support of
ground combat operations.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure that their decisions and actions comply with applicable U.S.,
international, and in some cases host-nation laws and regulations. Commanders at all levels ensure that their
Soldiers operate in accordance with the law of war and the rules of engagement. (See FM 6-27.)

ATP 3-53 uses joint terms, where applicable. Selected joint and Army terms and definitions appear in both
the glossary and the text. Terms for which ATP 3-53 is the proponent publication (the authority) are marked
with an asterisk (*) in the glossary. Definitions for which ATP 3-53 is the proponent publication are boldfaced
in the text. For other definitions shown in the text, the term is italicized, and the proponent publication number
follows the definition. All photography depicted originate with the United States Army Special Operations
Command or from the United States State Department as noted.

ATP 3-53 applies to the Active Army, the Army National Guard or Army National Guard of the United States
and the United States Army Reserve unless otherwise stated.

The proponent of ATP 3-53 is the U.S. Army Special Operations Center of Excellence, United States Army
John F. Kennedy Special Warfare Center and School (USAJFKSWCS). The preparing agency is the
Psychological Operations Doctrine Division. Send comments and recommendations on a DA Form 2028

               Introduction

This version of ATP 3-53 updates doctrine to address current and future operating environments through
2030. It incorporates changes from FM 3-0 including multidomain operations, influence as a form of contact,
and definitions of conventional and irregular warfare. The publication covers visual, audio, multimedia, and
face-to-face and psychological actions (PSYACTs) to counter threats.

Building on more than 80 years of psychological warfare (PSYWAR) doctrine, this update expands beyond
psychological operations (PSYOP). It now includes the role of the Psychological Operations (PO) Branch
and its two primary influence functions: PSYOP and Department of Defense deception activities (DDAs).
To ensure clarity, it codifies the language for the PO Branch, aligning it with AR 870-21, FM 1-02.1, and
FM 1-02.2 and addressing terminology changes since 2010 (introductory table 1).

                                              Chapter 1

The Psychological Operations Branch, Units, and Force

    This chapter highlights the five fundamental Psychological Operations (PO) Branch elements
    and the branch’s two primary influence activities. It also briefly introduces the organization of
    Psychological Operations units in the Army, discusses the PO Branch’s strategic contexts for
    employment, and details their use of influence objectives.

OVERVIEW

     1-1. Militaries have historically used influence activities to demoralize enemies, gain support from
     populations, and achieve objectives. Americans have employed these tactics since the Revolutionary War,
     but dedicated United States (U.S.) military elements for influence activities were first created during
     World War I. The army established the Psychological Warfare Center and School in 1952, the
     Psychological Operations Regiment in 1998, and the Psychological Operations Branch as a basic branch
     on October 16, 2006.

     1-2. Since World War II, the Department of Defense (DOD) has used more than 13 terms to define a variety of
     U.S. influence activities. Some of these include propaganda, psychological warfare, and operational deception.

     1-3. ADP 3-13 codifies the Army's influence information activity,
     focusing on two tasks: influencing threat perceptions and behavior, and
     influencing foreign audiences. It identifies deception activities,
     psychological operations (PSYOP), civil affairs operations, and Soldier
     and leader engagement as key ways to achieve these goals.

             Note: The Influence Activities section (later in this chapter)
             addresses the DOD codification of influence.

1-4. While all Army units can conduct or support influence activities,

the Army PO Branch specializes in shaping foreign behaviors to

achieve military and national objectives across operations.  Figure 1-1. Influence Information

1-5. The branch’s influence process, the organization and equipment of Psychological Operations units, and

the assignment of PO Soldiers enable influencing foreign audiences through two primary influence

activities—PSYOP and Department of Defense deception activities (DDAs). The influence process, the

assignment of PO Soldiers, and coordination with Psychological Operations units contribute to successful

Soldier and leader engagement and civil affairs operations.

1-6. Psychological Operations unit commanders are equipped to lead operations in the information
environment by integrating information forces to influence behavior. Operations in the information
environment are military actions involving the integrated employment of multiple information forces to affect
drivers of behavior (JP 3-04). According to ADP 3-13, these operations involve informing, influencing,
attacking, exploiting, and protecting information, networks, and systems.

SECTION I – FUNDAMENTAL ELEMENTS

     1-7. The five fundamental elements of the PO Branch are its role, core competencies, base concepts,
     principles, and the influence process (figure 1-2, page 2). These five fundamental elements are integrated
     into branch training, education, and leader development efforts, from the branch qualification course and
     throughout a Soldier’s professional military education. Understanding these elements provides the foundation
     to support future force modernization. Current Army force modernization efforts focus on organizational,
     training, policy, and material changes; advancing concepts and doctrine for information advantage; and
     integrating space, cyberspace, and special operations capabilities. All these affect Army PO Soldiers.

ROLE

     1-8. As stated in the introduction, the role of PO Branch is to create and reinforce desired behaviors in
     selected foreign individuals and groups to achieve military and U.S. national objectives across the range of
     military operations. Psychological Operations units fulfill the role for which its branch was established
     through two primary influence activities: PSYOP and DDAs. Individual PO Soldiers assigned to the staffs
     of other Army and joint force commands create conditions for the employment of Psychological Operations
     units. These PO Soldiers also provide subject-matter expertise for the planning and command and control of
     operations that Psychological Operations units and other Army units (that conduct influence activities)
     execute.

     1-9. Psychological operations are operations to influence the behavior of targeted foreign audiences in
     support of a commander’s objectives. Also called PSYOP.

     1-10. A Department of Defense deception activity is a policy-guided operation conducted by a combatant
     command, Service, agency, or other components which employ military deception to influence the actions
     of adversary military, paramilitary, violent extremist organizations, or proxy decision makers, or to mislead
     a foreign intelligence entity, to aid in the accomplishment of the friendly mission and achieve a United States
     military advantage. (JP 3-54).

CORE COMPETENCIES

     1-11. A core competency is an essential and enduring capability that a branch or an organization provides to
     Army operations (ADP 1-01). PO Branch core competencies describe the capabilities required to execute
     PSYOP and DDAs and contribute to Army operations. The competencies are collective; however, the
     required skills are individual. The varying level of skills among individuals contribute to the collective
     competencies of the branch. Each core competency compliments the other, providing units the capability to
     plan and execute the two primary influence activities. The PO Branch core competencies are analyzing target
     audiences (TAs) and influencing foreign audiences. A target audience is an individual or group selected for
     influence (JP 3-04).

INFLUENCING FOREIGN AUDIENCES

     1-12. Influence is the ability to cause an effect in direct or indirect ways. An enduring influence capability requires
     Soldiers with a diverse set of skills. For Soldiers to advance their proficiency in required skills from basic to expert
     levels requires deliberate institutional, organizational, and individual development.

ANALYZING TARGET AUDIENCES

     1-13. Analyzing TAs is essential to influencing foreign audiences and influencing foreign audiences provides
     the purpose for analysis. Like the previous core competency, analyzing TAs requires Soldiers to advance
     their proficiency from basic to expert levels over the course of their careers. TM 3-53.11 provides details on
     the target audience analysis (TAA) activity and supersedes previous information on the topic from ST 33-01.

CULTURAL SKILLS, REGIONAL EXPERTISE, AND KNOWLEDGE

     1-14. Cultural skills, regional expertise, and the acquisition of knowledge for both are intertwined and
     provide foundational understanding required for both the core competencies.

     1-15. According to Intercultural Communication - A Reader, culture is the deposit of knowledge, experience,
     beliefs, attitudes, meanings, social hierarchies, religion, notions of time, roles, spatial relationships, concepts
     of the universe, and material objects and possessions acquired by a group of people during generations
     through individual and group striving. Culture significantly influences how humans interpret information,
     make decisions, and behave. Culture incorporates knowledge, experiences, beliefs, values, attitudes,
     meanings, social hierarchies, religions, notions of time, roles (including gender and age-related roles), and
     spatial relationships. Psychological Operations Soldiers must understand culture from a general perspective
     to be able to work toward expertise in specific cultures. Figure 1-3, page 4, highlights topics Soldiers must
     acquire knowledge about to achieve proficiency in cultural skills and gain regional expertise.

A SPECTRUM OF INFLUENCE

     1-20. Psychological Operations units conduct operations in support of the highest echelons of
     government and down to the smallest units of action. Psychological Operations units achieve influence
     across a diverse spectrum. A four-tier framework serves as a foundation for clear, mission-specific
     discussions about influence.

National Strategic Influence

     1-21. JP 3-0 outlines four core elements of competition: interests (the foundational element), influence,
     advantage, and leverage. Among these, national strategic influence represents the highest level within the
     spectrum of influence and is synonymous with the core element of influence. A nation advances and
     safeguards its interests by employing the other elements of competition (influence, advantage, and leverage)
     in a coordinated manner. These core elements are applicable to all relationships between the United States
     and other state or nonstate strategic actors, as defined within the competition continuum.

     1-22. Interests are the values, principles, and priorities that a nation or actor seeks to protect or achieve in the
     face of competitors. These may include matters of self-preservation, economic prosperity, national security,
     or ideological goals. For example, the United States prioritizes interests such as maintaining global stability,
     ensuring access to critical resources, and promoting democratic values.

     1-23. Influence refers to the ability to shape outcomes or behaviors (directly, indirectly, or through intangible
     means). Influence can be accumulated, spent, or lost depending on how it is wielded. For instance, the United
     States exercises influence through diplomatic efforts, economic efforts (through institutions like the
     International Monetary Fund and World Bank), and global leadership in institutions like the United Nations.
     By playing a leading role in brokering peace agreements, the United States shapes regional dynamics and
     align with U.S. interests in stability and security.

     1-24. Advantage is the superiority of position or condition that enables a nation to achieve desired outcomes.
     Nations can create an advantage by accumulating influence or by leveraging the instruments of national
     power (diplomatic, informational, military, and economic). For example, the U.S. maintains a technological
     advantage through innovation in sectors like artificial intelligence and defense systems.

     1-25. Leverage is the application of gained advantage to create effects or exploit opportunities. When a
     nation has leverage, it is better positioned to promote and protect its interests. For example, the
     United States uses its economic leverage (such as control over global financial systems and trade
     agreements) to influence the policies of other nations and global economic policies, ensuring favorable
     conditions for trade and investment.

     1-26. In the context of strategic competition, national strategic influence can be understood as the ability of
     a nation to shape the global environment in ways that align with its interests. This influence is exercised
     through a combination of soft power (cultural diplomacy and foreign aid) and hard power (military alliances
     and economic sanctions).

Persistent Behavior

     1-39. These are behaviors that continue despite the lack of U.S. military presence in the environment.
     Societal norms provide examples of persistent behavior (such as driving on the left side of the road,
     donating blood and organs, and body adornment). Cultural norms provide examples as well, for example
     the treatment of women, children, and the elderly; and accepting items with one hand versus the other.
     Language and dialect, dietary staples and culinary traditions, religious practices, agricultural practices,
     marriage and family structure, and informal economies provide other examples of persistent behavior.
     Unlike episodic behaviors, persistent behaviors occur regardless of the setting or situation. They stem from
     a connection a group has with society or culture.

Episodic Behavior

     1-40. Episodic behaviors occur consistently in response to specific stimuli. Examples of episodic behavior
     include voting, customs and courtesies, and autonomic reflexes. An example of an autonomic reflex is
     smelling something fatty and salty which triggers an autonomic response (feeling hungry), and the reflex
     would be to order an extra-large portion of fries. Seasonal migrations provide another example of episodic
     behavior. The movement of pastoral communities or herdsmen is triggered by the changing of seasons to
     find new grazing lands. Unlike persistent behaviors, episodic behaviors are a response to a specific trigger.

Practical Behavior

     1-41. Practical behavior serves a temporary and pragmatic purpose, such as some action that occurs during
     U.S. military (or another entity’s) presence but ceases during U.S. military absence. Examples include staying
     at home during military operations, observing military imposed curfews, and protesting U.S. presence. For
     example, using military-provided services like medical clinics, water purification stations, or generators
     occurs when the services only during a crisis. Hoarding goods, the stockpiling of fuel, food, or medicine in
     anticipation of shortages or movement restrictions imposed by military operations is another example to
     consider.

Timely Behavior

     1-42. These are actions prompted by stimuli that are only expected to occur once. Examples of timely
     behavior include reactions to feints and other tactical deception (TAC-D) activities, force protection
     activities, following crowd control instructions, and throwing objects at a public speaker. Responding to a
     warning shot by immediately dropping to the ground is one example. Another is following specific
     instructions broadcast from a military vehicle, such as “Move to the stadium for aid distribution!”.

Context

     1-43. Behaviors can be episodic in some contexts but not in others. For example, speaking loudly is episodic
     in response to noise but not if one has hearing problems. The intensity of the response can vary depending
     on the intensity of the trigger and other contextual factors. Culture can also strongly influence behavior and
     what triggers a response in one culture may not in another. Furthermore, even within a culture, individuals
     may have different thresholds for triggering behaviors based on their personal experiences.

     1-67. Unlike OP-D and TAC-D, DISO only targets foreign intelligence and does not focus on generating a

INFLUENCE OBJECTIVES

     1-93. Military operations are objective focused. Each operation has at least one objective and each objective
     is clearly defined. The DOD model for objectives begins with higher-level objectives that commanders and
     staffs at lower echelons support by creating and achieving supporting objectives. This integration continues
     as commanders and staffs identify intermediate objectives that fill the gap between the current situation and
     the lowest-level objective. This model also applies to PO Soldiers throughout the influence process
     (figure 1-4, page 11). PO Soldiers ensure any proposed influence objective—

          • Establishes a single desired result.
          • Links (directly or indirectly) to higher-level objectives or to the end state.
          • Does not imply ways or means.
          • Is not written as a task. It is—

                 Specific.
                 Measurable.
                 Achievable.
                 Realistic.
                 Time-bound or condition-bound.

PSYCHOLOGICAL OBJECTIVES

     1-96. A psychological objective is a statement that reflects the desired behavioral effect. Also called a PO.
     When developing psychological objectives, planners ensure they are specific, measurable, attainable, relevant,
     and time bound. If any of these elements are lacking (either directly in the objective statement, or in the
     operational context), it is likely not a valid objective. Psychological objectives may take years to achieve. To be
     time-bound, they must be tied to distinct events or measurable changes in behavior or conditions.

SUPPORTING PSYCHOLOGICAL OBJECTIVES

     1-97. A supporting psychological objective is the desired behavior of a foreign target audience that
     contributes to accomplishing a psychological objective. Also called an SPO. SPOs are unique for each
     psychological objective; and each psychological objective always has two or more SPOs.

     1-98. If it is impossible to develop two or more SPOs from a psychological objective, the psychological
     objective is too narrow in focus and must be rewritten. All SPOs must assist in achieving the psychological
     objective. The ability to write specific, measurable SPOs validates the psychological objective. The ability to
     write specific measurable indicators (for measures of effectiveness) validates the SPOs. If it is impossible to
     write measurable indicators for a subsequent objective, the higher objective is invalid and must be rewritten.

                                 Competition

    This chapter identifies the tactics, practices, and resources that Psychological Operations units
    use to achieve or enable positions of relative advantage. This chapter emphasizes the importance
    of the commander’s operational approach and the various types of security cooperation and
    military engagement programs and activities that support the Army’s role in our nation’s strategic
    competition and integrated deterrence efforts while preparing for war.

OVERVIEW

     2-1. Competition deters aggression, prepares for armed conflict should deterrence fail, counters adversaries’
     competitive strategies that threaten U.S. national interests, and supports the efforts of interorganizational
     partners. U.S. strategic competition is dependent on unified action partners that act as force multipliers to
     enhance Army capabilities, optimize its advantages, and mitigate its vulnerabilities.

ARMY

     2-2. The Army engages in competition and contributes to joint force efforts to protect and advance U.S.
     national interests by applying military power to gain a competitive advantage. Army forces contest adversary
     activities by supporting combatant command campaign plans and theater Army operations and preparing for
     large-scale combat operations with unified action partners. The objectives that Army forces help achieve are
     typically aligned to deterring adversary malign action, setting conditions for armed conflict on favorable
     terms should deterrence fail, and shaping environments with unified action partners to support U.S. strategic
     interests.

PSYCHOLOGICAL OPERATIONS UNITS

     2-3. The activities to achieve these objectives fall under numerous programs within a combatant command.
     Therefore, specific influence activities that Psychological Operations units execute may not strictly fall under
     a PSYOP program or a combatant command MILDEC plan. These activities could be any of several other
     programs such as security cooperation and military engagement programs. Whatever the case, commanders
     and staffs integrate and synchronize those activities with the plans and objectives of the Department of State,
     other government agencies, country teams and the relevant ambassadors. However, the units primarily
     support the combatant command campaign plan.

     2-4. During competition, specific activities deter adversary actions; shape the operational environment to
     better enable friendly operations; create and deepen human, physical, and information advantages; and
     establish favorable conditions for a potential shift to armed conflict. Since adversaries tend to pursue long-
     term strategic objectives, enduring DOD engagement and influence activities are vital to ensure lasting
     outcomes and derail adversary objectives. Such shaping and related activities coincide with overall
     preparation for large-scale combat operations if deterrence fails. Influence activities in this context shape the
     operational environment to create and build positions of advantage (relative to threats) if conditions devolve
     into crisis.

     2-5. Typical echelons employing Psychological Operations units include combatant commands, theater
     special operations commands, and theater armies. Since those echelons participate in joint, regional,
     multinational, and bilateral exercises, Psychological Operations units also participate, generally in line with
     established rules of allocation or operation plans. Battalions aligned to specific geographic regions,
     transregional objectives, or named operations rely on their entire battalion to create persistent presence and
     achieve strategic outcomes or end states.

     campaign plan or in support of a component command’s supporting operations. A challenge with discussing
     planning regarding campaign plans is that they are always in execution; therefore, Psychological Operations

     2-12. Security cooperation is Department of Defense interactions with foreign security establishments to

     enables and encourages unified action partners to provide U.S. forces with essential access, develop and
     apply their capabilities and capacity to address shared threats, and carry out other activities that promote
     U.S. security interests.

     2-13. In security cooperation, Psychological Operations personnel establish and maintain relationships with
     regional counterparts to build partner capability and capacity, increase interoperability, and conduct planning

                                      Crisis

    This chapter addresses Psychological Operations unit operations and activities from the Army’s
    strategic context of crisis. This includes the range of operations found in the categories of crisis
    response and limited contingency operations (in particular, operations to shape and operations
    to prevent both providing frameworks to achieve specific objectives).

OVERVIEW

     3-1. Crisis response and limited contingency operations focus on changing an adversary’s risk calculus by
     raising the adversary’s costs for actions that threaten U.S. interests. In crisis response and limited contingency
     operations, Psychological Operations units conduct influence activities that support threat deterrence.
     Deterrence is the prevention of action by the existence of a credible threat of unacceptable counteraction
     and/or belief that the cost of action outweighs the perceived benefits (JP 3-0). Psychological Operations units
     provide commanders and other partners with a means to affect threat decision-making as well as shape the
     operational environment to the U.S. advantage.

     3-2. Deterrence and other long-term persistent and preventive shaping activities help assure partners of the
     USG resolve and commitment to shared interests. However, deterring the threat is their primary objective.
     Psychological Operations unit influence activities may be executed in support of combatant commander
     objectives focused on assuring partners.

     3-3. In a crisis, limited contingency operations focus on protecting U.S. interests through a combination of
     military force deployment and interorganizational activities. If a crisis escalates to conflict, the joint force
     may have to respond by executing large-scale combat operations. However, most crisis responses call for a
     limited contingency operation that is short of armed conflict. Even though the limited contingency operation
     may be short of armed conflict, this does not mean the joint force cannot use lethal force; the rules of
     engagement determine such use. Limited contingency operations are narrow in scope, scale, and objectives—
     such as in personnel recovery and noncombatant evacuation operations.

             Note: Use of the term crisis in this context focuses on situations involving threat actions and
             activities. The joint force also employs Army Psychological Operations units in operations during
             natural disaster crises like the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunamis. While post-natural disaster crises may
             have an influence component, the primary focus may be informative in nature regarding security,
             health, and safety issues confronting affected populations. In such cases, influence efforts focus
             on deterring, mitigating, and defeating malign actors and rallying national and international
             support for the affected nations.

     3-4. Geopolitical events can quickly deteriorate into a crisis that requires a limited contingency operation
     involving planned activities in a campaign plan. However, all crises do not fall within the parameters of an
     existing plan. Consequently, advantages achieved in competition and consolidated as gains form the
     foundation for plans and activities to counter an adversary during a crisis.

     3-5. Whether specific plans exist or don’t exist for a given situation, regionally aligned Psychological Operations
     units conduct continuous analysis of prioritized environments and the potential TAs to ensure any response can be
     as quick and effective as possible.

     3-6. As a crisis escalates, Psychological Operations units may deploy as part of Army forces that establish an
     early forward presence, provide primary forces, or operate as part of a larger task force effort. Their influence
     activities directly contribute to—

          • Assessment.
          • Assurance.
          • Shaping the operational environment.
          • Provision of active deterrence.

          Year 2030: The agrarian country of Pyristina has suffered through years of drought

          Pyristina’s neighbor to the east, Cybvia, has disputed the internationally recognized

          The border region between Pyristina and Cybvia is demographically dominated by
          people of Puduan ethnicity who historically considered themselves more Puduan than
          Cybvian or Pyristinan. People of Puduan ethnicity live on either side of the international
          border within the demilitarized zone.

          Claiming risks to national security and the safety of its citizens in the demilitarized zone,
          the Cybvia government deploys military forces garrisoned in the Eastern portion of the
          nation to forcibly remove ethnic Puduan civilians from the demilitarized zone.
          The civilians, a mix of Cybvian and Pyristinan citizens are trucked to temporary camps
          set up in soccer stadiums in Eastern Cybvia in the same city as the deployed Cybvia
          military unit’s garrison. The Cybvian government occupies and declares martial law in
          the demilitarized zone.

          The dislocated ethnic Puduan Cybvia citizens were not interned, merely housed in the
          stadiums. Out of work, out of school, bored, and stoked by perceptions of mistreatment,
          petty crime became rampant and provided a means to strike out against the ethnic
          majority. Petty misconduct quickly escalated to violent crime.

          In response, the Pyristinan Influence Brigade sent targeted messages to the occupying
          Cybvian military units. Leaflets, radio broadcasts, social media posts, and films
          encouraged Cybvian soldiers to abandon their posts and return home to protect their
          families.

          Within 48 hours, individual deserters turned into dozens; dozens turned into entire units
          leaving their posts. Cybvian military leaders in the target zones made no attempt to
          stop their soldiers from leaving their posts to go home and protect their families.

          The Pyristina Ranger Regiment conducted an air assault into the demilitarized zone

          terrain. This allowed Pyristina conventional infantry units to immediately widen the
          secured areas and establish civil order without firing a shot.

          The Cybvian government lifted its martial law restrictions and ordered the remaining
          Cybvian units to return to their garrisons. The displaced civilians were allowed to leave
          the temporary camps in the east and return to their homes in the demilitarized zone.

    This chapter discusses armed conflict in the context of Army large-scale combat operations.
    Throughout this chapter, large-scale combat operations, armed conflict, and war are used
    interchangeably.

OVERVIEW

     4-1. According to JP 1 Volume 1, armed conflict or war “characterizes a strategic relationship surrounding
     an interest or set of interests where adversaries use lethal force as the primary means for imposing their will
     and achieving their objectives.” International law distinguishes armed conflict from localized disturbances
     (such as riots and violent protests) by the intensity of the conflict, organization of the actors involved, and
     the capacity of each actor to sustain armed violence.

     4-2. The employment of lethal force is the defining characteristic of armed conflict and is the primary
     function of the Army. Consequently, war and warfare are the Army's primary concerns in operations as the
     focus is on the ability to defeat threats.

     4-3. In war, the immediate effect of lethality is in the physical dimension, reducing the enemy’s capability
     and capacity to fight. However, the utility of this lethal force extends into the information and human
     dimensions where this military action (along with the other instruments of national power) influences enemy
     behavior, decision-making, and the will to fight.

     4-4. The Army’s operational concept is multidomain operations. Multidomain operations as described and
     framed in the current edition of ADP 3-0 represent the intellectual approach to the operations that Army
     forces conduct, not a type of operation. The Army plans and conducts all operations by integrating
     information with all available capabilities in a combined arms approach to—

          • Enable command and control.
          • Protect data, information, and networks.
          • Inform audiences.
          • Influence threats and foreign relevant actors.
          • Attack the threat’s ability to exercise command and control.

     4-5. According to ADP 3-13, “As a dynamic of combat power, Army forces fight for, defend, and fight with
     information to create and exploit information advantages—the use, protection, and exploitation of
     information to achieve objectives more effectively than enemies and adversaries.” Psychological Operations
     units are a critical part of any combined arms approach but particularly in
     large-scale combat operations where influence activities can protect lives and preserve resources.

     4-6. When deterrence fails and conflict becomes the inevitable or chosen course of action, the initial military
     response is usually to change the enemy’s behavior using defeat mechanisms. A defeat mechanism is the
     method through which friendly forces accomplish their mission against enemy opposition by creating
     physical and psychological effects. Physical effects can deprive an enemy of the ability to achieve objectives
     while psychological effects can deprive them of both the ability and the will to do so.

     4-7. Chapters 2 and 3 describe activities whose objectives are to deter conflict and set conditions for the Army to
     prevail should conflict become the inevitable or chosen course of action. The vignette titled United States Army
     Psychological Operations Detachment in the Republic of Korea, page 37, provides an example of activities
     conducted to strengthen a vital partnership, increase interoperability with a long-time ally to achieve mutual
     objectives and develop shared understanding, and contribute to strategic deterrence while preparing to prevail
     against one of our nation’s threats.

     4-8. Armed conflict demands a comprehensive influence effort that leverages all available capabilities to
     shape enemy behavior. Psychologically focused activities target the minds of the TA by using a tailored
     combination of capabilities, tactics, techniques, and procedures to achieve the desired outcomes. In armed
     conflict, aggressive, threat-focused, and offensive influence activities are destructive, divisive, deceptive, and

                          Targeted Killing as a Psychological Action

          On April 18, 1943, the U.S. Army Air Corps executed Operation VENGEANCE. This
          was the first acknowledged and verified U.S. targeted killing of a high-ranking enemy
          nation military leader—Imperial Japanese Navy Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto, architect
          of the Pearl Harbor attack and arguably the Japanese Empire’s best leader. Based on
          intercepted and deciphered Japanese military-coded transmissions, Army Air Corps
          fighters knew where his transport bomber and escort aircraft would be and when. With
          less than a week of preparation—and having to cross hundreds of miles of ocean over
          hostile territory—U.S. pilots hunted down and swiftly killed the admiral while losing one
          plane and pilot.

          Reportedly, aside from the expected decrease in combat effectiveness of the Imperial
          Japanese Navy due to the loss of one of their greatest military leaders, U.S. planners
          primarily intended to inflict a devastating psychological shock on Japan while providing
          a strong psychological boost for the United States. Yamamoto’s killing provided both.

          The Japanese government hid the news of his death for over a month out of justified
          concerns it would demoralize the military and civilians alike. Accordingly, it was a shock
          to national morale when it was finally acknowledged. In contrast, Americans celebrated
          an act of revenge against the man who led the sneak attack on Pearl Harbor. While
          targeted killing remains controversial, the psychological effects of doing so in specific
          circumstances are undeniable.

THE LAW OF ARMED CONFLICT

     4-35. In addition to U.S. and international law and agreements, military law requires Psychological
     Operations units to follow the law of armed conflict when targeting a threat’s support groups and
     organizations, especially civilians. While these supporting entities are indirect and secondary to the main
     threat, they still enable the threat to operate. This raises the question of whether they are legitimate targets
     for lethal and nonlethal actions. Legal experts may designate these supporting entities as combatants if they
     meet the legal criteria. If designated as combatants, they are legitimate targets under the rules of war. If they
     remain noncombatants, influence is limited to nonlethal actions.

     4-36. FM 6-27 notes that in determining when civilians may be directly targeted, neither treaty, law, nor
     customary international law provides a definition of the phrase “direct part in hostilities.” At a minimum,
     it includes actions that are, by their very nature and purpose, intended to cause actual harm to the enemy.
     Taking a direct part in hostilities extends beyond engaging in combat, it includes certain acts that are an
     integral part of combat operations or that effectively and substantially contribute to an adversary’s ability to
     conduct or sustain combat operations. However, directly participating in hostilities is not the only way that
     members of the civilian population contribute to their state’s war effort. For example, they may do so by
     working in a munitions factory or buying war bonds. The supporting staff judge advocate can provide more
     information about specific legal and policy parameters of influence targeting.

     4-37. Violations of the law of armed conflict can be a friendly vulnerability that the enemy can exploit and
     can be a potential target of opportunity for friendly forces when the enemy violates it. When the enemy
     violates the law, Psychological Operations units can exploit the opportunity by depicting the illegitimacy of
     the action to the civilian population in the conflict area and members of military coalitions, thereby
     influencing the national will within their respective countries.

MAIN EFFORT

     5-12. A main effort is a designated subordinate unit whose mission at a given point in time is most critical to
     overall mission success (ADP 3-0). Designation of a main effort reflects the establishment of a priority
     capability and a flexible and adjustable hierarchy for other capabilities based on mission requirements.
     As the mission evolves, the main effort changes to whatever capability becomes the necessary focus to best
     achieve the desired outcome. In the beginning of an operation, a Psychological Operations unit may conduct
     deception activities with the coordinated support of cyberspace units to disintegrate enemy unit cohesion,
     create infighting and dissension, and elicit bad decision-making. Once the full attack begins, combat arms
     units become the main effort with Psychological Operations units assuming a supporting effort.

SUPPORTING EFFORT

     5-13. A supporting effort is a designated subordinate unit with a mission that supports the success of the main
     effort (ADP 3-0). In general, units operating in a supporting effort role receive fewer resources and assets
     than the main effort (as the latter is where the command focus lies). However, supporting efforts are still
     critical to the success of the main effort. In the case of influence activities, efforts to break down and
     disorganize threat capabilities and forces can provide crucial disaggregation, disruption, and dilution of
     enemy efforts to resist U.S. forces and can increase the effectiveness of a friendly attack.

TASK ORGANIZATION CONSIDERATIONS

     5-14. Task-organizing is the act of designing a force, support staff, or sustainment package of specific size
     and composition to meet a unique task or mission (ADP 3-0). Task organization is a temporary grouping of
     forces designed to accomplish a particular mission (ADP 5-0). A commander task organizes units to enhance
     the ability to execute a specific mission.

     5-15. Task organization is part of the planning process. Task organization enables commanders to detach and
     move units, integrate new personnel and units into existing force structures, and allow for sufficient time to
     implement organizational changes for subordinate leaders and units. This planning produces a base plan,
     order, and Annex A (Task Organization) to a base plan or order that all direct a unit’s task organization.
     Planning may also produce an operation plan or operation order that both provide changes to task
     organization by phase. Commanders modify the task organization as required by the situation during
     execution through fragmentary orders. FM 5-0 provides more information on planning and orders.

PRIORITY OF SUPPORT

     5-21. In addition to task-organizing, commanders establish priorities of support during planning, and shift
     priorities during execution as the situation requires. A priority of support is a priority set by the commander
     to ensure a subordinate unit has support in accordance with its relative importance to accomplish the mission

     Establishing priorities of support are particularly critical when it comes to a limited resource like fires.
     This prioritization is a fundamental principle to consider when task-organizing Psychological Operations units
     to accomplish missions wherever those missions fall in the Army’s strategic context for employment of forces
     or across the range of military operations.

MUTUAL SUPPORT

     5-22. Commanders and staffs consider mutual support when determining how large an area to assign
     subordinates. Mutual support is that support which units render each other throughout joint operations,
     because of their assigned tasks, their position relative to each other and to the enemy, or their inherent
     capabilities (JP 3-31). In Army doctrine, mutual support is a planning consideration related to force
     disposition; it is not a command relationship. Mutual support has two aspects: supporting range and
     supporting distance. Given that the production, distribution, and dissemination capabilities of Psychological
     Operations units is not equivalent across formations, establishing support relationships is a principle to
     consider when task organizing these units—whether across a joint task force or within an Army echelon
     (such as a corps or division).

     5-23. Some terms related to support relationships include—
          • Close support. Action of the supporting force against targets or objectives that are sufficiently near
                the supported force as to require detailed integration or coordination of the supporting action with
                fire, movement, or other actions of the supported force (JP 3-31).
          • Direct Support. A support relationship requiring a force to support another specific force and
                authorizing it to answer directly to the supported force’s request for assistance (FM 3-0).
          • General Support. Support given to the supported force as a whole and not to any particular
                subdivision thereof (JP 3-09.3).
          • General Support—Reinforcing. A support relationship assigned to a unit to support the force as a
                whole and to reinforce another similar type unit. (FM 3-0).

COMMAND AND CONTROL

     5-53. The command and control warfighting function is the related tasks and a system that enable
     commanders to synchronize and converge all elements of combat power (ADP 3-0). Since the command and
     control warfighting function enables and facilitates the other functions, assigned PO Soldiers and unit leaders
     at their respective echelons are crucial to ensure integration of the warfighting functions and influence-
     focused capabilities to effectively execute operations and achieve objectives.

     5-54. A critical contribution to the warfighting function is the integration of influence activities and related
     information (such as TA and locations of contact with adversary influence capabilities) into the common
     operational picture. Planners and unit leaders use command and control processes to employ capabilities
     to create and exploit physical, human, and informational advantages as well as increase situational
     understanding, facilitate decision making, and enable better effects assessment. Command and control
     empowers and aids Psychological Operations planners and unit leaders to integrate influence activities
     into overall military operations for unity of effort. For more information on command and control, refer
     to ADP 6-0.

MOVEMENT AND MANEUVER

     5-55. The movement and maneuver warfighting function is the related tasks and systems that move and
     employ forces to achieve a position of relative advantage over the enemy and other threats (ADP 3-0).

     focused on positioning and dispersing forces (either in whole or in part) when maneuvering. Commanders

     operational advantages against threats.

     5-56. Psychological Operations planners and unit leaders use the movement and maneuver warfighting
     function to place and disperse units for maximum utility and effectiveness. For example, dispersed
     Psychological Operations units and elements at the tactical level can contribute to maneuver tasks like

     5-57. Higher echelon Psychological Operations units generate large amounts of information that aid movement
     and maneuver through analysis of potential and declared threats to identify and attack their vulnerabilities to
     achieve operational advantages. In doing so, Psychological Operations units and organizations (like task forces)
     can help Army and joint forces better position themselves relative to threat forces.

     5-58. Psychological Operations units and organizations also shape the information dimension of the
     operational environment to more effectively influence both threats and populations to the U.S. advantage

FIRES

     5-59. The fires warfighting function is the related tasks and systems that create and converge effects in all domains

INTELLIGENCE

     5-61. The intelligence warfighting function is the related tasks and systems that facilitate understanding the
     enemy, terrain, weather, civil considerations, and other significant aspects of the operational environment
     (ADP 3-0). The intelligence warfighting function supports operations through a broad range of supporting
     doctrinal tasks, referred to as intelligence warfighting function tasks. All Army units and organizations perform

     units are supporting other units integrating with the intelligence staff to support execution of warfighting
     function tasks is a key consideration for the unit.

     5-62. The intelligence warfighting function tasks include—
          • Provide intelligence support to force generation.
          • Provide support to situational understanding.
          • Conduct information collection.
          • Provide intelligence support to targeting.

     operational environment, collection and analysis of information, and identification of influence-related
     information requirements for the commander. See FM 2-0 for more information on the information

PROTECTION

     5-64. The protection warfighting function is the related tasks and systems, and methods that prevent or mitigate
     detection, threat effects, and hazards to preserve combat power and enable freedom of action (FM 3-0).
     Protection includes everything that decreases Army force vulnerabilities to detection and destruction.
     Understanding threats and hazards enables commanders and staffs to prioritize requirements and commit
     capabilities and resources according to those priorities.

     5-65. Potential threats reside within the operational environment and in space, cyberspace, and outside the
     area of operations, so protective measures account for them all. Since threats present continuous hazards to
     operations, personnel, and capabilities; planning, preparation, execution, and assessment of protection
     measures are continuous and permanent activities. The Army employs both active and passive protection
     measures to safeguard personnel, equipment, movement, and operations. Active protection measures involve
     the interception or destruction of the delivery systems, sensors, and data of threats and hazards before they
     can inflict damage on their intended targets. Passive protection measures include protecting personnel and
     equipment from detection and the effects of threats and hazards.

SECTION II – ADDITIONAL INTELLIGENCE AND ANALYSIS CONSIDERATIONS

     5-85. PO Soldiers are both intensive consumers of intelligence and frequent providers of information with
     potential intelligence value. Intelligence is vital for planning, development, delivery, and assessment.
     Intelligence supports these activities through fusion analysis and the development of timely, relevant, and
     directed information applicable to planning. Planning and execution require intelligence about the enemy,
     adversary, friendly and neutral individuals and groups, and other aspects of the operational environment to
     accomplish mission objectives. Intelligence enables a shared understanding of the enemy, the operational
     area, areas of influence, areas of interest, and the effects of each on friendly and threat operations.

     5-86. Intelligence also contributes directly to the prediction of probable enemy actions and messages
     (predictive intelligence). Predictive intelligence enables planners to address adversary countermeasures and
     assess potential friendly reactions to and consequences of influence efforts.

     5-87. Intelligence predictions are estimates or forecasts and there is risk in formulating plans based solely on
     intelligence assessments. While prediction is inexact and vulnerable to incomplete information, human behavior
     can be predictable to various degrees. However, free will enables humans to behave outside of anticipated
     behavior. To compensate, planners utilize intelligence to predict normative behavior and plan for abnormalities.
     Although imperfect, intelligence provides probable threat courses of action derived from verified or probable
     threat information capabilities and intent. When applied to the targeting cycle, two of the most important aspects
     of intelligence are target development and the timely assessment of desired effects. ADP 2-0 provides additional
     information on intelligence and ATP 2-33.4 provides more information about predictive intelligence.

     5-88. Target intelligence is intelligence that portrays and locates the components of a target or target complex
     and indicates its vulnerability and relative importance (JP 3-60). According to ATP 2-33.4, target
     development involves the systematic analysis of threat forces and operations to determine high-value targets,
     high-payoff targets, and systems and system components for potential engagement through maneuver, fires,
     electromagnetic warfare, or information operations (information advantage).

Center of Gravity Analysis

     5-113. Center of gravity analysis is a method that links a threat’s center of gravity (a military force,

     5-117. This section describes analytical approaches that can be used prior to or in conjunction with traditional
     operational environment analyses to provide commanders an understanding of who are the actors in the
     environment. This understanding facilitates decision-making, focuses analytical effort, and applies
     operational art.

             Note: This section does not provide details about this task in terms of the physical areas and

Issues, Goals, Influence, Vulnerabilities, and Opportunities

     5-120. The analytical method that uses issues, goals, influence, vulnerabilities, and opportunities (IGIVO)
     has been applied in many environments and proven valuable in helping staffs identify and inform
     commanders on what and who are important and why. IGIVO is applicable from combatant commander to
     tactical commander and allows them to provide informed direction for further staff focus and analysis.
     Therefore, the results of IGIVO analyses help focus operational efforts to achieve assigned objectives.

     5-121. Analysts assess each component of IGIVO from the actor’s perspective (not the perspective of the
     staff) as explained in the following paragraphs. The actors could be countries, populations, human networks,
     government elements, or sub-groups in a village.

     5-122. Issues are what the party perceives to be a problem, grievance, or need. An example of an issue is a
     town whose commercially supplied water source is tainted. Another example is a national government that
     is experiencing domestic terrorism, and which notices an increase in extremism.

     5-123. Goals are the ends the party wants to achieve. Goals are enduring end states. They are not the near-
     mid-, or long-term objectives of the party. Using the tainted water example, the goal of the townspeople is to
     have a safe, dependable, and attainable water source. Using the national government example, a goal may be
     to maintain a safe, secure, united, and governed environment.

     5-124. Influence, in this context, is the capacity or power of persons or things to be a compelling force on or
     produce effects on the actions, behavior, opinions, and so on of others. In the tainted water issue, the
     townspeople may think they have no capacity for influence, or they may think they can create influence
     through nonviolent rallies and by creating awareness through social media. The water company may believe
     its greatest influence on the situation is by being transparent in their actions and acknowledging the source
     of the problem. The state government may understand its influence comes from the ability to apply legal
     action against the water company while providing purification assistance to the company and externally
     sourced clean water for the townspeople.

     5-125. Vulnerabilities are fluid. Vulnerabilities are how the party perceives they are capable of being hurt,
     or susceptible to being hurt (physically, morally, or via criticism), or a blow to their efforts. The townspeople
     in the tainted water situation may perceive that their vulnerability is having a single, commercially supplied
     water source. The national government dealing with domestic terrorism and growing internal extremism may
     assess their greatest vulnerability as the perceptions of other governments that they are not taking human
     rights seriously and are at risk of losing economic ties, security assistance, and humanitarian aid.

     5-126. Opportunities are situations, conditions, or prospects that the party perceives as favorable for
     attainment or advancement toward a goal. The commercial water company may perceive this as an
     opportunity to receive state and federal aid to upgrade their infrastructure with little to no immediate internal
     financial commitment. This perceived opportunity increases their perception that they are on the side of the
     public by providing a safe, dependable, and attainable water source. In the domestic terrorism and extremism
     situation, anti-government groups may perceive that their greatest opportunity is through nonviolent
     resistance. Therefore, increasing visibility on what they perceive is a failure by the government to abide by
     most of the values of the public and positioning themselves in the perception of public as a potential legitimate
     governing party.

     5-127. By providing the IGIVO components as perceived by each party, the intelligence staff provides the
     commander with the situational awareness and knowledge to make informed decisions. The commander can

Irregular Warfare Strategic Estimate

     5-128. The USG conducts war with a combination of conventional and irregular warfare. From a DOD
     perspective, any operation outside of declared legal state of war is conducted under the umbrella of irregular
     warfare. Irregular warfare is also the predominant form of warfare used by actors who suffer asymmetric
     capabilities in relation to their opposition (most commonly a nation state). State-based competition tends to
     be irregular. According to Crafting Strategy for Irregular Warfare: A Framework for Analysis and Action,
     this “blend[s] separate instruments of power to offset military weakness, weaponize[s] narratives to ease
     strategic progress, and exploit[s] social and political contradictions to undermine and divide target societies.”
     While doctrine identifies this as irregular warfare, within this last decade, this blending has been described
     as “hybrid war” or “the gray zone.” The problem, roots, frame and narrative, threat strategy, and present
     response (PRFNTP) framework was developed to analyze this strategic-level use of irregular warfare.

5-135. The analyst determines which root causes are being used by the threat to mobilize support and whether

5-136. In addressing frames and narratives, it is important to understand the impact of information
technologies on societies and how actors exploit these technologies to create desired impacts. The use of
information technologies to disseminate tailored content has created symmetry between adversaries despite
the existence of asymmetric capabilities in diplomatic, economic, and military areas.

5-137. The wielding of information for effect by other nations and foreign relevant actors is called many
different things. U.S. doctrine categorizes all these efforts as information warfare, but the term remains
undefined, and this use of undefined jargon may cloud analysis. Others use descriptors like cognitive warfare,
psychological warfare, political warfare, propaganda, influence warfare, and cyber warfare. In the context of
this strategic analysis, frames and narratives are disseminated and amplified through information
technologies as just another capability in irregular warfare. The desired effects include:

     • Building legitimacy.
     • Eroding the legitimacy of the other.
     • Constraining options available to the competitor.
     • Favorably tilting the balance of strategic power.

5-138. A frame focuses attention on the proposed reality or paradigm, excluding other perceptions of reality
or paradigms. The frame is made up of three parts: diagnostic, motivational, and prognostic and each part
may be called a frame or a means of framing.

5-139. The diagnostic serves as the hook. It is designed to catch attention and explain (from the perspective
of the actor) what is wrong and who is responsible. The hook primes the target for a proposed solution (action)
by using grievances and root causes. However, not all TAs are called to action; they may provide moral
support but will not act without proper motivation.

5-140. Motivational framing provides the justification of personal risk for the good of the collective group.
Motivational framing motivates people into action by:

     • Pitching the in-group against an out-group.
     • Presenting sacrifice, injury, and struggle as a means to obtain future redemption, hope, and peace.
     • Providing mechanisms for group radicalization (such as isolation, threat, splitting, competition, hate,

          martyrdom).
     • Espousing ideology.

5-141. Effective motivation has depth and elicits strong emotions. Motivation is also used beyond the grass
roots level. Nation states use motivational framing to prompt individuals to action and justify violence and
sacrifice for the greater good. Both authoritarian and democratic states have histories of motivational framing.
One effective example is the motivations used by opposing sides in World War I and World War II.
In contrast, USG motivation for the Vietnam conflict did not resonate as broadly to its population and
other nations.

5-142. Diagnostic and motivational framing are combined with prognostic framing, which provides the
solution. The solution describes the path that leads from misery to the desired end state by presenting that
path as just, correct, necessary, and urgent.

5-143. Threat strategy can be conceptualized using ends, ways and means.

5-144. Describing and understanding the strategy employed by the threat must include more than a list of
activities or use of overarching terms. Analysts must determine the desired objectives of the threat, its
selected course(s) of action, how that course of action is being employed and, most meaningfully, answer
why for each of those. Publicly stated objectives may not always be the true objectives of a movement; they
may only serve to mobilize support. The analysts’ foremost consideration should be determining what the
movement leaders want.

     5-160. Information for effect is the use, publication, or broadcast of factual information to negatively affect

          • The premature announcement of collateral damage caused by friendly forces.
          • Release of information out of context.
          • Reporting or images of the results of insurgent attacks on friendly forces.
          • Release of captured sensitive or classified information.

MISINFORMATION

     5-161. Misinformation is unintentional incorrect information from any source (ADP 3-13). In an influence
     context, misinformation is simply incorrect information disseminated due to ignorance of its inaccuracy to
     solicit a response or interest from a potential recipient, or even for unknown reasons.

DISINFORMATION

     5-162. Disinformation is incomplete, incorrect, or out of context information deliberately used to influence
     audiences (ADP 3-13). Disinformation is derived, primarily from adversary intelligence organizations or
     other covert entities. Disinformation is designed to distort information and deceive or influence U.S. decision
     makers, U.S. forces and unified action partners via indirect or unconventional means.

PROPAGANDA

     5-163. Propaganda is any form of adversary communication—especially of a biased or misleading nature—
     designed to influence the opinions, emotions, attitudes, or behavior of any group to benefit the sponsor, either
     directly or indirectly. Disinformation and information for effect are forms of propaganda.

     5-164. Adversary information activities may be stand-alone efforts or conducted simultaneously with other
     activities. Adversary information activities aim to augment psychological effects and amplify the effects of
     lethal actions. Adversaries disseminate messages to deliberately—

          • Undermine the legitimacy of U.S. operations and activities.
          • Decrease the credibility of USG narratives, themes, and messages.
          • Prevent the achievement of U.S. objectives.

     5-165. Face-to-face intimidation, threatening pamphlets, and propaganda broadcasts are some of the ways
     the adversary engages in influential messaging. When messages fail to elicit a desired effect, the addition of
     violent activities becomes a more spectacular means of influence. For example, the kidnapping and killing
     of host-nation troops and the broadcasting of the event suggests to local populations and international
     audiences the government can neither protect its own soldiers nor provide a secure and stable environment.
     The potential result is an undermining of popular confidence in the effectiveness of the government and
     its institutions.

SECTION III – ADDITIONAL SUSTAINMENT CONSIDERATIONS

     5-208. Psychological Operations units rely on the gaining unit for the necessary logistics, finance, personnel,
     and health service support to sustain operations. Psychological Operations units employ specialized
     equipment and have unique funding needs that require continual logistical and monetary support. These
     requirements differ for each mission and are addressed during planning. Psychological Operations forces also
     require consistent and timely services and logistics to execute the core tasks and contribute to the overall
     mission. Consequently, sustainment planning is critical to operation success.

     5-209. Because Psychological Operations units lack organic sustainment capabilities, unit leaders participate
     in preparing documents and deployment orders that clearly state the processes and procedures to sustain the
     force and to identify the entities that have the appropriate responsibilities. Otherwise, Psychological
     Operations unit leaders may find themselves without support and run the risk of mission failure.

     5-210. Gaining units must plan for the sustainment of attached Psychological Operations units. Failure to do
     so adversely affects the ability of Psychological Operations forces to operate properly and efficiently, thereby
     potentially jeopardizing mission success.

     5-211. Sustainment planning should occur early to ensure proper integration of Psychological Operations
     units into the sustainment warfighting function. Defining support relationships in the theater support plan
     increases mission effectiveness and facilitates integration of assigned or attached Psychological Operations
     units. Message traffic that requests and authorizes the deployment of forces provides details about
     sustainment support relationships and funding.

     Operations Command or United States Army Reserve Command to determine the materiel and service
     requirements required to sustain the force. Gaining commanders ensure the sustainment element of the
     gaining unit can provide support with billeting, all classes of supply, development services and supplies,

     5-250. The Army has two types of maintenance: field and sustainment. Field maintenance activities include
     battlefield damage assessment and repair, recovery and evacuation, controlled exchange (the removal of
     serviceable components from equipment that is non-mission capable), communication security maintenance,
     and contracted maintenance support. Operators, organic maintenance personnel, and direct support
     maintenance personnel of the gaining unit perform this type of maintenance. The focus is on identifying and
     replacing damaged components, which facilitates a quick return of equipment to operation. Minimizing the
     loss of the assets needed to conduct operations is the primary intent of field maintenance.

     5-251. Sustainment maintenance consists of repairing major end items and components for return to the
     supply system. Examples of this include rebuilding engines and transmissions for reuse. These items are cost-
     effective to repair and after rebuilding are placed back into the supply system. These services are not provided
     below the level of the sustainment brigade and are not used to fix information and communication-specific
     equipment. Non-Army-common equipment specific to Psychological Operations units returns to the system
     through other processes.

OPERATIONAL CONTRACT SUPPORT

                                                Appendix A

                     A Brief History of the Branch

    Militaries have used influence throughout history to demoralize enemies, win the loyalty and
    support of civilian populations, and support military and national objectives.

WORLD WAR I

     A-1. During World War I, the U.S. Army employed what was then called propaganda via a small
     Propaganda Section within the Press and Censorship Division under the G-2, General Headquarters,
     American Expeditionary Forces. Part of its activity was the utilization of leaflets and loudspeakers against
     enemy operations. The effectiveness of the psychological operations led German Generals Paul von
     Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff to later remark that American and Allied efforts had devastated their
     troops’ morale.

WORLD WAR II

     A-2. In World War II, the U.S Army built on its previous experience and executed a planned, coordinated,
     and synchronized psychological warfare (abbreviated to PsyWar at that time) campaign on a grand scale with
     devasting effects on the enemy. Staff sections like the Psychological Warfare Branch, Allied Force
     Headquarters; the Psychological Warfare Division, Supreme Headquarters, Allied Expeditionary Force; and
     other entities at the theater, Army group, and field Army levels in Europe and the Far East complemented the
     efforts of the U.S. Office of the Strategic Services and Office of War Information.

             Note: Like its more modern variation (PSYWAR), PsyWar is an abbreviation that is only included
             for historical reference; therefore, it is not included in the acronyms and abbreviations list.

THE KOREAN WAR

     A-3. During the Korean War, the Army appointed Brigadier General Robert A. McClure as Chief of a new
     Psychological Warfare Division, G-3, and later as Chief of the Office of Psychological Warfare (a special
     staff section at the Pentagon). McClure’s efforts led to the establishment of the Psywar Division and school
     at Fort Riley. In 1952 the PsyWar Center was established. The PsyWar Center was established at Fort Bragg
     and became the lead institution for training Soldiers in the art of special warfare (unconventional warfare,
     psychological warfare, and counterinsurgency). The PsyWar Center evolved into the modern
     USAJFKSWCS.

THE VIETNAM WAR

     A-4. During the Vietnam War, the Army prioritized what it formally termed psychological operations
     (PSYOP) in its war efforts aimed at defeating communism worldwide through supporting a broad range of
     military operations. In the 1960s, the Army enlarged the organizational structure by officially activating the
     2nd Psychological Operations Group, 4th Psychological Operations Group, 7th Psychological Operations
     Group, and several POBs. More important changes occurred over the years in relatively rapid order.
     On 1 October 1990, military occupational specialty 96F, Psychological Operations Specialist (a military
     intelligence military occupational specialty) converted to 37F and became its own functional area
     independent from military intelligence. On 18 November 1998, the Army established the Psychological
     Operations Regiment.

Appendix A

POST 9/11

     A-5. On 16 October 2006, Psychological Operations officially became a branch in the United States Army.
     As information capabilities played an ever-increasing role in supporting U.S. military objectives after 9/11,
     senior DOD leaders recognized the need to change the term PSYOP to reflect the nature of these activities
     more appropriately. Consequently, in December 2010, the SecDef redefined the activity as MISO to capture
     the more nuanced set of the military’s information and influence activities. While the terms, methodologies,
     and media have changed, the fundamental mission has remained the same since WWI: to influence behavior
     change in foreign individuals and or groups across the range of military operations to achieve U.S. military
     and national objectives.

     A-6. On XX December 2025, the SecDef signed a memorandum changing the term military information
     support operations to psychological operations to more accurately reflect the psychological effects these
     operations hinge upon.

                               Individual Skills

    As noted in Chapter 1, the PO Branch core competencies describe the capabilities required to
    execute PSYOP and DDAs and contribute to Army operations. The competencies are
    collective; however, the required skills are individual. The varying level of skills among
    individuals contribute to the collective competencies of the branch. Each core competency
    compliments the others, providing units the capability to plan and execute the two primary
    influence activities. The PO Branch core competencies are influencing foreign audiences and
    analyzing TAs.
    This appendix provides brief descriptions of those individual skills in which competency
    required. This establishes context for development of education and training materials.

SHARED SKILLS ACROSS THE CORE COMPETENCIES

     B-1. These shared skills, identified in figure 1-2, page 4, include active listening, verbal and nonverbal
     communication, critical thinking, and emotional control as addressed below.

ACTIVE LISTENING

     B-2. Active listening comprises three aspects: cognitive, emotional, and behavioral. It is providing full
     attention to words, tone of voice, and body language (implicit and explicit information) and being aware of
     and controlling emotional responses (staying calm, empathetic, or compassionate). It is also asking questions
     for clarity, comprehension, and understanding; and providing appropriate feedback and reflection to the
     speaker that is non-judgmental or biased.

COMMUNICATION (VERBAL AND NONVERBAL)

     B-3. This consists of abilities and techniques for conveying or exchanging information (knowledge, ideas,
     beliefs, values, feelings, or behaviors) using speech, images, signals, symbols, writing, gestures, or behavior
     for building relationships, rapport, collaboration, negotiation, influence, transactions, or other purposeful
     interaction or activity.

CRITICAL THINKING

     B-4. This is the art of analyzing and evaluating thinking with a view to improving it.

EMOTIONAL CONTROL

     B-5. Emotional control is recognition and understanding of one’s emotional responses with an ability to
     appropriately display emotion in each situation or context.

SKILLS REQUIRED FOR INFLUENCING FOREIGN AUDIENCES

     B-6. Influence is the ability to cause an effect in direct, indirect, or intangible ways. An enduring influence
     capability requires Soldiers with a diverse set of skills. For Soldiers to advance their proficiency in required
     skills from basic to expert levels demands deliberate institutional, unit, and individual development.
     Table B-1, page 112, lists and describes the skills required for influencing foreign audiences.
