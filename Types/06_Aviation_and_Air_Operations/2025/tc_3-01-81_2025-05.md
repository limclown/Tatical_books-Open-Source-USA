                                 Preface

This training circular (TC) standardizes the counter-small unmanned aircraft system (C-sUAS) gunnery
program and skill qualifications through performance-oriented, sequential, progressive, realistic, and
challenging training. TC 3-01.81 is used to develop and test the proficiency of individuals, crews, and battery
personnel in C-sUAS gunnery tasks. This TC references the current crew drills, collective tasks, unit task
list, and subtasks to be performed by individuals and crews for gunnery certification.

The target audience for this publication are commanders, trainers, evaluators, leaders, and service members
assigned to units at the brigade level and below. It could also aid the joint force commander in planning and
executing cohesive joint operations against unmanned aircraft system (UAS) threats throughout the
battlefield and can be beneficial for sensor employment and force application.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure that their decisions and actions comply with applicable United
States, international, and in some cases host-nation laws and regulations. Commanders at all levels ensure
that their service members operate in accordance with (IAW) the law of war and the rules of engagement.
(See FM 6-27 The Commander’s Handbook on the Law of Land Warfare).

TC 3-01.81 uses joint terms where applicable. Selected joint and Army terms and definitions appear in the
glossary and the text. For other definitions in the text, the term is italicized, and the number of the proponent
publication follows the definition.

This TC applies to all US armed services unless otherwise stated.

The proponent for this publication is the United States Army Fires Center of Excellence and Fort Sill. The
preparing agency is the Directorate of Training and Doctrine, Air Defense Artillery (ADA) Branch. Send
written comments and recommendations on DA Form 2028 (Recommended Changes to Publications and
Blank Forms) directly to Commandant, United States Army Air Defense Artillery School, ATTN: ATSF-
DD, Fort Sill, OK 73503 or submit an electronic DA Form 2028 by email using the following link:
usarmy.sill.fcoe.mbx.dotd-doctrine-inbox@army.mil.

                              Introduction

This proponent publication provides trainers and commanders with gunnery qualification requirements and
is a guide to reach published goals and sustain unit and crew proficiency. Training will replicate real world
situations and potential future operations, to motivate our service members to execute each task to standard
every time. This TC also provides a general overview of C-sUAS unit operations, to assist in the qualification
process. Units equipped with C-sUAS capabilities should adhere to the gunnery tables that apply to their
specific capabilities. If a unit only has LIDS (fixed-site [FS] or mobile [M]), they only need the gunnery
tables for the family system of LIDS (Chapter 2). If a unit only has non-program of record counter-unmanned
aircraft system (C-UAS) capabilities, they should reference Appendix D.

Chapters presented within TC 3-01.81 discuss gunnery table qualifications for the C-sUAS platforms. These
chapters are:

     • Chapter 1 covers certification requirements.
     • Chapter 2 covers lids certification and qualification.
     • Chapter 3 covers target engagement process.
     • Appendix A covers planning and establishing gunnery ranges.
     • Appendix B covers score cards.
     • Appendix C covers written test example.
     • Appendix D covers a foundational overview of knowledge needed to operate generalized C-UAS

          equipment.
     • Appendix E covers handheld certification and qualification.

                                                 Chapter 1

                       Certification Requirements

    Crew requirements provide guidance on how to conduct of C-sUAS certification gunnery
    tables. Training and evaluations must replicate real world situations and potential future
    operations. Evaluations must be scenarios that exercise the leader’s ability to lead and make
    decisions that enable service members to successfully perform at all levels.

OVERVIEW

     1-1. Training tables provide mandatory qualification standards and training strategies for C-sUAS. These
     training tables focus on preparing the individual to perform as part of a crew to accomplish the unit mission.
     Standards outlined in the combined arms training strategy (CATS) and service member’s training
     publications, are the minimum acceptable levels of performance.

     1-2. The gunnery strategy is based on the building block approach. Individuals are trained in basic skills
     before being integrated into crews. Crews train progressively from basic tasks, intermediate tasks, and
     advanced tasks through integration as battery or battalion elements performing their wartime mission.

     1-3. Commanders must actively exercise flexibility in applying these strategies, tailoring them to their unit's
     unique needs and objectives, and they have the discretion to integrate mission command in accordance with
     ADP 6-0. Commanders can adapt their tactics to respond effectively to emerging situations and make
     decisions that enable their units to achieve their missions.

     1-4. Flexibility in strategy and emphasis on gunnery proficiency empowers unit commanders to make
     informed decisions, capitalize on opportunities, and mitigate risks, ultimately contributing to the success of
     their missions.

THREAT

     1-5. Unmanned aircraft system (UAS) threats are categorized into groups 1-5 based on weight, size, operating
     altitude, and operating airspeed. Refer to ATP 3-01.81 to see breakdown of UAS groups. These threats
     include commercial off the shelf, foreign military, and non-state actors. A UAS is defined as the unmanned
     aircraft and ground station controller. Threat UAS are used to observe, track, target, and attack friendly
     forces.

     1-6. UAS that target friendly forces generally have low radar signatures, hover, or fly low enough that
     detection is difficult. Some are controlled by datalinks to a ground controller, while others fly preplanned
     routes using way points and return to the ground controller without the need for active data links for
     navigation. UAS that fly preplanned routes using global navigation satellite information are less susceptible
     to electromagnetic warfare capabilities.

RESPONSIBILITIES

     1-7. Leaders and trainers are responsible for conducting gunnery training and qualification according to
     applicable CATS, technical manuals (TM), field manuals (FM), standard operating procedures (SOP), higher
     headquarters directives, and guidance from the commander.

     1-8. Safety remains a priority during all training events. The crew and evaluators must comply with cautions,
     warnings, and danger notes. Battalion master gunners must ensure standing operating procedures adhere to
     range control safety policies, commander safety policies, and C-sUAS safety operations.

EVALUATORS

     1-9. The next higher-level command will select evaluators from adjacent units, if possible, to evaluate table
     qualification. Evaluators are selected from a list of personnel certified by their battalion trainer.

     1-10. The evaluator has the discretion to halt evaluations for system failures or safety violations. For system
     failures, corrective actions must be observed, such as fix or fight criteria or maintenance procedures prior to
     the evaluator halting an evaluation.

     1-11. Evaluators ensure that the execution of the drill complies with all applicable TCs, SOPs, and the C-

DECERTIFICATION

     1-12. Units will lose their certification when the following occurs:
          • Any unit or crew that exceeds recertification time limits.
          • Any one of the equipment crews violating Safety, Caution or Warning during certifications,
                supporting sustainment crew certifications, or standalone crew-certifications.
          • Failure to maintain a minimum of two certified C-sUAS crews. If either crew loses any member, a
                replacement from a sustainment crew can take that crew member’s place.

GUNNERY TRAINING

     1-13. Units equipped with C-sUAS platforms are responsible for developing a training program. They ensure
     training plans and standards adhere to the commander's intent and battalion training strategy. Commanders
     report training issues and personnel status to the battalion.

     1-14. Units request assistance from the battalion master trainers, through the S-3, to ensure they are compliant
     with the C-sUAS gunnery program. The unit commander provides a battle roster of certified crews with
     qualification dates to the battalion S-3.

     1-15. The gunnery training program will be developed to follow a logical progression. Units conduct training
     in two phases: individual and collective gunnery.

          • Individual gunnery trains service members on individual level skills, qualification, and certification
                tables I-III.

          • Collective: The collective gunnery phase develops table IV-VI.

ESSENTIAL WARFIGHTING SKILLS

     1-16. Crews achieve proficiency and critical skills through the following tasks:
          • Engaging targets from a stationary position.
          • Engaging targets in a simulated chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear environment.
          • Engaging targets at night.
          • Detecting, identifying, classifying, and discriminating targets as friendly, neutral or threat.
          • Acquiring and engaging targets in an urban, woodland, or desert environment.
          • Engaging targets using the appropriate technique for the target type.
          • Engaging multiple and successive targets.

     1-17. Crews and sections must achieve proficiency in the following skills:
          • Engaging targets as a crew or section.
          • Maintaining situational awareness and ensuring personnel protection.

UNIT TRAINING MANAGEMENT

     1-18. The 8-step training model provides a common framework for guiding commanders as they lead and
     manage unit training and leader development. Effective unit training results from a sound analysis of the
     unit's mission and its ability to accomplish that mission. The higher unit's mission, the mission-essential task
     list (METL), and higher commander's guidance drive the commander's selection of collective tasks on which
     the unit trains to accomplish mission success.

     1-19. Commanders and other leaders plan to develop their subordinate leaders-through training, education,
     and experience-in the three training domains (institutional, operational, and self-development). Leaders use
     the 8-step training model to integrate leader development into a unit-training plan.

     1-20. The commander is central to determining the few tasks on which C-sUAS tasks the unit must train.
     Commanders should follow the operations process with the assistance of unit leadership.

                                                                                                                Certification Requirements

     1-21. Commanders first plan for training through identifying the collective tasks on which to train. They must
     identify and sequence training events, identify resources required, and provide the guidance necessary to
     achieve mission readiness. Commanders will exercise mission command to enable their subordinates to
     determine how they will achieve their C-sUAS training objectives.

     1-22. Thorough preparation ensures that training conditions reflect the expected mission and that
     commanders have the resources and enablers necessary to train. Commanders execute the training and then
     assess the training. Assessments aid commanders in determining if units need to retrain tasks and if the
     training plan requires modification.

COMMANDER’S ASSESSMENT

     1-23. Gunnery programs must be well designed and continuous for units to achieve, improve, and sustain
     gunnery proficiency. Commanders assess and evaluate all aspects of the training plan, preparation, execution,
     and ultimate task proficiency. Only the unit commander can assess the readiness of a task.

     1-24. Assessment refers to the leader's judgment of the organization's ability to perform its tasks and
     accomplish its mission. Evaluation refers to the process used to measure the demonstrated ability of
     individuals and units to accomplish specified training objectives and achieve task proficiency. Leaders
     continuously monitor the unit's METL proficiency and the progress of the unit-training plan.

     1-25. The following factors must be considered during the assessment:
          • Their own observations and those of subordinate leaders and other individuals.
          • Feedback from after action reviews.
          • Evaluations of previous training. The commander assesses training with input from the BN
                executive officer, S-3, subordinate leaders, and unit trainers. They can use individual and collective
                training to determine the unit's proficiency.
          • Individual. Common task test results show basic individual training proficiency. The C-sUAS
                gunnery skills test evaluates specific platform related individual proficiency. Individual training and
                qualification scores indicate proficiency levels with the various C-sUAS platforms.
          • Collective. Commanders must select collective tasks that support the mission and adapt to
                unexpected missions.

     1-26. During mission analysis the commander should consider the following:
          • The unit's current readiness assessment of the collective tasks to train.
          • The higher commander's guidance.
          • The unit's METL.
          • Time available to train.
          • The expected operational environment.
          • Risks involved in not training collective tasks that the mission might require.
          • Any resources needed for training that are not readily available at home station.
          • Input from subordinates.

LIVE FIRE TRAINING

     1-27. Table V with prescribed equipment requires a passing score as a prerequisite to firing live ammunition.

     1-28. These tasks are recommended as minimum requirements driven by the CATS for each proponent. The
     purpose of these tasks is to ensure the crew possesses the skills and experience necessary to execute live fire
     gunnery.

                                              Chapter 2

                    LIDS Certification and Qualification

M-LIDS GUNNERY

2-1. Commander, unit trainer, and planners must have a comprehensive understanding of the topics in this
chapter, TC 3-20.0, Integrated Weapons Training Strategy (IWTS), and can forecast and plan months in
advance to achieve the desired level of proficiency.

2-2. The ammunition requirements (found later in this publication) may be different from the training
ammunition authorized within the approved Department of the Army Pamphlet (DA Pam) 350-38, Standards
in Weapons Training. Commanders, unit trainer, and planners must be able to understand the differences, if
any, and request resources accordingly.

2-3. The mobile-low, slow, small unmanned aircraft integrated defeat system (M-LIDS) gunnery program is
nested within the Integrated Weapons Training Strategy (known as IWTS). It represents training and
qualification at the crew level. It is important for units to understand the basic concepts and principles of the
IWTS.

2-4. Table 2-1 shows the crew tables and provides the general description and primary method of training.
The environment column indicates a generalized training environment for the event and includes the
associated training principle or method for the event.

2-5. Battalion and battery commanders are authorized to choose which engagements are day or night,
offensive or defensive, and which engagement will be under chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear
(CBRN) conditions throughout all tables. The battalion standardization officer must brief the commanders
on the capabilities of the live fire complex to used so that the commanders can make an informed decision
on the engagements.

2-6. The engagement scenarios outlined in Tables II through VI involve electromagnetic attacks and Coyote
interceptor engagements. However, if the range constraints do not permit the use of electromagnetic attack
weapons alongside lethal defeat weapons, commanders may opt to skip the electromagnetic attack
engagements for now. In such cases, they will adhere to the fixed site-low, slow, small unmanned aircraft
system integrated defeat system (FS-LIDS) guidelines to complete the electromagnetic attack gunnery tables
at a later time.

2-7. Live firing Coyote interceptors is not permitted during training exercises. Instead, all Coyote
engagements will be simulated using training aids, devices, simulators, and simulations (TADSS), provided
that the system is available.

TABLE I, GUNNERY SKILLS TEST

   2-11. The Crew Table I (table 2-2), Gunnery Skills Test (GST), is a pre-live-fire, hands-on performance, and

   verifies the service member's ability to perform critical tasks and employ the weapons and systems safely
   during training and tactical operations.

   2-12. The GST evaluates the individual crewmember's ability to execute selected fundamental gunnery-
   related skills (see table 2-4). Table I evaluates the entire crew's ability to execute selected tasks that are critical
   to the safe, and successful live fire training. Table I is a mandatory prerequisite for any live fire event,
   including zero procedures, live fire accuracy screening test, or any live fire maintenance procedure.

Evaluation Standards

     2-74. Commanders should integrate the tactical and gunnery tasks to exploit the full training effectiveness of
     these tables. Each crew of the platoon must earn at least 420 points on collective tasks. Each crew of the
     platoon must earn at least 280 points on gunnery tasks (aerial and ground targets combined).

          • Vehicle exposure time in the defense must fall within 50 seconds.
          • Each table uses the following 1,000-point system for gunnery.

                ▪ Gunnery counts 1,000 points. The senior evaluator combines and averages the scores for aerial
                     and ground targets. The senior evaluator uses the final numbers to determine a total score for
                     that crew.

                ▪ The senior evaluator combines and averages scores for both crews of the platoon to obtain a
                     single, overall platoon rating.

          • To qualify the crew’s score must equal at least 700 points. The crew ratings are as follows.
                ▪ Distinguished. Combined score of at least 900 points.
                ▪ Superior. Combined score of 800 to 899 points.
                ▪ Qualified. Combined score of 700 to 799 points.
                ▪ Unqualified. Combined score of 699 or less, or failure to earn at least 420 points in gunnery.

                           Fire Control Process

    Chapter 3 covers the procedures used from the time targets are acquired, through the placement
    of fires on those targets, to the reporting of the effects of those fires to the unit commander.
    Also included are considerations for fire distribution and control during offensive and defensive
    operations. Although the discussion focuses on the actions at the platoon and section level,
    these actions are always integrated into and become part of the unit plan.

EFFECTIVE FIRE CONTROL

     3-1. Effective fire control requires a unit to rapidly acquire the enemy and mass the effects of fires to achieve
     decisive results in the close fight. When planning and executing direct fires, the commander and subordinate
     leaders must know how to apply several fundamental principles. The purpose of these principles of direct
     fire is not to restrict the actions of subordinates. Applied correctly, these principles help the unit accomplish
     its primary goal in any direct fire engagement-to both acquire first and shoot first giving subordinates the
     freedom to act quickly upon acquisition of the enemy. This discussion focuses on the following principles:

          • Mass the effects of fire.
          • Destroy the greatest threat first.
          • Avoid over engagement of a target.
          • Employ the best weapon for the target.
          • Minimize friendly exposure.
          • Prevent fratricide.
          • Plan for limited visibility conditions.
          • Develop contingencies for diminished capabilities.

MASS THE EFFECTS OF FIRE

     3-2. Units must mass its fires to achieve decisive results. Massing entails focusing fires at critical points and
     distributing the effects. Random application of fires is unlikely to have a decisive effect. For example,
     concentrating the unit's fires at a single target may ensure its destruction or suppression; however, that fire
     control technique will probably not achieve a decisive effect on the enemy formation or position.

ASSESS AND DESTROY THE MOST DANGEROUS THREAT FIRST

     3-3. The order in which the unit engages enemy forces is in direct relation to the danger they present. The
     threat posed by the enemy depends on the weapons, range, and positioning. Presented with multiple targets,
     a unit will, in almost all situations, initially concentrate fires to destroy the greatest threat, and then distribute
     fires over the remainder of the enemy force.

AVOID OVER ENGAGEMENT OF A TARGET

     3-4. Use only the amount of fire required to achieve necessary effects. Over engagement of targets waste
     ammunition and saturates weapons that are better employed acquiring and engaging other targets. The idea
     of having all weapon systems engage a different target must be tempered by the requirement to destroy the
     greatest threats first.

EMPLOY THE BEST WEAPON FOR THE TARGET

     3-5. Using the appropriate weapon for the target increases the probability of rapid enemy destruction or
     suppression and saves ammunition. The unit has many weapons to engage the enemy with. Target type,
     range, exposure, weapons and ammunition availability, and desired target effects are key factors in
     determining the weapon or electromagnetic warfare effector that should be employed. Additionally, leaders
     should consider individual crew capabilities when deciding on the employment of weapons. The commander
     will task organize and array their forces based on the terrain, enemy, and desired effects of fires.

MINIMIZE FRIENDLY EXPOSURE

     3-6. Units increase their survivability by exposing themselves to the enemy only to the extent necessary to
     engage them effectively. Natural or man-made defilade provides the best cover from direct fire munitions.
     Crews and squads minimize their exposure by constantly seeking effective available cover, attempting to
     engage the enemy from the flank, remaining dispersed, firing from multiple positions, and limiting
     engagement times.

PREVENT FRATRICIDE

     3-7. All personnel must be proactive in reducing the risk of fratricide and noncombatant casualties. There are
     numerous tools to assist in this effort. They are identification training for combat vehicles and aircraft, the
     unit's weapons safety posture, the weapons control status, recognition markings, and a common operational
     picture. Knowledge and employment of applicable rules of engagement are the primary means of preventing
     noncombatant casualties.

DEVELOP CONTINGENCIES FOR DIMINISHED CAPABILITIES

     3-8. Leaders initially develop plans based on their unit's maximum capabilities; they make alternate plans for
     implementation in the event of casualties or weapon damage or failure. While leaders cannot anticipate or
     plan for every situation, they should develop plans for what they view as the most probable occurrences.
     Building redundancy into these plans, such as having two systems observe the same sector, is an invaluable
     asset when the situation (and the number of available systems) permits. Designating alternate sectors of fire
     provides a means of shifting fires if adjacent elements are knocked out of action. Examples of contingencies
     for diminished capabilities include-

          • Designating certain C-sUAS systems to engage certain targets due to ammunition shortages or
                weapon malfunctions.

          • Designating certain C-sUAS systems within the combined arms platoon to engage certain targets
                due to inherent weapon range or firepower limitations.

TARGET REFERENCE POINT

     3-9. A target reference point (TRP) is a recognizable point on the ground that leaders use to orient friendly
     forces and focus and control direct fires. In addition, when TRPs are designated as indirect fire targets, they
     can be used in calling for and adjusting indirect fires. Leaders designate TRPs at probable enemy locations
     and along likely avenues of approach. These can be natural or man-made points. A TRP can be an established
     site, such as a hill or a building, or an impromptu feature designated as a TRP on the spot, like a burning
     enemy vehicle or smoke generated by an artillery round. Friendly units can also construct markers to serve
     as TRPs. Ideally, TRPs should be visible in three observation modes (unaided, passive-IR, and thermal) so
     they can be seen by all forces. Example of TRPs include the following features and objects:

          • Prominent hill mass.
          • Distinctive building.
          • Observable enemy position.
          • Destroyed vehicle.
          • Ground-burst illumination.
          • Smoke round.

ENGAGEMENT AREA

     3-10. This fire control measure is an area along an enemy avenue of approach where the commander intends
     to mass the fires of available weapons to destroy an enemy force. The size and shape of the engagement area
     is determined by the degree of relatively unobstructed inter-visibility available to the unit's weapon systems
     in their firing positions and by the maximum range of those weapons. Typically, commanders delineate
     responsibility within the engagement area by assigning each platoon a sector of fire or direction of fire; these
     fire control measures are covered in the following paragraphs.

SECTOR OF FIRE

     3-11. A sector of fire is a defined area that must be covered by direct fire. Leaders assign sectors of fire to
     subordinate elements, crew-served weapons, and individual service members to ensure coverage of an area
     of responsibility. Leaders may also limit the sector of fire of an element or weapon to prevent accidental
     engagement of an adjacent unit. In assigning sectors of fire, commanders and subordinate leaders consider
     the number and types of weapons available. In addition, they must consider acquisition system type and field
     of view in determining the width of a sector of fire Conversely, most fire control acquisition systems have
     greater detection and identification ranges than the unaided eye, but their field of view is narrow. Means of
     designating sectors of fire include-

          • TRPs.
          • Clock direction.
          • Terrain-based quadrants.
          • Friendly-based quadrants.

DIRECTION OF FIRE

     3-12. A direction of fire is an orientation or point used to assign responsibility for a particular area on the
     battlefield that must be covered by direct fire. Leaders designate directions of fire for the purpose of
     acquisition or engagement by subordinate elements, crew-served weapons, or individual service members.
     Direction of fire is most employed when assigning sectors of fire would be difficult or impossible because of
     limited time or insufficient reference points. Means of designating a direction of fire include-

          • Closest target reference point.
          • Clock direction.
          • Cardinal direction.
          • Tracer on target.
          • Infrared laser pointer.

QUADRANTS

     3-13. Quadrants are subdivisions of an area created by superimposing an imaginary pair of perpendicular
     axes over the terrain to create four separate areas or sectors. Quadrants can be based on the terrain, friendly
     forces, or enemy formation.

     3-14. The method of quadrant numbering is established in the unit SOP; however, care must be taken to avoid
     confusion when quadrants based on terrain, friendly forces, and the enemy formations are used
     simultaneously.

TERRAIN-BASED QUADRANT

     3-15. A terrain-based quadrant entails use of a target reference point, either existing or constructed, to
     designate the center point of the axes that divide the area into four quadrants. This technique can be employed
     in both offensive and defensive operations. In the offense, the commander designates the center of the
     quadrant using an existing feature or by creating a reference point. The axes delineating the quadrants run
     parallel and perpendicular to the direction of movement. In the defense, the commander designates the center
     of the quadrant using an existing or constructed target reference point.

     3-16. Quadrants are marked using the letter "Q" and a number (Q1 to Q4); quadrant numbers are in the same
     relative positions as on military map sheets (from Q1, as the upper left-hand quadrant, clockwise to Q4, as
     the lower left-hand quadrant).

This chapter outlines procedures, duties, and responsibilities for planning and establishing
gunnery ranges and tactical courses, to include developing scenarios for gunnery ranges to
support C-sUAS. A combat training program is conducted on training areas to include dry and
live firing. It provides an opportunity to acquire targets in a realistic environment and use
weapon systems to engage targets. Tactical training should be integrated with gunnery training.

PLANNING GUNNERY EXERCISES

A-1. When planning a C-sUAS gunnery exercise the following procedures are followed:
     • Determine the commander's intent for the end state of the exercise.
     • Make tentative plans for crews and collective gunnery.
     • Evaluate range and ammunition resources to support the plans.
     • Forecast resources necessary to execute the training event(s).
     • Develop scenarios for crew and collective gunnery tables.
     • Proof all scenarios prior to execution.
     • Coordinate with supporting elements.
     • Plan range operations and personnel.
     • Execute the exercise.
     • Evaluate the exercise through after-action reviews.

COMMANDER’S INTENT

A-2. Planning a gunnery training exercise begins with the commander's intent for the desired end state of
the exercise. The commander will issue guidance to their staff on the training mission(s) that the unit is to
perform. The staff first plans for the collective gunnery phase to meet the commander's intent. The staff
tailors a gunnery training program that supports the collective training plan. The S-3 and unit trainer develop
the gunnery program using their inherent flexibility to tailor the tasks to the mission to meet the commander's
intent.

A-3.    To develop the gunnery program:
     •     The S-3 and unit trainer identify how crew tables and tasks are going to be designed to train to the

     •     The S-3 identifies the primary collective tasks to evaluate based on the core missions.
     •     The S-3 identifies which collective tasks will be firing tasks.
     •     The S-3 and unit trainer develop scenarios that require sections or platoons to conduct designated

     • Sand table layouts of the entire range area to aid rehearsals.
