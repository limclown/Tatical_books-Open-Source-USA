                                     Preface

TC 21-24 is the primary reference and instruction source providing techniques for all Soldiers and leaders when
conducting rappelling operations. Guidelines on how to conduct safe rappelling maneuvers are also contained in
this training circular. The safety notes and considerations presented provide only minimal acceptable standards.

The principal audience for TC 21-24 is all members involved with rappelling operations, whether resident or
nonresident Soldiers. Rappelling is inherently dangerous. Commanders at all levels analyze completed training
events to determine the degree of risk involved for Soldiers and equipment. After determining the dangers, risk
reduction options or controls should be integrated into the training activity. These can range from safety
briefings, providing additional safety resources, or selecting other means for accomplishing the mission.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure their decisions and actions comply with applicable United States,
international, and in some cases host-nation laws and regulations. Commanders at all levels ensure that their
Soldiers operate in accordance with the law of war and the rules of engagement. (See FM 6-27/MCTP 11-10C.)
In addition, the use of a trade or brand name does not constitute endorsement of the product.

TC 21-24 applies to the Active Army, Army National Guard/Army National Guard of the United States, and
United States Army Reserve unless otherwise stated.

                                            Chapter 1

                          Tower Rappelling

Introduction to rappelling is taught on a static tower (see figure 1-1). Using the building block
approach to training, Soldiers systematically progress to more demanding platforms including
taller static towers and helicopters. The static tower used may vary in size and height, but a 34-
foot minimum is required. The concept of learning basic rappelling techniques before
helicopter operations does not vary.

                                 Figure 1-1. Static rappel tower

Note. Static rappel towers should be inspected annually by post safety or post engineers. Anchor
points should be load tested every six months. Installations, major Army commands, and unit
commanders may establish additional policies and safety procedures, as needed, to ensure safe
and effective rappelling operations.

SECTION I – PERSONNEL

RAPPEL MASTER

     1-1. Rappel training is overseen by the rappel master (known as RM). The proponent for accreditation,
     evaluation, and information for Army RM courses is the U.S. Army Infantry School, 1 Karker Street, Fort
     Moore, Georgia 31905-5410.

     1-2. The RM is responsible for rappeller safety, the serviceability of all equipment (installation, unit, and
     personal property), and the personal supervision of rappelling operations.

     1-3. RM qualification is awarded only after successfully completing a U.S. Army Training and Doctrine
     Command accredited RM course that includes the following subjects:

              Duties and responsibilities of an RM.
              Safety standard operating procedures, regulations, and references.
              Utilization of a deployment bag.
              Conduct an equipment rappel off the rappel tower.
              Conduct a lock-in.
              Talking a rappeller through completion of a rappel.
              Conduct ground training.
              Inspecting for proper hook-ups.
              Inspecting and maintaining equipment (gloves, carabiners, ropes).
              Identifying the rappel capabilities of aircraft used.
              Controlling rappels from UH-60 or CH-47 aircraft.
              Tying knots such as the figure-eight on a bight, end of line bowline, Prusik, clove hitch, Munter

                  hitch, water knot; safety lines; and the hip-rappel seat.
              Inspecting a hip-rappel seat.
              Aircraft rigging for rappelling operations.
              Aircraft command and control.

     1-4. To remain current, RMs must execute their duties in a tactical or training exercise once every six months.
     If RMs do not execute their duties once every six months, they must take a refresher class taught by a current
     RM. The refresher class includes subjects listed in the RM qualifications listed above.

     1-5. Units may conduct ground training and wall-side tower rappelling without a school-trained RM if a fully
     qualified rappel safety officer (known as RAPSO) is on site to assume the duties and responsibilities of the
     RM. The RAPSO must be certified and appointed by the commander.

RAPPEL SAFETY OFFICER

     1-6. The RAPSO serves as the officer in charge during all rappel operations. Holding the rank of staff
     sergeant or above, the RAPSO is trained on applicable RM tasks and is certified to serve in the position.
     Extensive training in rappel operations and risk management, or graduation from one of the following courses
     are RAPSO requirements: Rappel Master Course; Air-Assault Course, Ranger Course, or Basic Military
     Mountaineering Course (summer or winter). During open-side rappels, the RM can assume the duties of the
     RAPSO with the commander's permission.

     1-7. The RAPSO is responsible for the overall safety of all rappellers and ensures that all safety precautions
     are followed. The RAPSO briefs visitors and inspecting authorities on training, safety requirements, and
     layout of training areas.

RAPPEL LANE NONCOMMISSIONED OFFICER

1-8. Safety is the rappel lane noncommissioned officers (NCOs) number one priority. The rappel lane NCO—
     • Ensures proper safety procedures are followed.
     • Ensures proper hookup once directed to a rappel lane.
     • Issues commands and maintains eye contact with the rappeller at all times.

1-9. The rappel lane NCO is a commander-appointed corporal or above who is trained in the safe and proper
execution of rappelling operations. Each tower rappel lane has a qualified rappel lane NCO to supervise lane
operations. With the commander's approval, an experienced certified RM lane NCO can supervise two lanes
simultaneously.

1-10.   The rappel lane NCO is also trained on the following  subjects:
     •     Responsibilities and safety requirements.
     •     Inspection and maintenance of equipment.
     •     Identification of satisfactory anchor points.
     •     Identification of safe and unsafe hookups.
     •     Establishment of a rappel point.
     •     Inspection of a hip-rappel seat.
     •     Rappelling procedures.
     •     Rescue rappelling.
     •     Belay control procedures.

1-11. If a rappel lane NCO has not conducted duties within the last six months, they are required to complete
the training listed in the above paragraph under the supervision of a current RM.

RAPPELLER

1-12. Participants in tower rappel training complete the following requirements under the supervision of an
RM. The unit commander ensures that personnel successfully complete these requirements before beginning
aircraft rappelling. Individual rappellers—

     • Identify all rappelling equipment.
     • Demonstrate construction of the hip-rappel seat and/or donning a manufactured harness, proper use

          and hook-up of a rappel device and rappel rope.
     • Identify unsafe attachments, equipment, rope connections, and seat construction.
     • Define terms used in rappelling operations.
     • Identify knots used in rappel operations.
     • Understand and demonstrate rappel commands.
     • Demonstrate rappelling positions.
     • Demonstrate belaying procedures.
     • Conduct one rappel from the wall side of the tower, then two from the open side. When the rappeller

          is conducting an aircraft rappel with equipment, then the two open-side rappels are with the
          equipment that is being used during that rappel.
     • Demonstrate the ability to lock-in.

BELAYER AND BELAY SAFETY

1-13. Belay requirements are a subtask of basic rappel training. Soldiers learn to belay before learning rappel
training. The belayer—

     • Assumes a position at the base of the lane about one pace away from the tower area.
     • Removes gloves and rucksack, if worn.
     • Loosely holds the rappel rope with both hands to avoid interference with the rappeller while still

          maintaining control to stop the rappeller should they fall.
     • Immediately stops the rappeller by pulling downward on the rappel ropes if the rappeller shouts,

          FALLING or loses control of their brake hand during descent.
     • Watches the rappeller at all times and maintains constant voice or visual contact.
     • Wears a helmet to prevent injuries from falling debris.

SECTION II – PREOPERATION BRIEFINGS AND SAFETY PROCEDURES

     1-15. The RM ensures Soldiers have a basic understanding of requirements and safety procedures before
     conducting training.

     1-16. The following personnel and equipment are present during static tower training:
          • One RM for each tower.
          • One RAPSO.
          • One rappel lane NCO per rappel station.
          • One medic equipped with cervical collar, pelvic binder, and backboard.
          • One belayer for each rappel lane.
          • One safety or medical evacuation vehicle with dedicated driver.
          • One belay safety for each two rappel stations (four ropes).
          • Two military rappel ropes for each rappel station.

SAFETY BRIEFING

     1-17. As in all training, a safety briefing precedes rappel operations. The RM briefs all personnel on safety
     and include the following instructions:

          • Each rappeller ensures loose clothing and equipment is secure.
          • Hip-rappel seats are tied (or harnesses donned) by the Soldier and inspected by the RM before

                climbing the tower. Hip-rappel seats are removed upon completion of every rappel. They are then
                retied and reinspected by a qualified RM or rappel lane NCO before subsequent rappels.
          • Rappellers climb the tower only when directed by the RM or rappel lane NCO.
          • Rappellers stay in the center of the tower until instructed to move to a rappel point.
          • All personnel working within 3 feet of the edge on the top of the tower are attached to an anchor.
                The rappeller is properly hooked up to the rappelling ropes.
          • When attaching the rappel rope to the carabiner, rappellers pull the slack toward the anchor point.
                The RM or rappel lane NCO physically check each hookup.
          • In the case of an extremely heavy rappeller, a friction hookup is ordered at the discretion of the RM.
                A friction hookup is created by placing an additional two ropes in the gate of the carabiner (for a
                total of six ropes in the carabiner).
          • Combat equipment is positioned on the rappeller so it does not interfere with the brake hand. The
                weapon is slung diagonally across the back with the muzzle pointing down and on the opposite side
                of the brake hand.
          • Heavy-duty gloves with friction pads are required for all rappel training. Less preferred but also
                authorized is using standard-issue leather work gloves with issued liners.
          • Ballistic or safety helmets with chin straps fastened are worn during tower rappel training.
          • While on the tower, the rappeller maintains eye contact with the RM or rappel lane NCO and
                receives all commands from them.
          • The rappeller ensures that they have a belayer on their rope.
          • The belayer always keeps both hands on the rope and faces the rappeller at all times.
          • All tower rappelling is performed with a double strand of rope when rappelling with the
          • carabiner system.
          • No running is allowed on the tower.
          • All participants who are unable to rappel, lack confidence, or refuse to rappel are reported to the
                RM or officer in charge.

TOWER SAFETY AND PREPARATION

     1-18. The RM oversees the tower. Duties include conducting a visual and physical inspection of every item
     of equipment. Other safety and preparations include:

          • A tower is not used during thunderstorms. If ice or other inclement weather is present, rappelling is
                conducted with caution and reflected in the commander's risk mitigation.

          • All rappel lanes are rigged with a primary and secondary anchor point (see figure 1-2). Only
                authorized knots are used. The RM removes all slack between the knots to create equal tension on
                the anchor points and ensures rappel ropes reach the bottom with an additional 10 feet of additional
                rope on the ground.

          • In the case that a tower has a unique anchor point configuration, a rappel lane must have its own
                single dedicated primary anchor point. If necessary, a maximum of two rappel lanes may share a
                secondary anchor point.

          • When rigging a lane with a hitching post, although not mandatory, one turn of the rappel ropes may
                be used to prevent lateral movement along the horizontal beam.

Note. The clove hitch-figure-eight on a bight anchor system is shown, but other authorized anchor
knots may be preferred when the tower has nonconventional anchor points.

RAPPELLER PREPARATION

     1-19. Before conducting a rappel, every rappeller prepares their individual clothing and equipment. Strict
     attention is paid to the following:

          • Secure shirt tails, loose clothing, equipment, straps, and long hair.
          • Wear a helmet during rappelling. Properly fasten all straps and buckles.
          • Wear heavy duty leather gloves with friction pads.
          • Wear identification tags.
          • Fasten equipment in the front or in a manner that allows visual inspection and does not impede the

                use of rappelling equipment when rappelling with authorized load-bearing equipment.
          • Wear the rucksack high and tight on the back of the rappeller to allow the brake hand to reach the

                small of the back. Rucksack adjustment straps are fastened across the chest or tucked away.
          • Sling weapons diagonally across the back with the muzzle down. Ensure the muzzle is on the guide-

                hand side and the stock is toward the brake hand.

SECTION III – RAPPELLING PROCEDURES

     1-20. This section discusses procedures used in tower rappelling.

HIP-RAPPEL SEAT

     1-21. There are two types of rappel seats: the manufactured harness and the hip-rappel seat. The
     manufactured harness is a preconstructed webbing loop system that connects the rappeller's body to the
     carabiner. It is industry produced rather than being tied by the rappeller. The hip-rappel seat is a field
     expedient harness that is tied by the rappeller using rope. When using a rappel seat, friction is created between
     the connecting carabiner and the rappelling rope. This method provides an efficient and controlled descent.
     Wear heavy-duty leather gloves with friction pads to prevent rope burns.

HIP-RAPPEL-SEAT HOOKUP

     1-22. To hookup using the hip-seat method, perform the following:
          • Place the square knot with two overhand knots toward the anchor point for all hip-rappel seats.
                When using a manufactured harness, the rappeller's guide hand side is toward the anchor point.
          • Grasp the two ropes with both hands and drop them through the gate of the carabiner. (At this point,
                two ropes should be running through the carabiner.)
          • Using the hand closest to the anchor point, pull the slack toward the anchor point. Rotate the slack
                under and then over the top of the carabiner.
          • Drop the two ropes a second time through the gate of the carabiner. (At this point, four ropes should
                be running through the carabiner.)
          • Lock the carabiner.
          • Place the guide hand on the rope between the anchor point and the carabiner (palm facing up).
          • Place the brake hand around the running end of the rope (palm facing down). Place the brake hand
                with the rope in the small of the back.

TOWER PROCEDURES

     1-23. After the rappeller climbs the tower, the following procedures are adhered to:
          • The RM waives the rappeller forward to their rappel lane and ensures a proper hookup.
          • The RM tells the rappeller their lane number and gives the command GET READY, accompanied
                by the hand and arm signal.
          • The rappeller looks over the edge of the tower, makes eye contact with the belayer, and states their
                lane number followed by ON RAPPEL. The belayer acknowledges by replying with the lane number
                followed by ON BELAY.

     bag technique and the log coil technique. This deployment bag technique applies to the original deployment

DEPLOYMENT BAG TECHNIQUE

     2-17. The rappeller places the deployment bag (known as a D-bag) (standard) on a flat surface with the stow
     loop facing upward. If the D-bag still has a static line, the rappeller removes it by cutting the static line where
     it attaches to the bag. A 12-foot section of the static line can be used as a safety line in the helicopter.

          • Use a sandbag with about one E-tool spade full of sand as a weight in the deployment system. Roll
                the sandbag into a rectangular shape, tie it, then place it in a small plastic bag, rolled and taped.
                Place a retaining band over the middle of the weight and place the weight on top of the D-bag. The
                weight should be about the same width as the D-bag.

                retaining band over the stack. Ensure that it is over all the zigzags in the stack. Repeat the process

    This chapter discusses the basic equipment used for rappelling operations (see appendix A).

SECTION I – ROPES AND CARABINERS

     3-1. Ropes are the rappellers’ most important items of equipment. They provide access down the obstacle or
     aerial platform while ensuring individual safety.

TYPES OF ROPES

     3-2. The following describes kernmantle and laid ropes:
          • Kernmantle rope. Static kernmantle rope is the preferred rope for military rappelling due to its
                strongly rated capacities and resistance to wear with minimal stretch. Construction includes a core
                of nylon fibers protected by a woven sheath (see figure 3-1). The internal core (kern) of kernmantle
                rope is made of a continuous multifilament-nylon yarn that is spun into continuous parallel strands.
                Over 70 percent of the rope's overall strength comes from the kern. The kern is then covered with a
                nylon-braided outer sheath (mantle). The minimum required tensile strength for rope used in
                military rappelling is 4,500 pounds.
          • There are two different types of kernmantle rope available to the rappeller-static and dynamic:
                 Static kernmantle rope allows for minimal stretch and is preferred for rappelling operations. It
                     stretches about 5 to 15 percent at the point of failure, and about 2 percent under a working load.
                     Eleven-millimeter rope is the preferred size for military rappelling.
                 Dynamic kernmantle rope allows for stretch within the fibers of the rope. This can be a
                     disadvantage in rappelling, Prusik climbing, and other applications. It has about a 5 percent to
                     10 percent working stretch.

     3-7. All rappelling material and individual rappelling equipment is inspected before conducting any

ROPES

     3-8. Ropes should be inspected before and after use. If any of the following deficiencies are identified, the

          • Any cuts, frays, or burnt sections larger than one third the diameter of the rope.

          • Lateral movement when the carabiner gate is open.

          • Any cracks, distortion, corrosion, rust, and sharp edges on the carabiner.

SECTION I – PREPARATION, MAINTENANCE, INSPECTION, AND
    TERMINOLOGY

     4-1. The service life of a rope depends on the frequency of use in applications such as rappelling, climbing,
     or during rope installation training; speed of descent; surface abrasion; terrain and climate conditions; and
     quality of maintenance. Regular rope inspection and maintenance ensures rappelling safety.

             Note. Any rope can fail under extreme conditions such as shock load, sharp edges, or misuse.

PREPERATION

     4-2. The RM selects the proper rope for the intended task according to type, diameter, and tensile strength.
     All ropes are inspected and prepared before rappelling operations.

PACKAGING

     4-3. New rope comes from the manufacturer in different configurations; boxed on a spool in various lengths
     or coiled and bound. Precut ropes are usually packaged in protective covers made of plastic or burlap. Do not
     remove the protective cover until the rope is ready for use.

SECURING THE ENDS OF THE ROPE

     4-4. If still on a spool, the rope is cut to the desired length. Laid rope frays at the ends unless it is whipped
     and dipped or taped and burned. Static and dynamic kernmantle rope ends are secured in the same manner.
     The ends are heated to the melting point using a proper rope cutter, so that the inner core strands are melted
     to the outer sheath. By fusing the two together, the sheath cannot slide backward or forward. Make sure this
     is done only to the ends of the rope. If the body of the rope is exposed to extreme temperatures, the sheath
     and inner core could be weakened, reducing overall tensile strength.

MAINTENANCE AND INSPECTION

     4-5. Rope is a rappeller's lifeline and should be cared for and used properly. Use the following general
     guidelines when handling ropes:

          • Do not unnecessarily step on or drag ropes on the ground.
          • While in use, do not allow the rope to come into contact with sharp edges. If it must be used over a

                sharp edge, pad the edge for protection.
          • Always keep the rope as dry as possible. If it becomes wet, hang it in large loops off the ground and

                allow it to dry. Never dry a rope with high heat or in direct sunlight.
          • Never leave a rope knotted or tightly stretched any longer than necessary. Over time, this reduces

                the strength and life of the rope.
          • Never allow one rope to continuously rub over or against another. When using nylon rope, allowing

                rope-on-rope contact is extremely dangerous. Heat produced by the friction can cause the nylon to
                melt.
          • Inspect ropes before each use for frayed or cut spots, mildew, or rot. New rope is also inspected
                before first use.
          • The ends of the rope should be whipped or melted to prevent unraveling.
          • Do not splice ropes for use in rappelling.

TERMINOLOGY

     4-7. It is important to understand basic terminology when using ropes. The following terms are commonly

          • A bight of rope is a simple bend of rope where the rope does not cross over itself.

          • A running end is the loose or working end of the rope.
          • The standing part is the static, stationary, or nonworking end of the rope.
          • The lay is the direction of twist as in construction of the rope.
          • The pigtail (tail) is the remaining portion of the rope's running end after tying a knot.

SECTION II – COILING, CARRYING, THROWING

     4-8. The ease and speed of rope deployment and recovery greatly depends upon technique and practice. The
     following procedures are recommended for optimal performance.

COILING AND CARRYING THE ROPE

     4-9. Use the mountain coil or butterfly to prepare and carry the rope. Each coil technique is easy to
     accomplish and has a minimal number of kinks, twists, and knots during deployment.

MOUNTAIN COIL

     4-10. To start a mountain coil (see figure 4-2), grasp the rope approximately 3 feet from the end with one
     hand. Run the other hand along the rope until both arms are outstretched. Grasping the rope firmly, bring the
     hands together forming a loop, then lay the loop in the hand closest to the end of the rope. This is repeated
     forming uniform loops that run in a clockwise direction until the rope is completely coiled. The rope may be
     given a quarter twist as each loop is formed to overcome any tendency for it to twist or form figure eights.

     4-11. When finishing the mountain coil, form a bight approximately 1 foot long with the starting end of the
     rope and lay it along the top of the coil. Uncoil the last loop. Using this length of rope, begin making wraps
     around the coil and the bight, wrapping toward the closed end of the bight. Make the first wrap bind across
     itself to lock it into place, followed by six to eight wraps. Route the end of the rope through the closed end
     of the bight, pulling the running end of the bight tight, securing the coil.

     4-12. The mountain coil can then be carried in the pack by forming a figure eight, doubling it, and placing it
     under the flap; or by placing it over the shoulder and under the opposite arm, slung across the chest. Figure
     4-2 shows the steps involved in routing and wrapping a mountain coil.

THROWING THE ROPE

     4-16. Before throwing the rope, it is properly managed to prevent it from tangling during deployment. The
     rope should first be anchored to prevent its loss over the edge when thrown. Basic techniques for throwing
     the rope are as follows:

          • Back-stack the rope beginning with the anchored end of the rope working toward the running end.
          • Once stacked, make six to eight smaller coils in the left hand. Pick up the rest of the larger coils in

                the right hand. The arm should be generally straight when throwing.
          • Prior to throwing the rope, the thrower should sound off with the warning ROPE, ROPE, ROPE to

                alert anyone below the site.
          • Throw up and out. A slight twist of the wrist so the palm of the hand faces up as the rope is thrown

                allows the coils to separate easily without tangling.
          • When a slight tug on the left hand is felt, toss the six to eight smaller coils out. This prevents the

                ends of the rope from becoming entangled with the rest of the coils as they deploy.

     4-17. All knots used by a rappeller are divided into four classes. The knot classes are provided as a general
     guide. Some knots may fall under more than one class. The classes are:

          • Class I: Joining knots.
          • Class II: Anchor knots.
          • Class III: Middle rope knots.

JOINING KNOTS

     4-18. Joining knots connect two ropes together, not necessarily of the same size. They typically consist of
     loops and are sometimes referred to as bends.

SQUARE KNOT

     4-19. The square knot is used to tie the ends of two ropes of equal diameter under tension (see figure 4-5). It
     is a joining knot. To tie a square knot:

          • Step 1. Holding one working end in each hand, place the working end in the right hand over the one
                in the left hand.

          • Step 2. Pull the working end under and back over the top of the rope in the left hand.
          • Step 3. Place the working end in the left hand over the one in the right hand and repeat step 2.
          • Step 4. Dress the knot down and secure it with an overhand safety knot on each side of
          • the square knot.

          • Both ends should exit opposite each other without any bends.

BOWLINE ON A BIGHT

     4-40. The bowline on a bight is used to form two fixed loops in the middle of a rope (see figure 4-15). To tie
     a bowline-on-a-bight knot:

          • Step 1. Form a bight in the rope about twice as long as the finished loops will be.
          • Step 2. Tie an overhand knot on a bight.
          • Step 3. Hold the overhand knot in the left hand so the bight is running down and outward.
          • Step 4. Grasp the bight with the right hand and fold it back over the overhand knot so the overhand

                knot goes through the bight.
          • Step 5. From the end (apex) of the bight, follow the bight back to where it forms the cross in the

                overhand knot. Grasp the two ropes that run down and outward and pull up, forming two loops.
          • Step 6. Pull the two ropes out of the overhand knot and dress the knot down.
          • Step 7. A final dress is required. Grasp the ends of the two fixed loops and pull, spreading them

                apart to ensure the loops do not slip.

TWO-LOOP FIGURE EIGHT

     4-42. The two-loop figure eight is used to form two fixed loops in the middle of a rope (see figure 4-16). To
     tie a two-loop figure eight knot:

          • Step 1. Using a doubled rope, form an 18-inch bight in the left hand with the running end facing to
                the left.

          • Step 2. Grasp the bight with the right hand and make a 360-degree turn around the standing end in
                a counterclockwise direction.

          • Step 3. With the working end, form another bight and place that bight through the loop just formed
                in the left hand.

          • Step 4. Hold the bight with the left hand and place the original bight (moving toward the left hand)
                over the knot.

          • Step 5. Dress the knot down.

THREE-LOOP BOWLINE

     4-49. The three-loop bowline is a bowline tied using two strands of rope. A third loop is formed when tying
     the knot when one rope is folded, forming a bight (see figure 4-20). To tie a three-loop bowline knot:

          • Step 1. Form an approximate 24-inch bight or grasp two strands of rope.
          • Step 2. With the right thumb facing toward the body, form a doubled loop in the standing part by

                turning the wrist clockwise. Lay the loops to the right.
          • Step 3. With the right hand, reach down through the loops and pull up a doubled bight from the

                standing part of the rope.
          • Step 4. Place the running end (bight) of the rope (on the left) through the doubled bight from left to

                right and bring it back on itself. Hold the running end loosely and dress the knot down by pulling
                on the standing parts.
          • Step 5. Safety it off with an overhand safety knot.

TRANSPORT KNOT (OVERHAND SLIP KNOT/MULE KNOT)

     4-51. The transport knot is used to secure the transport tightening system (see figure 4-21). To tie a transport

          • Step 3. Secure the knot by tying a half hitch around the standing portion with the bight.

KLEMHEIST KNOT

     4-52. The Klemheist knot provides a moveable, easily adjustable, high-tension knot capable of holding
     extremely heavy loads while being pulled tight (see figure 4-22). To tie a Klemheist knot:

          • Step 1. Using a utility rope or webbing, offset the ends by 12 inches. With the ends offset, find the
                center of the rope, and form a bight. Lay the bight over a horizontal rope.

          • Step 2. Wrap the tails of the utility rope around the horizontal rope back toward the direction of pull.
                Wrap at least four complete turns.

          • Step 3. With the remaining tails of the utility rope, pass them through the bight (see step 1).
          • Step 4. Join the two ends of the tail with a joining knot.
          • Step 5. Dress the knot down tightly so all wraps are touching.

             Note. Ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene spectra fiber is an example of a type of rope which
             should not be used for the Klemheist knot. It has a low melting point and tends to slip. Other types
             of rope with similar properties can also lead to failure in a system.

                64).

                                               Appendix A

                  Basic Equipment for Rappel Operations

The equipment listed in this appendix comprise the standard basic items required to conduct
ground and air rappelling operations.

TOWER RAPPELLING

     A-1. For tower rappelling, the following equipment is needed:
          • Static kernmantle ropes.
          • Locking carabiners.
          • Heavy duty leather gloves with friction pads.
          • Rope coiling log or standard deployment bag.
          • Hip-rappel seat ropes or manufactured harnesses.

AIRCRAFT RAPPELLING

A-2. For aircraft rappelling, the following equipment is needed:

• All items in paragraph A-1.

• Radio for communication with aircraft.

• High performance, waterproof, pressure sensitive tape.

•  Army approved personal gear:

    Ear protection.

    Eye protection.

    Helmet.

    Long-sleeved shirt.
