                                     PREFACE

This circular provides training information and guidance to ammunition sustainment forces for the
purposes of training Ammunition Support Activities (ASA) in Large-Scale Combat Operations (LSCO).
TC 9-10 provides practical procedures and examples useful to mission planning and unit training
programs of ammunition support activities, intended to be used as a guide and are not prescriptive. This
training circular provides nonprescriptive ways and information for individual and collective training. It is
also intended to be used in conjunction with current supporting regulations and relevant doctrine.

The principal audience for TC 9-10 is commanders, staff officers, and supporting personnel at the
ordnance company and battalion, and brigade sustainment levels, and all observers/controllers responsible
for evaluation and training assistance to those units. The arrangement of the circular is designed for
commanders and subordinate leaders of ammunition ordnance units and units with an Ammunition
Support Activity to use in the development of ordnance training programs to attain and sustain combat
readiness and meet requirements of Objective-T.

TC 9-10 is also useful to special operations forces (SOF) commanders and staffs in better understanding
the parameters of conventional forces (CF) sustainment level engaged in ammunition supply and
resupply. This publication applies to both Active and Reserve Component soldiers.

The proponent of TC 9-10 is the Headquarters, United States Army Training and Doctrine Command
(TRADOC). The preparing agency is the United States Army Ordnance School, send comments and
recommendations on DA Form 2028 (Recommended Changes to Publications and Blank Forms) to
Commandant, United States Army Ordnance School, ATTN: ATSL-TDF, 2221 Adams Ave, Fort Gregg-
Adams, VA 23801-2102.

                                   Introduction

         “The crucible for the Ordnance Department, like the individual fighting man, is the
         battlefield.”

                                                               Brig. Gen. Hal C. Pattison, U.S. Army, 17 June 1966.

This chapter introduces ammunition support activity (ASA) operations and key personnel within the ASA
structure. The availability of munitions directly impacts mission accomplishment. Ammunition battalions,
modular ammunition companies, ammunition platoons, and ammunition sections in the theater of
operations must be able to support the operational plans of tactical commanders.

AMMUNITION SUPPORT ACTIVITY OPERATIONS

         1-1. War is a major conflict between nations that may or may not be declared. Protracted wars
         can last longer than a year. Contingencies are crises, often with complex political implications.
         These crises may happen anywhere in the world where US interests are threatened. Present and
         future battlefields require that ammunition units be mobile and agile. Future battles may be linear
         or nonlinear, with rapid movement and changes. Thus, ammunition support units must train for
         operations in many different scenarios and configurations.

         1-2. The unstable, uncertain nature of Multi-Domain Operations (MDO) means a change in
         how ammunition units are situated. In peacetime, ammunition units operate out of fixed sites,
         with all associated support and facilities in place. In contingency operations, the unit enters a
         country, not always friendly, to support a rapidly deploying force. It is imperative that
         ammunition units train for not just one scenario in Large-Scale Combat Operations (LSCO), but
         the multitude of operations that it may be assigned. From operating an air or seaport of
         debarkation for the receipt of Class V stocks to operating an ASA. Ammunition units could be
         supporting corps task forces, operating as a unit in an ordnance-specific formation, or as a
         fragmented unit of modular platoons providing support to special operations forces in a
         counterinsurgency operation.

         1-3. Training in peacetime is vital to the success of ammunition sustainment units in wartime.
         Ammunition unit leaders are responsible for training their personnel to meet the Commander’s
         Training Guidance and standards set in soldier training publications (STP). Training should be
         based on the current threat and should realistically simulate conditions created by enemy actions
         that will affect ammunition support activity operations. Simulations should include such things as
         chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear (CBRN) threats, contested logistics, site security,
         and disconnected operations. Additional information can be found in ATP 4-35 and ATP 4-35.1.

         1-4. The Army Service Component Command (ASCC) commander establishes priorities for
         theater Class V supplies, giving priority to the highest usage and most critical ammunition. The
         Theater Sustainment Command (TSC) Material Management Branch (MMB) coordinates the
         shipment and delivery of stocks from strategic support area to support the campaign plan.

         1-7. An ammunition supply point is an ammunition support activity operated by one or more
         modular ammunition platoons and is a retail ammunition support activity where ammunition is
         received, stored, issued, and accounted for. The ammunition transfer holding point is a designated
         site operated by a brigade support battalion distribution company where ammunition is received,
         transferred or temporarily stored to supported units within a brigade combat team. The modular
         ammunition transfer point provides the BCT with the ability to scale, tailor, and surge class V
         capability and capacity required to sustain combat power during large-scale combat operations
         across a widely dispersed area of operations. For more information, see ATP 4-35.

Ammunition Support Activities, Types of Storage.

         1-8. Strategic storage is the storage of ammunition throughout the Department of Defense
         (DoD) ammunition enterprise occurs at a number of depots, plants, arsenals, ammunition centers,
         and commercial storage facilities in the continental United States (CONUS). Operational storage
         is the linkage between strategic storage to tactical storage of ammunition, support tactical storage
         facilities, and store reserve stocks. Tactical storage of ammunition directly supports the combat
         division or portions of the division. For more information on types of storage, refer to DA PAM
         700-16.

         1-9. Ammunition support activities have echeloned sustainment. Echeloned sustainment is an
         array of capabilities placed at critical locations to link and facilitate support between echelons in
         an area of operation (ATP 4-90). The type will be dependent on the support relationship and
         mission, and where on the battlefield the ammunition support activities are located. At the lowest
         level, echeloned sustainment is executed at the company and battalion level. Training for the vast
         array Class V missions is essential and the expected locations that are designated to receive, store,
         maintain, and provide munitions support to Army forces across the area of operations. The array
         for ammunition units on the battlefield are illustrated in figure 1-1. The following activity types
         include—

             • Theater Ammunition Supply Point,
             • Field Army or Corps Ammunition Supply Point,
             • Division Ammunition Supply Point,
             • Brigade Ammunition Transfer and Holding Point, and

External Stakeholders

         1-25. Commanders. Each commanding officer is responsible for supplying their units within
         their command to be successful in combat. This responsibility cannot be transferred, but the
         duties imposed by this responsibility may be delegated to commanders of lower echelons. Within
         each command, the commander will delegate many of these duties to their coordinating staff. In
         units at the division and below, these duties may be delegated the support operations officer
         which is responsible to the commander for the proper execution of these duties and for Class V,
         the ammunition warrant officer.

         1-26. Staff Officers. Staffs support commanders in making and implementing decisions and in
         integrating and synchronizing combat power. Competent staffs multiply a unit’s effectiveness.
         They provide timely and relevant information and analysis, make estimates and
         recommendations, prepare plans and orders, assist in controlling operations, and assess the
         progress of operations for the commander. A staff section is a grouping of staff members by area
         of expertise. There are three basic types of staff officers: coordinating, special, and personal. The
         most typical related to munitions is the operations staff officer (S-3) and the logistics staff officer
         (S-4). For more information, see FM 5-0 and FM 6-0.

         1-27. Support Operations Officer. A support operations officer (SPO) is only authorized in
         sustainment headquarters (theater sustainment command, expeditionary sustainment command,
         sustainment brigade, division sustainment brigade, combat sustainment support battalion, division
         sustainment support battalion, and brigade support battalion). As the principal staff officer for
         coordinating sustainment support, the SPO develops the plan the sustainment command will
         execute to support the sustainment concept. The SPO is the key interface between the supported
         unit and the sustainment headquarters. The support operations staff does not develop the
         supported unit sustainment plan but provides the information to enable sustainment planning.

         1-28. Brigade Ammunition Officer. The brigade ammunition officer (BAO) is assigned to the

         duties of AO when designated.

         battalions, and companies that conduct multidomain operations and require munitions for training
         and operations. Ammunition support activities provide all munitions necessary for mission
         accomplish

                                  CHAPTER 2

                 Ammunition Support Activity Operations

This chapter discusses munitions requisitions overview, stock control, ammunition supply operations, and
combat-specific operations related to training information and used for integrated training, non-
perspective in nature. Ammunition operations include receipts, storage, inventory, surveillance, issue,
turn-in, and shipments.

MUNITIONS REQUISITION OVERVIEW

         2-1. Munitions requisitions and stockage management follows a specific pathway through the
         Army munitions requirements, prioritization, and authorizations management system before
         munitions arrive to the ASA. The following is a general overview. For more information on
         munitions requisitions, see AR 5-13.

         2-2. Phase I: Requirement Approval Process. The Army produces the Total Munitions
         Requirements annually. To Identify the types, listed by Department of Defense identification
         codes (DODIC) and quantities of munitions required to meet war reserve, operational, training
         and test requirements; and is used to inform readiness assessments and to identify long-term
         investment requirements for munitions.

         2-3. Phase II: Procurement and Production (Staging). The ammunition procurement and
         production phase follow development of requirements and apportionment of appropriated
         program funds (through Office of the Secretary of Defense (OSD) and Headquarters, Department
         of the Army (HQDA)) to the operating agency (Joint Program Executive Office (JPEO)
         Armament and Ammunition) with their assigned project management offices.

         2-4. Phase III: Allocation Process. The authorization process is the by-DoDIC quantity of
         munitions that a unit or organization may receive in support of its approved requirements. Army
         munitions managers use authorizations to prioritize support for the most critical requirements. All
         authorizations are published and managed in Total Ammunition Information Systems (TAMIS).
         The allocation process is the actual supply distributed or redistributed to resource an authorized
         quantity of munitions.

         2-5. Phase IV: Receipt, Storage, and Distribution. The most important phase for ammunition
         support activities in support of mission accomplishment is receipt, storage, and distribution phase.
         Echeloned sustainment will dictate the level of capabilities required for support. Which includes
         receive, configure, inspect, inventory, issue, shipments, and retrograde of munitions stocks.

         2-6. Phase V: Munitions Draw and Use. Customer units request ammunition in TAMIS using
         a TAMIS-generated DA Form 581-SG [System Generated] (Request for Issue and Turn-In of
         Ammunition). Unit commanders or their DA Form 1687 (Notice of Delegation of Authority -
         Receipt for Supplies) designated representative sign and date DA Form 581(Request for Issue and
         Turn-In of Ammunition). Requests for pickup of ammunition are approved by automated means,
         by the approving commander’s signature, or by DA Form 1687-designated approving official.
         DA Forms 581 are used to turn in live ammunition and residue. For more information on
         ammunition requests, see DA PAM 700-16.

STOCK CONTROL

         2-8. The stock records are the core of the stock control system used by ammunition support
         activities. Stock control processes cover all transactions used to maintain and update the stock
         records and supporting documents of a formal stock record account (SRA). The SRA is a holding
         account for ammunition stocks, and ammunition is recorded in an SRA while stored for issue or
         disposal. The SRA is used by modified table of organization and equipment (MTOE) and table of
         distribution and allowances (TDA) units having an ammunition support mission to service their
         customer units. An accountable stock record officer (SRO) must keep the ammunition accounting
         record on prescribed forms or on the accountable property system of record (APSR). For
         prescriptive information on stock control, see AR 700-28 and DA PAM 700-16.

Major Supply Functions

         2-9. Stock Control Monitoring. An accurate inventory is required to make proper decisions
         that control the inventory. The data in the SRA is the basis for inventory control functions of
         requirements, acquisition, disposal, inspection, and repair of stock (supply studies, due-in/due-out
         analysis, and excess).

         2-10. Stock Control Operations. The vouchers posted to the SRA are the basis for the
         ammunition control functions of receiving, issuing, recovering, shipping, and storing which
         includes the transactions (receipts, issues, turn-ins, shipments, inventories, and ammunition
         maintenance).

INVENTORY OPERATION

         2-25. The purpose of inventory and adjustment is to verify the general condition, quantity,
         location of ammunition stock by a physical inspection and count. Adjust stock record balances as
         required to match the results of the physical inspection and count.

         2-26. Types of inventories. Ammunition inventories are conducted as follows:

              • Scheduled Wall-To-Wall. In this inventory, all stocks are counted as of a specific date. In
                   place of doing a wall-to-wall inventory under one SAAS document number, Special
                   inventories by warehouse maybe conducted. However, all storage structures must be
                   inventoried within the prescribed time frame. During a wall-to-wall inventory, all
                   receipts, issues, obligations, and shipments are held until the inventory is over. No
                   transactions are posted to the accountable stock records. If emergency issues and receipts
                   are necessary, tightly control and supervise them to minimize discrepancies. Wall-to-wall
                   inventory is coordinated with customers in garrison and are not generally conducted in
                   combat operations

              • Scheduled Cyclic. In a cyclic inventory, a selected number of DODICs are inventoried
                   during a prescribed period so that all DODICs on hand are inventoried at least once
                   annually or as prescribed by DA PM 700-16. During a cyclic open inventory, processing
                   and posting of receipts, issues, obligations, and shipments continue.

              • Damaged Warehouse. Damaged warehouse inventory is used only in wartime and then
                   only against a warehouse that has been damaged or destroyed.

              • Special. A special inventory is taken on selected ammunition items for a specific reason.
                   Special inventories are conducted either by DODIC, lot number, or warehouse.

         2-27. Ammunition inventory is dictated by the intended use, or account code, for all
         ammunition and falls into categories, such as training and other. The inventory frequency is based

Serviceability Standards.

         2-35. The object of an inspection is to find any deterioration and determine the degree of
         serviceability of the item inspected; that is, whether the item inspected is serviceable as it stands,
         requires maintenance, or must be rejected. Before inspecting an item, the inspector should be
         familiar with all available information about the item, including its components, packaging, and
         the characteristics of the weapon in which it is used. Serviceability standards references are in DA
         PAM 742-1.

         2-36. Inspection procedures include observation, physical tests (such as gauging or strength
         tests), and functional tests. Unserviceability can usually be detected by casual observation. As a
         general guide, Class V munitions must not have any defects that would alter their characteristics,
         make them unsafe, or make them perform in any way other than for what they were designed.
         The inspector must determine whether any defects found can be corrected. Serviceability is not to
         be assumed from the fact that the ammunition can be fired safely in the weapon for which it was
         designed. In addition, it must function correctly, the velocity and pressure must be within
         prescribed limits, and any fuze or detonator present in the projectile must function correctly.

         2-37. The prime enemies of ammunition are heat and moisture. They affect all ammunition
         components to a varying degree. Deterioration is faster when moisture is combined with a rise in
         temperature. Inspectors should be especially watchful for indications of moisture, rust, or
         corrosion on projectiles and fuzes; corrosion and cracks on cartridge cases; deterioration of
         propellants; loose closing caps; and moisture or dampness inside containers

Maintenance Operations

2-44. Maintenance of Class V items include those actions necessary to ensure that stocks are
serviceable or that unserviceable stocks are restored to a serviceable condition, Maintenance
responsibilities are assigned to ammunition units based on the unit’s primary mission and the
availability of personnel, skills, time, tools, equipment, and supplies.

2-45. For ammunition units, maintenance operations during combat operations are based on
Mission, Enemy, Terrain, Troops, Time, and Civil Considerations (METT-TC). The preservation,
packaging, marking, and minor spot-painting of items is normal, but judgment should be used if
the situation calls for more than minor maintenance. Ammunition units in the joint security area
(JSA) may have time to perform maintenance that is above the minor level.

2-46. Ammunition maintenance planning must be aligned closely with the operational needs of
the supported units. Maintenance planners must consider realistically the availability of supplies
and maintenance resources, A decrease in ammunition maintenance increases the amount of
ammunition taken from the supply system. Conversely, the inability of the supply system to
replace unserviceable ammunition requires a greater maintenance effort. Proper maintenance, as
well as proper storage and handling of ammunition, increases readiness, reduces supply
requirements for replacements, and conserves resources for other purposes. The ammunition
planner must recognize the interdependence of maintenance and Class V support. If an ASP is to
perform care and preservation, DA PAM 742-1 provides guidance on setting up a care and
preservation line.

high state of readiness. Maintenance ranges from minor operations, such as cleaning and rust

removal, to major operations, such as complete renovation. Provisions must be made to do as
much maintenance as possible at the storage location. In some cases, ammunition will have to be
evacuated for maintenance. However, since the movement of ammunition involves not only

         3-1. Five principles that apply to all Army inspections. These principles state that all
         inspections must be: (a) Purposeful to accomplish a specific function. Inspections must be related
         to mission accomplishment and tailored to meet the commander's needs. All inspections begin
         with an evaluation against a recognized standard. (b) Coordinated to avoid duplication and
         complement or make whole other inspection activities. Proper coordination minimizes the
         inspection burden on subordinate organizations. (c) Focused on feedback by providing the
         commander with a written or verbal report of the inspection that identifies root causes, names
         strengths and weaknesses, implements corrective actions, and leads to the sharing of inspection
         results. (d) Instructive to bridge gaps in knowledge and experience through teaching and training.
         (e) Followed up to ensure that corrective actions have occurred that fixed the problem areas
         identified in the inspection report.

              • Operational munitions in the hands of troops, such as ammunition on hand within the unit

MATERIEL MANAGEMENT

         4-1. Materiel management is the continuous situational understanding, planning, and
         execution of supply and maintenance capabilities to anticipate, synchronize, and direct all classes
         of supply to maximize combat power, and enable freedom of action in accordance with the
         supported commander's priorities. Materiel management is executed during all four Army
         strategic roles to support the range of military operations.

         4-2. Materiel management is divided into various functions. As an ammunition support
         activity, many of the functions will directly impact the unit’s mission. As an ammunition support
         activity, the training and planning for a mission is not done in isolation. Training that incorporates
         external operation orders (OPORD), material release orders, and shipping directives allows for
         dynamically trained supply point personnel. The two most prevalent materiel management
         functions that affect the ASA are stock control and storage.

              • Stock control is the maintaining of the proper location and identification of materiel
                   within storage locations regardless of class of supply. Materiel managers need correct
                   identification and location of materiel stored in warehouses to ensure the proper item of
                   supply is issued to meet requirements. Unidentified, improperly stored items result in
                   excess or shortage of items being ordered by materiel managers.

              • Storage is the organizing, sorting, and safeguarding of materiel. Storage includes
                   warehouse management, receiving, storing, issuing, securing, inventory management, and
                   accounting for materiel. Warehousing does not imply the use of fixed facilities. Tents,
                   containers, and open pads also serve as warehouses.

         4-3. Additionally, supply planning is forecasting to establish supply stock levels at each
         support echelon to meet mission requirements. Supply planning translates an operating force's
         composition and mission into specific supply requirements. Planning ensures that adequate
         supplies and transportation assets are available. The two most prevailing functions are—

              • Supply which is the provision of materiel to supported units by supply support
                   organizations and activities. It involves requesting, receiving, storing, issuing,
                   maintaining, and establishing accountability of all classes of supplies required to execute
                   a unit’s assigned mission.

              • Resupply is obtaining supplies to meet operational requirements through acquisition, the
                   requisition process, cross-leveling, contracting, and local purchase. Resupply is executed
                   by supply support organizations and activities.

Theater Ammunition Supply Point

         5-2. The Theater ASP is the largest ammunition activity in the changing from one mode of

         5-3. The Theater ASP is normally a permanent or semi-permanent storage activity. It may
         expand to numerous square miles, METT-TC dependent. However, in a combat environment, the
         Theater ASP may be relocated to a field environment where ammunition stocks are kept in open
         storage. If so, it should have as much hardstand as possible and a good road network to support
         heavy traffic. The Theater ASP must be set up to move both breakbulk and containerized
         ammunition onto and off both railcars (if available) and line-haul transporters. To ensure smooth

         rail, air, and port facilities. Other units in or near the Theater ASP (transportation and terminal
         support units) helps the modular ammunition company conduct railhead or port operations
         changing from one mode of transportation to another.

         5-4. The Theater ASP receives 100 percent of its ammunition from the port of debarkation
         (POD), whether it be seaport, air terminal supply point (ATSP), or logistics-over-the-shore

         breakbulk, or a combination of both. The ammunition arrives at the Theater ASP on theater

CAMOUFLAGE, CONCEALMENT, AND DECOYS

         5-20. The possibility of setting up ammunition supply operations in a village or other built-up
         area is very real and requires consideration when planning wartime operations. In the urban built-
         up storage, the real estate could be in an existing small city, a village, or a structure in the
         outlying countryside.

         5-21. Increased survivability is the goal of Camouflage, Concealment, and Decoys (CCD)
         tactics. The unit encourages each soldier to think of survivability and camouflage as one and the
         same. Training soldiers to recognize this correlation instills a greater appreciation of survivability.
         Ammunition support activities are no different than other tactical operations, providing logistical
         support. Using camouflage, concealment, and decoys is one of the most effective ways to
         improve survivability in combat operations.

         5-22. In planning dispersion, advantage is taken of natural concealment. Supply points make
         use of natural cover and concealment whenever possible. Stacks of supplies are dispersed to
         minimize damage from a single explosion. Also, access roads and paths are planned to use
         existing overhead cover. In more permanent installations, tracks running through short open areas
         can be concealed by overhead nets slung between trees. Traffic control includes measures to
         conceal activity and movement at, to, and from the supply activity.

         5-23. Camouflage. Camouflage discipline measures at supply points include track plans that
         result in a minimum of changes in the appearance of terrain, control of debris so that it does not
         accumulate and attract enemy attention, concealment and control of trucks waiting to draw
         supplies, and maintenance of camouflage measures. Supplies may be stacked to simulate many

                  Attacks on Qui Nhon Ammunition Base Depot: Vietnam, 1969

    During 23 February - 23 March 1969, the Ammunition Base Depot at Qui Nhon located
    in Valley "F” Phu Thanh, Republic of Vietnam sustained three sapper attacks by an
    unknown-sized hostile force. The attacks were all detected by an explosion in the depot
    area and contact never was made with a hostile force in the depot or surrounding area.

    The Qui Nhon Ammunition Base Depot was located at the northern base of Cu Mong
    Pass and is surrounded on four sides by higher ground. To the north, there is a natural
    avenue of approach with a low ridgeline and dense vegetation. To the south is Cu Mong
    Pass, which is protected by 7th Company, 2nd Battalion, 26th Regiment, ROKA Capitol
    Division (Tiger). To the east, there is a high ridgeline with extremely dense vegetation.
    On top of the ridgeline, there are several trails, but there are no streams or other sources
    of water resupply. To the west are high hills with extremely dense vegetation, numerous
    valleys, well-used trails, and sufficient streams to provide a resupply of water.

    On 23 February 1969, the unknown-sized enemy sapper infiltrated the depot, set their
    charges on pads 92, 95, and 103 in the northeast area, and left it undetected. The three
    pads, which were satchel-charged, contained 105mmm HE rounds.

    On 10 March 1969, the sapper unit attacked the Qui Nhon Ammunition Base Depot a
    second time. The unit entered the depot in the northeastern perimeter area, placed satchel
    charges on three pads, and left the depot area without being detected. The first explosion
    was on Pad 89, which contained 105mm illumination rounds. Pad 23 contained 81mm
    HE mortars ammunition, and pad 90 contained 2.75-inch rockets.

    On 23 March 1969, a sapper unit once again entered the depot and placed satchel charges
    on pads 76 and 64. As a result of these attacks, the 184th Ordnance Battalion (Ammo)
    received the following casualties: 3 missing in action and presumed dead, 8 wounded as a
    result of hostile action. The three attacks destroyed 23 pads and one small arms area, and
    one pad was damaged with the complete loss of all the stored munitions.

    The attacks on the Qui Nhon Ammunition Base Depot in the Vietnam War offer valuable
    lessons that continue to be relevant in modern warfare, especially concerning ammunition
    storage and security. The attacks, carried out by trained enemy sappers, highlight the
    vulnerability of ammunition depots for infiltration and sabotage. Modern forces must
    employ multi-layered security, including advanced surveillance systems, and better
    perimeter defenses.

                  Ammunition Support Activity Training

In this chapter training for ammunition support activities are discussed, including training principles,
individual and collective task related to military occupational skills directly involved with munitions
operations. Additionally, other types of munitions-specific training that are available to conduct
challenging, relevant, and realistic training. The Army trains the way it operates, closing the gap between
operations and training is imperative for ammunition support activities.

TRAINING PRINCIPLES

6-1. Leaders create training environments as close to combat-like conditions as possible.
Such training environments include field exercises with opposing forces that replicate tough,
realistic, and relevant near peer threats in a variety of operational variables. This allows for
Soldiers and units to train to overcome the stress, chaos, uncertainty, and complexity of combat.

6-2. Units train to a level that is sustainable. As the unit progresses toward training
proficiency, leaders must ensure there is adequate recovery, services, and maintenance operations
to replenish Soldiers, equipment, and systems. This must be done prior to and while the unit
executes its long-range training plan in order to achieve and sustain required training
proficiencies. Including material handling equipment, computer systems, tool kits, and tactical
shelters.

6-3. A mission-essential task (MET) is a collective task on which an organization trains to be
proficient in its designed capabilities or assigned mission. A mission-essential task list is a
tailored group of mission essential tasks. Most deployable company level, and above units have a
standard METL. These are proponent developed and approved by Headquarters, Department of
the Army (HQDA).

6-4. Due to time and resource limitations, units are rarely able to achieve and sustain fully
trained proficiency on all METs simultaneously. Commanders therefore prioritize their METs to
identify the METs that must be resourced and trained to proficiency (Trained: T). Prioritization of
METs is based on mission requirements (concept plan or operation plan and future expected
missions) approved by the next higher echelon commander. METs identified and approved as
lower priority are resourced to maintain proficiency but are not the unit’s training priority.

6-5. Leaders of echelons below company level (platoons, squads, crews, teams, and other
small units) must also prioritize the individual and collective tasks on which their echelon trains.
Limited time and resources prevent lower echelon organizations from simultaneously attaining a
trained proficiency on all collective tasks that support their unit’s METs.

6-6. Leaders of echelons below company level select and prioritize the collective tasks for
their echelon that are most critical to the accomplishment of their company’s prioritized METs.
These prioritized collective tasks below company level that are critical to the successful
accomplishment of prioritized company METs are battle tasks. A battle task is a platoon or lower
echelon collective task that is crucial to the successful accomplishment of a company mission
essential task.

TRAINING MANAGEMENT

         6-39. Units train to three proficiencies and include the following—

              • Mission-essential task proficiency,
              • Weapons qualification, and
              • Collective live-fire tasks.

         6-40. Mission-essential task proficiency is achieved by the unit’s ability to perform mission-
         essential task training to standard; in multiple complex and dynamic operational environments,
         with external evaluation while accomplishing the appropriate Soldier, unit and leader tasks as
         outlined in training and evaluation outlines. See appendix A for training and evaluation outlines.

         6-41. Weapons qualification. Weapons qualification involves individual, crew-served, and
         platform weapons proficiency. The unit achieves weapons qualification by qualifying on all its
         organic weapons under all conditions as specified by applicable weapon system publications.

         6-42. Collective live-fire task proficiency. Collective live-fire task proficiency is achieved by
         the unit executing specified collective tasks while employing its organic weapons systems in a
         live-fire environment.

         6-43. The unit is trained when it achieves the proficiencies specified by the commander.
         Collective task proficiency is rated using the following ratings—

              • Trained: T (Advanced Task Proficiency). A Trained proficiency rating means a unit is
                   trained. It has attained advanced task proficiency free of significant shortcomings. The
                   unit’s shortcomings require minimal training to meet the Army standard.

              • Practiced: P (Basic Task Proficiency). A Practiced proficiency rating means a unit is
                   practiced. It has attained basic task proficiency with shortcomings. The unit’s
                   shortcomings may require significant training to meet the Army standard.

Task Crosswalk

         6-46. Prioritized MET training acknowledges units cannot achieve or sustain fully trained
         proficiency on every task simultaneously. Commanders focus training on their unit’s prioritized
         METs to optimize limited training time and resources. Similarly, subordinate leaders prioritize
         training on the collective tasks, battle tasks, and individual tasks that directly support their unit’s
         prioritized METs. This is done by conducting a task crosswalk.

         6-47. A task crosswalk is the process of linking higher echelon tasks to a lower echelon task
         based on mission requirements. Conducting a task crosswalk allows units to prioritize the tasks
         most important to train at each echelon. This ensures leaders select the right tasks that best
         support the unit training mission. For example, individual tasks must link to a team, crew, or
         squad battle task. Squad battle tasks must link to a platoon battle task. Platoon battle tasks must
         link to a company MET. The task crosswalk also provides leaders a means to analyze training
         time requirements and resources to train before progressing to the next level of training.

         6-48. Step 1: Select the prioritized higher echelon mission-essential task or battle task to
         crosswalk. A task crosswalk begins with the highest prioritized higher echelon MET, platoon or
         squad battle task, or another collective task. Leaders perform a task crosswalk from this starting
         point to identify the supporting tasks at their echelon that best support the higher task.

         6-49. Step 2: Select subordinate collective tasks. In this step, leaders identify the tasks at their
         echelon that best support the higher-level task. These tasks and their prioritization are then
         approved by the next higher echelon leader or commander to ensure proper mission nesting
         (when practicable, leaders select high-payoff tasks that support more than one mission- essential-
         task or battle task). The vignette illustrates the task crosswalk process.

Glossary

Section II

Terms
"T P U"
Trained, Practice needed, Untrained
463L system
Aircraft pallets, nets, tie down, and coupling devices, facilities, handling equipment, procedures, and other
components designed to interface with military and civilian aircraft cargo restraint systems. Though designed for
airlift, system components may have to move intermodally via surface to support geographic combatant commander
objectives. (JP 4-01)
Department of Defense Ammunition Code (DODAC)
An eight-character numeric-alphanumeric code used to identify an item of ammunition. The first four digits consist
of the FSC code, and the last four characters make up the DODIC assigned to the generic description of the item (for
example, 1330-G881, Grenade, Hand, Fragmentation).
accountability
(DOD) The obligation imposed by law or lawful order or regulation on an officer or other person for keeping
accurate record of property, documents, or funds. The person having this obligation may or may not have actual
possession of the property, documents, or funds. Accountability is concerned primarily with records, while
responsibility is concerned primarily with custody, care, and safekeeping. (Army)
Ammunition and explosives holding area (AHA)
An area that is used for temporary storage, weapons assembly and staging. This area is established to fulfill the
operational requirements at the battalion echelons and below. (Army)
ammunition lot number
Coded identification number assigned to a quantity of ammunition that has been produced by the same manufacturer
under uniform conditions. The number is assigned to each lot of ammunition when it is manufactured.
ammunition supply point
An area designated to receive, store, reconfigure, and issue Class V material. It is normally located at or near the
division area. Also called ASP. See also classes of supply. (ATP 4-35)
ammunition transfer and holding point
A designated temporary site from which Class V material is transferred from corps transportation to unit vehicles.
See also classes of supply. (ATP 4-35)
Basic load ammunition holding area (BLAHA)
(DOD) storage area for basic load ammunition located within the boundaries of a barracks or in the immediate
vicinity thereof, in armored vehicles, trucks, trailers, structures, or on pads to ensure mission readiness. The
BLAHAs consist of one or more storage sites and involve acceptance of risks to personnel, facilities and equipment
that are greater than that normally permitted. (Army)
echeloned sustainment
(DOD) An array of capabilities placed at critical locations to link and facilitate support between echelons in an area
of operation. (ATP 4-90)
required supply rate (ammunition)
(DOD) The amount of ammunition expressed in terms of rounds per weapon per day for ammunition items fired by
weapons (and in terms of other units of measure per day for bulk allotment and other items) estimated to be required
to sustain operations of any designated force without restriction for a specified period. Tactical commanders use this
rate to state their requirements for ammunition to support planned tactical operations at specified intervals. The
required supply rate is submitted through command channels. It is consolidated at each echelon and is considered by
each commander in subsequently determining the controlled supply rate within the command. Also called RSR. See
also controlled supply rate. See FM 4-0.
throughput distribution
Throughput distribution bypasses one or more intermediate supply echelons in the supply system to avoid multiple
handling. It leverages configured loads and containerization to deliver supplies directly to units in forward areas. See
ATP 4-42.
