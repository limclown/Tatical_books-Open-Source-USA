                      Introduction

TC 3-20.31-105 provides the details of the integrated weapons training strategy structure
specifically designed for U.S. Army medium tanks. This training circular provides the
training strategy for maneuver commanders to train, evaluate, and assess their unit’s
overall proficiency at home station. The medium tank training strategy includes only the
critical training events to build crew platform readiness using the integrated weapons
training strategy’s standard training structure.

The medium tank training strategy maximizes the training aids, devices, simulators,
simulations, and live-fire events capabilities in a systematic manner to increase and
sustain Soldier and crew proficiency, effectiveness, and lethality. The training strategy
is capability-based (Soldier, crew, ammunition, optics/sensors, and weapon system) and
maximizes the training resources available across the force.

The medium tank training strategy encompasses all critical training that builds a unit’s
ability to shoot, move, and communicate effectively and efficiently. Although units
conduct other training as part of their training plan, the critical training path includes
only those events that are conducted to ensure the maximum experience is achieved or
gained by the trained Soldier and crew.

The term tank is applicable to all variants of the M10 Booker medium tank within this
publication for simplicity. This publication categorizes tanks in three classes based on
their chassis, armor protection, primary armament, and weight as:

     • Heavy tank—a tracked armored vehicle with a 105-millimeter (mm) or larger
          cannon as its primary armament, and a gross weight over 60 tons:
           Its primary purpose is to defeat all types of ground threats including heavy
                tanks, as well as rotary wing aircraft.
           It operates as part of an armor, mechanized, or heavy Cavalry
                company/troop formation.

     • Medium tank—a tracked armored vehicle with a 90-mm or larger cannon as its
          primary armament, with a gross weight between 35 and 60 tons:
           Its primary purpose is to defeat all types of ground threats including light
                and medium tanks, as well as rotary wing aircraft.
           It operates tactically in an Infantry support role with fortification and
                armor defeating capabilities.

     • Light tank—a tracked armored vehicle with a 75-mm to 105-mm cannon as its
          primary armament, with a gross weight less than 35 tons:
           Its primary purpose is to defeat all types of ground threats including light
                and medium armored wheeled and tracked vehicles, as well as dismounted
                threats.
           It operates tactically in an Infantry support role with light-armor and
                fortification defeating capabilities.

PURPOSE

     The medium tank training strategy has six training events that culminate in their
     demonstration of proficiency during a live-fire qualification event. Medium tank
     training consists of all the training events required to provide the commander with an
     effective demonstration of crew capabilities, employment skill, and lethality to build
     unit readiness.

     plans such that crews train through proficiency to support the Infantry squad, section,

     higher echelon collective training seamlessly. Introductory figure 1 shows the structure

     publication follows the standard training structure found in TC 3-20.0 and synchronizes
     with the training principles found in FM 7-0.

Introduction

TRAINING STRATEGY DESIGN

     It is important for units to follow the training strategy as designed. Tanks are complex
     platforms with state-of-the-art fire control systems, ballistic computers, thermal optics,
     target hand-off capabilities, hunter/killer functionality, with a high level of mobility. The
     munitions employed are purpose-based and provide extended stand-off distances that
     directly impact crew survivability and mission accomplishment on the battlefield.

     Engagements are designed to increase the speed of the crew’s direct fire kill chain to
     defeat tactical dilemmas. All training and standards are based on achieving the desired
     effects against the tactical dilemma before those threats deliver lethal fires. Training to
     this standard requires a training model that instructs and evaluates the crew’s ability to
     employ their platform, fire control system, optics, munitions, and redundant systems
     against the variety of target packages they are expected to defeat.

     The training model identifies the general capabilities of the crew, system, optics,
     ammunition, most probably threat arrays, and battlefield conditions to determine the
     maximum number of engagements a trained crew should experience. The training model
     includes the maximum number of engagements possible based on the basic conditions:

          • Day, night, or conditions that limit visibility.
          • Offense or defense operations.
          • Precision or degraded engagement skills.
          • Threat array:

                 Number of threats—one to four within presentation.
                 Posture of threats—stationary or moving.
                 Range band—close (less than 300 meters), mid (300 to 900 meters), long

                     (900 meters to 1,600 meters) or extended (more than 1,600 meters).
          • Chemical environment.

     Without considering the possible ammunition types and two separate firers, or the ability
     to engage simultaneous targets with multiple weapon systems, the training model
     baseline includes over 10,000 engagement possibilities. Within those engagements,
     approximately 1,920 engagements are designed for training within precision gunnery
     simulations per fiscal year by a crew. Simulations are critical to the crew’s education
     and experience.

     To augment the training strategy, the unit needs experienced tank commanders and
     gunners who serve as subject matter experts, trained and certified trainers, a Master
     Gunner, instructor/operators, and vehicle crew evaluators. Providing training resources
     alone is not enough. Without certified trainers and leaders, units will struggle to achieve
     the fundamentals of tactical employment and experience difficulty achieving trained,
     qualified, and lethal crews.

     Training is comprehensive and should be focused on addressing cognitive skills
     (recognition of target signatures, identification of threat, classification and prioritization,
     understanding of fire control systems, optics, and capabilities of ammunition), fine
     motor skills (tracking and manipulation), and instinctual reactions to situational stimuli
     (battle drills, conduct of fire responses, and application of the direct fire kill chain). Such
     training is formed through correctly performing the tasks in repetition, on demand of the
     tactical situation.

Introduction

     Units with a robust simulation training plan and follow the training strategy provide
     2,000 engagements to their crews. When units cannot provide the training to the crews
     per the training strategy, the impacts on the crew’s experience can be described using
     the number of engagements fired.

     Figure 4 shows the impacts of the training strategy’s 2,000 engagement-designed
     structure. Common instance in the unit’s training calendar causes the crew’s experience
     on engagements to drop to 727 engagements total.

     A crew that loses over 60 percent of the training strategy’s programmed engagement
     experience may provide a unit a qualified crew initially. Crews that are not provided the
     training resources by their unit will not fully develop their engagement skills and
     lethality. In subsequent live-fire events, crew evaluations will identify basic failures in
     the conduct of fire, reticle aim, fire control system management, and engagement
     techniques. Those training gaps will ultimately result in failed qualification attempts.

     When units remove the training events from their training plan, including the use and
     management of simulations, the proficiency and lethality of the unit diminishes rapidly.
     In those instances, the commander loses qualified crews, unit readiness, and the
     flexibility to correct talent management or personnel turbulence issues rapidly.

     the training event definition, the purpose, method, and end state of the training, and a
     guide to plan, prepare, execute, and assess each event. Chapter 7 details methods to
     manage the proficiency of the crew once they achieve proficiency.

CHAPTER 1—TABLE I, GUNNERY SKILLS TEST

     Chapter 1 describes the training and evaluation requirements for each Soldier’s Gunnery
     Skills Test. It includes required training tasks for crew protection such as roll-over drills,
     crew evacuation from all positions, and react to multiple types of vehicle fires. The
     chapter provides general information for the unit to plan, prepare, execute, and assess
     the event using the eight-step training model.

CHAPTER 2—TABLE II, SITUATIONAL TRAINING EXERCISE-VIRTUAL

     Chapter 2 describes the section-level Situational Training Exercise-Virtual. The chapter
     includes the purpose, method, and end state of the event based on the simulation
     system’s training model and the required gate to live-fire exercise. The chapter provides
     general information for the unit to plan, prepare, execute, and assess the event using the
     eight-step training model.

     training aids, devices, simulators, simulations and includes the purpose, method, and
     end state of the event based on the platform’s capabilities and crew’s tasks. The chapter
     provides general information for the unit to plan, prepare, and execute, the event using
     the eight-step training model. Chapter 3 provides required training tasks for crew

               Table I–Gunnery Skills Test

    Understanding the weapons and weapon system platforms is instrumental in
    the development of the warrior skills trained in marksmanship and gunnery.
    The information taught and evaluated in the Gunnery Skills Test for crew
    platforms teaches, tests, and evaluates the critical live-fire tasks that increase
    Soldiers’ proficiency. The Gunnery Skills Test is used to instruct Soldiers
    on how to maintain, operate, and correct malfunctions of the platform’s
    weapons, as well as the procedures and performance measures required to
    place the vehicle in operation for live-fire event. It includes safety-specific
    tasks that reinforce the proper procedures in the event of an emergency on
    the vehicle.

OVERVIEW—TABLE I

     1-1. The crew platform, Table I, Gunnery Skills Test (GST), is a hands-on
     demonstration of proficiency and written evaluation of weaponry tasks, characteristics,
     capabilities, and vehicle safety related skills that serves as a prerequisite to any live-fire
     event. It verifies the Soldier’s ability to perform critical tasks, has the basic
     understanding of combat identification, emergency procedures, and other vital skills to
     participate in live-fire training as part of a crew.

                              Live-Fire Prerequisites

          Table I is a prerequisite for any live-fire event, including zero
          procedures, sub-caliber or in-bore training, or any live-fire
          maintenance procedure. Units must maintain the training records
          and results, for the longer of 12 months, or through the next testing
          event.

     1-2. Training prior to Table I is a platoon and company or troop responsibility. Table I,
     GST, is a test that serves as the commander’s official record of training proficiency prior
     to entering a live-fire training density.

     1-3. Units must complete Table I, GST, for record between six weeks or months (as
     applicable) before execution (T-6) and training execution (T-execution) or the week of
     training execution (T-week) of the live-fire event. Units can include additional tasks
     they require to support the training program but cannot eliminate tasks for any weapon
     system. Figure 1-1 on page 2 shows where Table I resides within the medium tank
     training strategy through crew qualification.

  Table II–Situational Training Exercise–Virtual

    Crew Platform, Table II, Situational Training Exercise-Virtual is a
    simulations-based demonstration of the performance of employing the
    element to its primary capabilities. Table II itself is the culminating
    evaluation of the crew after completing the appropriate simulations training
    strategy within the simulation system itself. Table II is a replication
    (simulation) of the crew’s live-fire qualification course. It provides a
    measured demonstration of performance prior to any live-fire event for the
    element.

OVERVIEW—TABLE II

     2-1. The purpose of crew platform, Table II, Situational Training Exercise-Virtual
     (STX-V), is to ensure the crew possesses the skills and experience necessary to execute
     live-fire events and exercises safely, which implies a functional understanding of the
     engagement process, fire commands, engagement techniques, and lethality. This is the
     culminating test of their abilities and skills on their platform in simulation after
     completing the appropriate simulations training plan.

                              Live-Fire Prerequisite

          Table II is a prerequisite for any live-fire event, including zero
          procedures, sub-caliber or in-bore training, or any live-fire
          maintenance procedure. Units must maintain the training records
          and results, for the longer of 12 months, or through the next testing
          event.

     2-2. Table II is completed after successful advancement in the approved simulation
     system’s training model. Table II is the GTLF exercise within that training system which

       Table III–Situational Training Exercise

    Table III, Situational Training Exercise, is a live environment, hands on
    training event using training aids and devices, and includes the use of blank
    ammunition, pyrotechnics, and battle effects simulators. Situational
    Training Exercise is a station-based event that consists of three distinct
    training and evaluation stations that contain various crew skills that
    progressively build crew proficiency. All events require both day and night
    execution. Table III, Situational Training Exercise is designed to be
    conducted independently of the direct fire engagement event. Table III is
    conducted in a live environment in a training area with established training
    lanes (course of fire and protection tasks). It is the first training event where
    the crew is tactically operating their platform as a crew under varying
    conditions.

OVERVIEW—SITUATIONAL TRAINING EXERCISE

     3-1. Table III verifies the crew’s ability to complete basic crew engagements effectively
     using various platform capabilities, provides for day and night driver’s training as part
     of the unit driver’s training program, and ensures the crew demonstrates proficiency
     performing specific emergency procedures on their platform. It provides time for
     training and evaluation of these skills that are the foundation to all maneuver training
     focused on the ability to move, shoot, and communicate.

     3-2. Units must complete Table III, Situational Training Exercise (STX), for record
     between T-6 and T-execution, or T-week of the live-fire event. Units can include
     additional tasks they require to support the training program but cannot eliminate tasks
     for any weapon system.

                              Live-Fire Prerequisite

          Table III is a prerequisite for any live-fire event, including zero
          procedures, sub-caliber or in-bore training, or any live-fire
          maintenance procedure. Units must maintain the training records
          and results, for the longer of 12 months, or through the next testing
          event.

     3-43. The Instrumentable-Multiple Integrated Laser Engagement System Combat
     Vehicle Tactical Engagement Simulation System (I-MILES CVTESS) is a laser-based
     training device to for use on Abrams, Bradley, medium tank, and opposing forces tanks

     evolutionary approach for replacing older systems with equipment that provides better
     training fidelity. It will reinforce crew duties, reward proper engagement techniques,
     and develop tactical maneuver skills of armor and mechanized infantry combined arms
     teams up to brigade level. The I-MILES CVTESS provides unit commanders an
     integrated training system in force-on-force and force-on-target training events at home
     station training areas through instrumented training. The system interfaces with
     instrumentation systems at combat training centers.

Crew Module Unit/Crew Module Unit Recorder/Integrated Player Unit

     3-45. The crew module unit (CMU)/integrated player unit (IPU)/crew module unit
     recorder (CMUR) is an advanced eight channel audio/video recording device for combat
     vehicles. The CMU/IPU/CMUR can simultaneously record up to eight channels of
     video, one channel of audio, and has the capability to monitor dual 1553 data busses.
     The CMU/IPU/CMUR is equipped with a global positioning system that captures the
     vehicle’s position location information. The CMU/IPU is for use on a digital range
     complex whereas the CMUR does not require any external support. The CMUR has an
     integrated AAR suite and stores information on a solid-state drive. The AAR suite
     uploads the data from the solid-state drive allowing a full AAR presentation.

    Table IV, Basic, is designed to train basic skills to enable proper leader and

    single and multiple stationary or moving targets placed in a tactical array,
    during the day, with an operational or simulated degraded fire control
    system, as applicable. Crews execute these tasks from the defense using full-
    caliber ammunition for zero and machine gun ammunition for the tactical
    engagements.

OVERVIEW—TABLE IV

     4-1. Table IV provides crews the ability to align their fire control system to zero their
     ammunition by type, and complete basic machine gun engagement skills from a
     defensive firing position. There are two task sets to Table IV, Basic:

          • Zeroing.
          • Defensive engagements.

PURPOSE OF TABLE IV

     4-4. Table IV, Basic, is a skills assessment and risk mitigation tool for the commander.
     It assists the commander in determining the safe and effective use of the platform, its
     weapons, identification of threats and common ammunition by the crewmembers in the
     unit. Further, it identifies shortfalls and training gaps in those gunnery specific skills,
     which build crew proficiency and unit readiness.

METHOD—TABLE IV

     4-5. Table IV is a hands-on, performance-based evaluation of basic gunnery skills to the
     Army-standard of knowledge or skill in a live-fire environment. It utilizes experienced
     evaluators to educate, train, test, and retrain Soldiers to meet or exceed those standards.

END STATE—TABLE IV

     4-6. The VC and gunner assigned as the crew have successfully demonstrated the skills
     and knowledge required to continue training on the platform through platform
     qualification. Commanders are confident in their Soldier’s abilities in the position
     assigned.

     4-7. The tasks evaluated in Table IV provide the commander a means to verify the
     crew’s basic weapons platform proficiency prior to further live-fire training. It is a guide
     for the commander to identify individual and crew weapons platform strengths and
     weaknesses on specific engagement techniques and employment of the vehicle’s fire
     control system capabilities and enablers. The commander and Master Gunner use the
     results from Table IV, Basic to structure the unit’s crew training program.

     5-1. Table V, Practice, serves as a means to train and evaluate the crew’s ability to

                  Table VI–Qualification

    Crew platform, Table VI, Qualification, is the record course of fire used to
    determine the live-fire proficiency of a crew platform. Table VI serves as
    the live-fire proficiency event for readiness reporting. It is also the live-fire
    prerequisite for the crew to move to collective live-fire events. Table VI,
    Qualification, is a live-environment training event under live-fire conditions
    using full-caliber training ammunition, realistic targetry and engagement
    scenarios, at a combat tempo, conducted on an authorized live-fire facility.
    Regardless of crew platform type, this table reinforces the basic skills
    trained during previous events to build leader and element tactical and
    technical mastery.

OVERVIEW—TABLE VI

     6-1. Table VI, Qualification, is conducted from the crew’s vehicle and uses full caliber
     training ammunition on an authorized live-fire facility or safety certified training area
     that assesses a crew’s tactical employment proficiency and lethality. Table VI is used to
     train and evaluate the crew’s ability to engage stationary or moving targets placed in a
     tactical array, during day and limited visibility conditions, with an operational or
     simulated degraded fire control system, as applicable. Crews execute these tasks from
     the offense and defense using full-caliber ammunition, against full-scale targetry at full
     range.

     6-2. This table requires each crew to successfully complete the live-fire prerequisites,
     all zeroing procedures for their platform, and at a minimum, complete the defensive
     engagements on Table IV. It is the qualification event for medium tank crews.

                              Table VI, Qualification

          This event builds unit proficiency and is a requirement to build unit

          purposes. Failing indicates a clear lack of proficiency at basic
          engagement skills.

     6-3. Figure 6-1 on page 164 shows where Table VI, Qualification, resides withing the

TRAINING PLAN—TABLE VI

     6-14. Table VI, Qualification, provides a challenging scenario to the crews to prepare
     them for collective live-fire events and deployment. The qualification course exercises
     the crew’s conduct of fire and engagement skills in a demanding manner that
     demonstrates the minimum proficiency of the crew.

     6-15. Units are required to use full-scale targets at full range for the engagement
     scenarios unless specifically directed. To maintain training standards across the Army,
     there is no authorization for deviation from the engagements on Table VI beyond the
     guidelines listed in TC 3-20.31 for alternate targetry.

     6-16. The design of Table VI is purposefully challenging to the crews while providing
     realistic engagements that exercise the crew’s ability to utilize the key capabilities of the
     vehicle’s fire control system. Units retrain as necessary with full-caliber ammunition
     using first-round hit savings (harvested ammunition) from the previous tables in the
     training strategy. Units must manage the available resources to ensure all crews
     successfully complete Table VI before proceeding to any collective live-fire event or
     deployment.

     6-17. All crew platform tables are multiple qualification criteria tables. Crews must
     score at least 70 points per target according to the target kill standards in TC 3-20.31 on
     single and multiple target engagements to qualify the engagement. Crews must also
     score more than 700 points overall with a minimum of 70 points per target during 7 of 10
     engagements on the qualification table to be used. Crews must qualify at least one
     engagement during day and one engagement during limited visibility to be qualified.

     6-18. Table VI uses full caliber training ammunition on an authorized live-fire facility
     or safety certified training area that assesses an element’s tactical employment
     proficiency and lethality.

     6-19. Crews must successfully complete the qualification course prior to executing any
     collective live-fire training. Units must follow the training guidelines for those gates in
     the appropriate training circular. Units may conduct make-up qualifications prior to live
     collective training when required. However, units must accomplish live-fire
     prerequisites as directed.

DISQUALIFICATION

     7-24. A disqualification is the immediate removal from live training of any unsafe crew
     until the crew satisfactorily completes a recertification program. Disqualification is a
     critical failure in the safe conduct of the range by leaders using weapons or weapon
     systems in such a manner that has the potential to injure or kill Soldiers or civilians, or
     excessive damage to government property.

     7-25. The standards by which a crew or crewmember may be judged includes certain
     criteria. Disqualification occurs—

          • When a crew performs any actions listed in the immediate disqualification
                section of DA Form 8265-1.

          • When a crew performs three 30-point penalties each as listed on
                DA Form 8265-1 during the same table.

          • For reasons not associated with firing—
                 The unit commander may disqualify individuals or crews that consistently
                     perform unsafe acts during non-firing or preparation tasks.
                 This type of disqualification will be at the discretion of the unit
                     commander.

     7-26. Any crew disqualified for any reason may not refire without brigade commander
     approval after corrective training is complete. Corrective training for disqualified crews
     includes, at a minimum, repeating and successful completion of:

          • Table I, GST.
          • Table II, STX-V. GTLF on the authorized training system.
          • Table III, STX.

     7-27. Disqualified crews indicate a significant lack of awareness for safety on the
     vehicle. The requirement for retraining on Tables I, II, III, and V, at a minimum, may
     be waived by the first general officer in the chain of command and may not be delegated.

             Note. The battalion or squadron commander is the approving authority for all
             recertification. A commander may impose additional retraining requirements
             as desired to ensure the crew’s safety and effective performance during
             live-fire training.

Assess

     7-37. Units must assess new Soldier and crewmember proficiency on their platform.
     Commanders that receive new leaders who achieved proficiency within the last
     qualification period have assessment options to maintain element qualifications.
     Commanders can assess two previously qualified key leaders from different elements to
     determine their proficiency status if they are assigned together in an element. This is the
     least preferred method of talent management and requires the commander to make
     assessments using previous crew records, sustainment training in simulations, and other
     assessments to determine their proficiency status.

Assign

     7-38. Select the tank commander and gunner combinations together. This maximizes the
     key leader’s longevity and future proficiency ratings more stable. Assign each
     crewmember to their position on their potential and longevity. Place those leaders in the
     next higher position (gunner to VC, or driver to gunner, for example).

Transfer

     7-39. When promotions occur, commanders should make every effort to keep the trained
     key leaders together, including inter-battalion transfers. Commanders should consider
     moving the newly promoted key leader and key leader combination together where the
     gaining unit within the battalion will benefit from maintaining proficient key leaders.

Cross-train

     7-40. Experienced Soldiers are easier to train than new Soldiers. Units should utilize
     harvested training ammunition to train and qualify alternate firers to build experience.
     Units should invest first-round-hit ammunition savings, commonly referred to as
     ammunition harvesting, to cross training young leaders in key leader positions. In doing
     so, commanders can mitigate key leader turnover with trained and proficient leaders in
     stride with key leader turbulence. Ammunition available through first round hit savings
     vary from training event to training event. Units must be prepared to execute training
     based on the varying training munitions availability and plan for additional time on
     range facilities to build depth in their trained and qualified cross-trained crews.

     7-41. Commanders must ensure the personnel officer is involved in crew management
     through requesting stabilization for Soldiers or organizations in unique situations. Some
     of these situations are described in paragraphs 7-42 to 7-44.

Appendix A

105-MM AMMUNITION COMPONENTS

     A-6. All 105-mm ammunition cartridges consist of five basic components, the cartridge
     case, primer, propellant, obturator, and projectile. See figure A-5 for more information.

                       Figure A-5. 105-mm ammunition components

CARTRIDGE CASE

     A-7. The cartridge case houses the propellant and the projectile. The case causes rear
     obturation, which is the action of the cartridge case expanding against the rear portion
     of the chamber region by the high pressure of the internal and forward gases. The case
     is locked with the forward obturator. The case base has markings with the cartridge lot
     number, cartridge case lot number, cartridge nomenclature, cartridge case type, and the
     weapon system (see figure A-6).

     Figure A-6. Case base

226  TC 3-20.31-105         04 April 2025


---

                                                                                                         Ammunition

PRIMER

     A-8. The primer, as shown in figure A-7, is screwed into the case base. This receives
     the electrical charge from the circuit which ignites the primer filler. The primer filler is
     a volatile igniter made of benite strands that ignite the propellant.
     A-9. Benite is extruded strands of black powder (potassium nitrate, charcoal, and
     sulfur) embedded in nitrocellulose. When the electrical charge is applied to the ignitor,
     the ignitor actuates the benite strands which vent through the primer case holes causing
     deflagration of the propellant. Activation of the ignitor occurs between 280 milliamperes
     and 1.25 amps.

PROPELLANT

     A-10. The propellant is contained within the cartridge case (see figure A-8). The
     different propellant types vary by the model number of the round, listed later within this
     appendix.

OBTURATOR

     A-11. The obturator, located between the cartridge case and the projectile, causes
     forward obturation (see figure A-9). This is used to prevent propellant gases from
     leaking around the outside of the projectile. The slip band on the obturator absorbs
     approximately 80 percent of the projectile spin on fin and static stabilized rounds. The
     drive band, or rotating band, imparts spin on spin stabilized rounds.

PROJECTILE

     A-12. The projectile is the cylindrical portion of the cartridge between the end of the
     primer tube and the end of the cartridge case. The projectile travels down the bore toward
     the target. The projectile, based on the type of round, may house a sub-projectile,
     payload, or explosive chemical energy compound and fuze.
     A-13. Based on the type of round, after shot exit, the round may—

          • Arm a chemical warhead.
          • Begin separating sabot petals from a sub-projectile.
          • Continue downrange on the ballistic path.
     A-14. The projectile has three different color applications: black, olive drab, or light
     blue. The black projectile is an armor defeating round, olive drab is for anti-personnel
     or anti-material, and the light blue color indicates training (target practice). See

                                     Appendix B

         Tracking and Manipulation Boards

    Tracking and manipulation panels are tools to train hand-and-eye,
    coordination, gun control, speed, and accuracy using the platform’s fire
    control system. In this appendix, there are six variations as examples for
    units to consider; however, units can create unique layouts, as their training
    program requires.

    T&MBs, more commonly referred to as snake boards, should have a wide
    variety of designs with horizontal, vertical, and oblique motion in any
    direction. The panels may also incorporate (along the panel’s tracking path)
    scaled targets that replicate the most effective way to lay on, lase, and
    engage a target. Paths on the boards can be tracked in all fire control modes
    and incorporate the use of all sights in most cases.

OVERVIEW–TRACKING AND MANIPULATION
BOARDS

     B-1. T&MB exercises are used to augment the STX-V or use when no simulator system
     is available or accessible to the crew. T&MBs are locally manufactured based on the
     unit need. Completion of the tracking board exercise is the least preferred method of
     completing Table II for live fire.

     B-2. T&MBs should only be used in extreme cases (new vehicle with no simulator
     system, unit is deployed, or retraining is necessary in an austere or field environment)
     or as opportunity training in the motor pool, training area, or administration area on a
     range facility.

     B-3. The purpose of crew platform, Table II, STX-V, is to ensure the crew possesses
     the skills and experience necessary to execute live-fire events and exercises safely,
     which implies a functional understanding of the engagement process, fire commands,
     engagement techniques, and lethality. This is the culminating test of their abilities and
     skills on their platform in simulation after completing the appropriate simulations
     training plan (see figure B-1 on page 240).

     B-4. Table II, STX-V, is a mandatory prerequisite for any live-fire event, including

PURPOSE–TRACKING AND MANIPULATION BOARDS

     B-6. Table II, STX-V, is a skills assessment and risk mitigation tool for the
     commander. It assists the commander in determining the safe and effective use of the
     platform, its weapons, identification of threats and common ammunition by the
     crewmembers in the unit. Further, it identifies shortfalls and training gaps in those
     gunnery specific skills, which build crew proficiency and unit readiness.

METHOD–TRACKING AND MANIPULATION BOARDS

     B-7. Table II is a hands-on, performance-based evaluation of basic gunnery skills to
     the Army-standard of knowledge or skill in a simulator system (crew shelter). It utilizes
     experienced evaluators to educate, train, test, and retrain Soldiers to meet or exceed
     those standards.
