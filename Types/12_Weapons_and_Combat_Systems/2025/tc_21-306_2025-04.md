                                 Preface

TC 21-306 provides unit commanders and unit master drivers with guidance on developing tracked, combat
vehicle driver training programs for their organizations. The Central Army Registry and the Unit Training
Assistance Program provide units with resources and guidance to support training that promotes safe driving
practices, accident avoidance, and technical competence to produce more lethal and effective combat vehicle
crews. Hands-on training in a controlled environment and under the close supervision of qualified and
licensed trainers is a must in all phases of driver training.

The principal audience for TC 21-306 is all members of the profession of arms. Commanders and staffs of
Army headquarters serving as joint task force or multinational headquarters should also refer to applicable
joint or multinational doctrine concerning the range of military operations and joint or multinational forces.
Trainers and educators throughout the Army will also use this publication.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure that their decisions and actions comply with applicable United
States, international, and in some cases host-nation laws and regulations. Commanders at all levels ensure
that their Service members operate in accordance with the law of armed conflict and the rules of engagement.
(See FM 6-27 for legal compliance.)

TC 21-306 applies to the Active Army, Army National Guard/Army National Guard of the United States,
and United States Army Reserve unless otherwise stated.

The proponent of TC 21-306 is the United States Army Maneuver Center of Excellence. The preparing
agency is the Doctrine and Collective Training Division, Department of Tactics, Training, and Doctrine,
United States Army Maneuver Center of Excellence. Send comments and recommendations on

                                                 Chapter 1

                                 Introduction

    Leaders must emphasize driver training within their organizations because of the length, width,
    and high ground pressure of tracked combat vehicles, the terrain tracked combat vehicles are
    required to operate in, and the numerous blind spots associated with tracked combat vehicles.
    These variables demand additional skills and knowledge beyond those required for wheeled
    vehicles. Unit drivers training programs must challenge drivers to use safe driving practices,
    increase their awareness to improve accident avoidance, and improve crew performance.
    Programs must train drivers to understand the operational limitations of the vehicles they are
    operating so they can negotiate complex terrain under difficult and stressful conditions.

SECTION I – SCOPE

     1-1. TC 21-306 provides leaders with the tools they need to produce quality drivers. Leaders must emphasize
     driver training because of the variety of vehicle characteristics and inherent dangers associated with the
     platforms. Because of this, tracked combat vehicle drivers must possess excellent driver skills and knowledge
     beyond that of any other family of tactical vehicles. Operators must know how to operate vehicle equipment
     effectively in the most challenging environments. Operators must be challenged to use safe driving practices
     and increased awareness for accident avoidance. Due to the extreme weight and operating characteristics
     associated with tracked vehicles they have a greater potential to cause fatalities and extreme damage when
     being operated.

     1-2. Vehicle commander (VC) is defined as the occupant of a vehicle assigned responsibility for the overall
     safe operation and employment of the vehicle, the weapon systems, and any special equipment. The VC is
     responsible for the safety of all occupants. VCs must be licensed on the vehicle they are responsible for.

     1-3. This training circular provides vehicle operators with techniques to enhance their driving skills. This
     training circular does not replace AR 600-55 that specifies the regulatory requirements for selecting, training,
     testing, and licensing drivers. Unit commanders have the flexibility to make minor modifications in their
     training due to the mission set of the unit without compromising the integrity of this training circular and its
     supporting doctrine. Unit constraints on terrain and resources may also limit the extent to which a training
     circular can be fully implemented. However, operators must pass the written test for each specific vehicle
     before issuance of an OF 346 (U.S. Government Motor Vehicle Operator’s Identification Card) or
     DA Form 5984-E (Operator’s Permit Record), according to AR 600-55. The information in this training
     circular applies to all Department of Defense (DOD) civilians, DOD contractors, Active Army, Army
     Reserve, and National Guard units that employ tracked combat vehicles.

SECTION II – SUPPORTING MATERIAL

     1-4. In addition to TC 21-306, the following training and regulatory materials apply to the conduct of tracked
     combat vehicle training:

          • AR 600-55 describes the following:
                 Responsibilities for conducting the motor vehicle driver program.
                 Licensing requirements for applicants of motor vehicle driver positions.
                 The process for choosing potential drivers.
                 Training programs for operators of all types of vehicles and equipment.
                 Driver testing program.
                 Controls on issuing licenses.
                 Types of licenses that may be issued.
                 Procedures for renewing, revoking, or suspending licenses.

          • AR 190-5.
          • Applicable technical manual for the tracked vehicle system or variant being operated.

                                                 Chapter 2

  Safety Awareness and Environmental Risk Management

    This chapter describes the hazards and associated risk management efforts for tracked vehicle
    driver training programs. Always monitor safety practices and application during the training
    phase. (See ATP 5-19 for more detailed risk management guidance.)

SECTION I – SAFETY AWARENESS

     2-1. Tactical vehicle accidents are a large contributor to on-duty Soldier injury and fatalities. Driving can be
     one of the deadliest hazards Soldiers face daily. The following paragraphs describe subjects related to tactical
     vehicle safety. When followed, these areas will aid in the increase of safety awareness and reduce vehicle
     related accidents.

SEAT BELTS

     2-2. The ability to remain restrained to a stable surface is an essential component of safety and survival in
     the event there is a mishap. With consistent practice, this essential element of tactical discipline becomes
     second nature. Concerns about one’s ability to rapidly exit a vehicle must be tempered by the knowledge that
     seat belts prevent occupants from being tossed around the vehicle’s interior because of enemy action or a
     vehicle accident. Remaining conscious offers the best chance to escape from a burning or submerged vehicle.
     All vehicle occupants must wear seatbelts when the vehicle is in motion. If equipped, restraint harnesses will
     be utilized. Vehicles should have seatbelt cutters for all occupants to cut away the seat belt during an
     emergency.

     2-3. The vehicle commander is responsible for ensuring all Soldiers are properly using the vehicle restraint
     systems. All crewmembers must be experts in the use and operation of the systems. Checking for and
     enforcing the use of restraint systems should be done prior to any vehicle movement. Periodic checks during
     operations should also be conducted.

PERSONAL PROTECTIVE EQUIPMENT

     2-4. Hearing loss among Armor crewmembers can occur due to improper fit, wear, and maintenance of
     combat vehicle crewmember (CVC) helmets. All crewmembers wear CVC helmets: passengers wear
     earplugs and the advanced combat helmet when the vehicle is operating. When the CVC helmet is worn,
     crewmembers ensure that the chinstrap is fastened. The CVC helmet does not reduce sound properly unless
     it is fastened. Tracked combat vehicles are heavily laden with fuel and ammunition; while they are designed
     to direct explosions away from the crew, there is still a greater risk of fire associated with tracked combat
     vehicles than other vehicles. When operating a tracked combat vehicle, drivers and other crewmembers will
     wear the flame-resistant CVC uniform as prescribed in DA Pam 670-1 and in accordance with their unit
     standard operating procedure (SOP).

SHOP AND MOTOR POOL

     2-5. Crewmembers must take certain precautions in a maintenance shop or motor pool. Oil, water, and
     antifreeze spills can cause serious injury. To prevent injuries, all spills must be cleaned up immediately and
     the work area should be kept clean. Injuries often result from using the wrong tools and equipment. Trainers
     must instruct all personnel to properly use the tools and basic issue items associated with their vehicle. To
     prevent severe injuries to fingers, wrists, and limbs, crewmembers must remove all jewelry before mounting,
     dismounting, or performing operator maintenance on a vehicle. Vehicle chock blocks must be used when the
     vehicle is parked. Hair must be worn in a manner that prevents loose or dangling strands from being caught
     or tangled up in machinery.

Chapter 2

MOUNTING AND DISMOUNTING TRACKED COMBAT VEHICLES

     2-6. Drivers and track commanders must ensure everyone observes the following rules for mounting and
     dismounting tracked vehicles:

          • Use extreme caution when mounting or dismounting a vehicle.
          • Never climb in front of a weapon system to mount the vehicle, and VCs must ensure that all weapon

                systems are clear and positioned to allow safe access.
          • When mounting or dismounting a vehicle with the engine running, ensure the driver is aware of

                personnel mounting and dismounting.
          • Ensure the driver is aware of a crewmember’s intention to mount.
          • Always maintain three points of contact (one hand and two feet or two hands and one foot) with the

                vehicle when mounting, dismounting, or moving around on the vehicle.
          • Never mount or dismount a moving vehicle, and drivers must bring the vehicle to a complete halt,

                place the vehicle in neutral, and set parking brake before allowing anyone to mount or dismount.
          • Never dismount a vehicle by jumping from it.

HATCH COVERS

     2-7. Injuries caused by unsecured hatch covers are common. All crewmembers must check the hatches before
     operating the vehicle to make sure they are serviceable and locked in the proper position. Many vehicles are
     equipped with chains to secure the hatches. If the vehicles are equipped with chains, they must be used.

RIDING POSITION

     2-8. Crewmembers in tracked combat vehicles must wear CVC helmets and eye protection and ride with only
     their heads and shoulders extended (nametag defilade) out of the hatches if the operational environment
     permits it. When a tracked vehicle is involved in a collision or overturns, injuries are usually the result of
     crewmembers being thrown around inside the vehicle. To prevent injuries during rollovers or collisions,
     crewmembers and vehicle occupants must be in their assigned positions and wear the proper restraints during
     vehicle operations.

SLAVE STARTING

     2-9. When slave starting a vehicle, crewmembers must observe the following safety guidelines:
          • Drivers always position the live vehicle alongside the dead vehicle.
          • Drivers never position the vehicles nose-to-nose.
          • Crewmembers must not stand between the moving vehicle and the dead vehicle; serious injury or
                death could result.
          • Drivers and crews must consult their vehicle’s technical manual to ensure they are performing
                vehicle slave starting procedures correctly for their specific model of vehicle.

             Note. Terrain must be carefully considered prior to conducting slave start operations. It may be
             necessary to have the vehicle moved to a safe location if the terrain adds an additional danger due
             to slope/grade or other restrictions. Moving the vehicle may require maintainer assistance.

VEHICLE ACCIDENTS

     2-10. As previously mentioned, vehicle accidents contribute to Soldier injury and fatalities. Paragraphs 2-11
     through 2-18 describe problematic areas as they pertain to vehicle accidents. Each area discusses the danger
     and ways to mitigate the hazard. The areas discussed are not all inclusive when identifying hazards related
     to vehicle accidents. Each platform may have additional associated hazards due to the vehicle’s operating
     characteristics.

FATIGUE AND SLEEP LOSS

     2-11. Fatigue and sleep loss are often factors in vehicle accidents. To minimize the effects of sleep loss,
     commanders must develop and follow a sleep plan based on the following considerations:

          • Adequate sleep is required to enable an individual to maintain optimal performance; humans do not
                adapt well to shortened sleep cycles.

          • Physical strength remains unimpaired until extreme levels of sleep deprivation are reached.
          • The most difficult jobs for the sleep-deprived are tasks requiring swift decisions or complex

                planning.
          • Sleep loss typically causes errors of omission.
          • Prolonged heat exposure, confinement, noise, and vibration degrade performance and ability to cope

                with sleep loss.
          • Diminished awareness:

                 Check operators for symptoms of fatigue or use of controlled substances.
                 Personnel taking prescription drugs that may cause drowsiness should not drive.

SITUATIONAL AWARENESS

     2-12. Drivers and track commanders must be alert to avoid situations conducive to accidental injury and to
     minimize the possibility of injury in those situations that cannot be avoided. Personnel must take extra
     precautions when the vehicle’s surfaces are wet, muddy, or icy. The surface is extremely slippery under those
     conditions. Likewise, operators and occupants should remain alert to the position of weapons and other metal
     projections. Ensuring items inside the vehicle are secured in accordance with the load plan to prevent them
     from becoming projectiles in the event of a rollover or collision will also greatly reduce chances of injury.
     Adhering to a load plan also provides Soldiers the ability to easily locate and identify mission-essential items
     during limited visibility.

BLIND SPOTS

     2-13. Most tracked vehicles have blind spots where the turret or hull blocks the forward or peripheral vision
     of the driver preventing them from seeing objects on the ground. The track commander and other
     crewmembers must help the driver identify objects in the driver’s blind spot. The driver should anticipate
     objects that may fall into their blind spot as they are approached. When in doubt, the VC should use a ground
     guide to assist the driver.

LOSS OF CONTROL

     2-14. Driving too fast for road conditions is one of the leading causes for loss of control in tracked vehicles.
     If the driver loses control of the vehicle, they must take immediate steps to regain control of the vehicle. The
     driver must release the accelerator, avoid applying the brakes, and let the vehicle coast to a stop. If the vehicle
     is sliding, the driver must steer in the direction of the skid to regain control of the vehicle.

ROLLOVERS

     2-15. Although tracked combat vehicles generally tend to be stable due to their wide profile, operations still
     require vigilance to prevent rollovers. There are three rollover types (fall-initiated, maneuver-initiated, and
     impact-initiated) and fall-initiated rollovers are by far the most common, generally due to poor road
     conditions. Fall-initiated, rather than maneuver, are likely when operating along roads near ditches, bridges,
     and culverts incapable of handling the vehicle’s weight. Fall-initiated rollovers usually occur on unimproved
     roads, possibly near bodies of water where the road shoulders are soft. Studies conducted by the Army have
     found that rollovers in the Bradley fighting vehicle (BFV) family of vehicles were often caused by the BFV’s
     high profile, drivers operating at unsafe speeds, environmental conditions, and lack of platform expertise.

     2-16. The safest place for a crew during a rollover is inside the vehicle. If a tracked vehicle is about to
     rollover, the driver or another crewmember must announce ROLLOVER, ROLLOVER, ROLLOVER!
     across the vehicle’s intercom system so the rest of the crew can drop inside the vehicle and assume a safe
     position by bracing themselves inside the vehicle. Drivers must lower their seat and brace themselves inside
     the vehicle. Crews must practice rollover procedures frequently so that they become second nature in the
     event of a vehicle rollover.

     2-17. Leaders incorporate the potential for rollovers into risk assessments by assessing the route, vehicle
     speed, vehicle limitations, and the environment along the route by—

          • Being alert and using caution on roads close to bodies of water.
          • Allowing greater clearance when traveling along the edge of a road.
          • Considering the probability of the road surface collapsing and pitching the vehicle.
          • Ensuring that hazards are briefed to operators before the mission and considering options for

                alternate or bypass routes.

CREW EVACUATION DRILLS

     2-18. Instructors often overlook crew evacuation drills during training. The probability of injuries is reduced
     significantly if crews practice proper evacuation techniques in different conditions and in response to
     different situations. Vehicle technical manuals and training support packages (TSPs) provide emergency
     evacuation procedures, which should be incorporated into driver and crew training programs. Performing
     emergency evacuation procedures will be rehearsed prior to any vehicle movement.

ACCIDENT PREVENTION

     2-19. To prevent accidents, operators must—
          • Adjust speed and interval to allow for the condition of road surfaces and the environment.
          • Notify the VC when a lack of sleep or fatigue is becoming a danger.
          • Employ proper techniques to prevent or recover from a skid.
          • Slow down after dropping off the edge of the roadway before pulling back onto the pavement.
          • Ensure vehicles have been safety inspected and maintained.
          • Use the recommended pumping action in emergencies instead of locking the brakes.
          • Allow for the added force of the weight of the vehicle when quick stops are necessary.
          • Come to a complete stop and downshift at the crest of hills or steep grades to use the engine as a
                braking action to control speed.
          • Know the distance required for braking at various speeds to make emergency stops safely, which is
                especially important for vehicles towing or moving heavy loads.
          • React and brake to maintain interval with lead vehicles, for instance:
                 The driver ahead has already reacted to something.
                 The follow-on vehicles must slow down or stop in the remaining distance.
          • Inspect beneath vehicles for sleeping personnel after rest stops.
          • Use tow bars rather than cables to move disabled vehicles on roads, but if tow cables are needed,
                use a third tracked vehicle of equal weight or heavier as a braking vehicle.
          • Inspect personnel heaters to avoid carbon monoxide poisoning.
          • Refer to the vehicle’s technical manual for specific instructions or safety measures concerning the
                recovery and towing of a tracked combat vehicle.

FIRE PREVENTION

     2-20. All crewmembers and vehicle occupants must be thoroughly knowledgeable on all fire related
     emergency procedures for their platform. These procedures should be rehearsed and practiced prior to vehicle
     movement. Flammables and miscellaneous items should not be stored in the vehicle. Leaders should establish
     simple rules or an SOP to help prevent fires on vehicles.

     2-21. Crewmembers must clean up all fuel spills immediately, using only authorized cleaning agents. Leaders
     must brief vehicle crews on the type and locations of the spill kits provided to their unit.

     2-22. All tracked combat vehicles are equipped with fire extinguishers, both fixed and portable.
     Crewmembers must know how and when to use them. To ensure fire extinguishers are ready for instant use,
     crewmembers periodically weigh or check them to determine operability and replace them if necessary.

     2-23. Fixed fire extinguishers that require only the action of a trip handle or sensor to operate are installed
     on vehicles to cover areas where fires are most likely to start. They must be inspected during preventive
     maintenance checks and services (PMCS). Drivers must not start the engine of the tracked vehicle if the fixed
     fire extinguishers are inoperable or if they have been removed for maintenance.

     2-24. Portable extinguishers must be manned whenever the vehicle is being refueled. Portable fire
     extinguishers are inspected during the PMCS. If they are inoperable, they should be replaced or refilled before
     the vehicle is started.

REACT TO FIRE ON THE VEHICLE

     2-25. The first crewmember to identify that a fire has broken out on the vehicle alerts the rest of the crew.
     The driver and crew execute the actions for responding to a fire in the engine compartment or the crew
     compartment of the vehicle. Actions in response to a fire on a tracked vehicle can be found in the driver’s
     TSPs for specific vehicles and the vehicle’s technical manuals.

ADDITIONAL VEHICLE TRAINING

     2-26. Depending on the vehicle type, there may be other areas that require additional training and
     consideration. Vehicle heaters can develop leaks and are susceptible to fire. Ammunition racks, if equipped,
     also require additional awareness and procedures that crewmembers must be proficient with in the event there
     is an accident. All vehicle specific equipment and procedures must be covered during driver’s training and
     reinforced during subsequent training events.

GROUND GUIDES

     2-27. Ground guides are required for tracked vehicles to be moved safely. Leaders must ensure all
     crewmembers and drivers can recognize standard hand-and-arm and flashlight signals before guiding or
     driving tracked vehicles. Drivers and ground guides must know and observe the following rules:

          • Ground guides are required when a tracked vehicle is moving in a confined or congested area, during
                limited visibility, or if the driver is in doubt about adequate clearance, for example, on narrow
                bridges or in passages with low overhead clearances.

          • Ground guides should never stand in front of a vehicle when the engine is running but should stand
                beside the right or left fender when talking to or directing the driver.

          • Ground guides must be used in cantonments, bivouac sites, and parking areas.
          • Ground guides must never run in front of vehicles or walk backwards while guiding vehicles.
          • Ground guides should use flashlights with colored filters when moving vehicles at night.
          • Ground guides should walk approximately nine meters in front of and to the left of the vehicle’s left

                track to observe traffic to the front and rear of the vehicle (the ground guide is the correct distance
                from the tracked vehicle when the driver can see the ground guide’s feet).
          • Two ground guides must be used any time a tracked vehicle is being moved in reverse, and the rear
                ground guide must always be visible to the front ground guide.
          • Should the driver lose sight of the signal, or if there is any question about the signal from the ground
                guide, the driver stops until the signal is visible, or the confusion is eliminated.
          • The front ground guide immediately signals the driver to stop if the front ground guide is unable to
                see or identify the signals coming from the rear ground guide.
          • When a vehicle arrives at a night parking area (other than the occupation of an assembly area as a
                part of a tactical operation), a ground guide dismounts and establishes contact with the guard on
                duty:
                 The guide and the driver must understand where the sleeping area is and where to park the

                     vehicle.
                 The ground guide must search the area for people sleeping on the ground where the vehicle is

                     to park before moving the vehicle into the parking area.
                 As a minimum, blackout drive must be used, and the service drive turned on if conditions

                     permit.
          • All tactical sleeping areas must be marked with a chemical light or flashlight and have a guard

                equipped with night vision goggles:
                 The guard must hand carry a flashlight or chemical light to signal or guide vehicles.
                 Guards must be briefed on their duties and on what actions should be taken when a vehicle

                     drives into the bivouac or assembly area.
                 The guard must carry or have readily accessible, a loud, distinct, and immediately audible

                     warning device in case a vehicle enters the designated sleeping area.

VEHICLE RECOVERY OPERATIONS

     2-29. Vehicle recovery is difficult and time consuming. ATP 4-31 explains in detail the various techniques
     and methods of vehicle recovery. Vehicle self-recovery is a critical and perishable skill. All drivers and crews
     should practice vehicle self-recovery on a regular basis to maintain proficiency. It is important to recognize
     that vehicle recovery consists of three categories: Self-recovery (in which a vehicle recovers itself under its
     own power using available equipment), like-vehicle recovery (where a like vehicle is used for recovery), and
     dedicated recovery (where unit recovery assets are utilized for the recovery effort).

     2-30. Self-recovery is a skill in which tracked vehicle crews need to be proficient because of the limited
     amounts of dedicated recovery assets assigned to units. If environmental factors cause a vehicle to become
     immobilized, for example mired in wet, loose soil or high centered (bellied) on a rock or stump, the crew can
     assess the situation and determine if self-recovery is possible. When considering self-recovery operations,
     crews must consider the tactical situation and whether self-recovery can be completed safely. Even though
     most tracked vehicles lack winching equipment, self-recovery of tracked vehicles can still be accomplished
     using the methods described in ATP 4-31. Vehicle self-recovery training, when incorporated into unit drivers
     training programs, allows units to maximize the limited recovery assets organic to Armored formations by
     giving crews the skills needed to self-recover.

     2-31. When the equipment has a mechanical failure, the operator or crew uses the equipment’s technical
     manual to perform troubleshooting procedures with the tools available in the basic issue items and additional
     authorized list or on-vehicle equipment. When self-recovery fails, the operator or crew can request assistance
     from available like vehicles or unit recovery assets.

SECTION II – USUAL DRIVING CONDITIONS

OPERATING SPEEDS

     2-53. Most operations will not require use of all the speed the tracked vehicle can deliver. Ultimately, the
     unit commander or the VC determines and sets the rate of movement based on a thorough understanding of
     METT-TC (I) mission, enemy, terrain and weather, troops and support available, time available, civil
     considerations, and informational considerations. Operating at high speed in a tactical environment will get
     vehicle crews into a dangerous situation faster than can be countered or reacted upon. Some things to keep
     in mind when operating the tracked combat vehicles are:

          • Driving at high speed does not increase safety or security during combat operations. Higher speeds
                reduce driver reaction time, extend stopping distances, and increase roll rates that can lead to tip
                over or rollover.

          • Traveling at high speeds significantly decreases the ability to visually scan the road surface
                accurately.

          • Slow speeds reduce the possibility of an accident, tip, or rollover.

ACCELERATION AND BRAKING

     2-54. Emergency stopping procedures in response to the loss of brakes, steering, or engine power is as
     follows:

          • Driver notifies the VC that the brakes, steering, or engine power have malfunctioned.
          • Driver moves the gear select to N (neutral). Driver centers the steering column or laterals. Driver

                lets the vehicle coast to a stop.
          • Driver sets the parking brake if the vehicle has one.
          • Driver shuts down the engine once the vehicle has stopped.
          • VC notifies the chain of command.

     2-55. In the event the driver and the VC lose communications, the driver immediately brings the vehicle to a
     halt and does not move the vehicle until communications have been restored.

BRIDGES AND SOFT SOIL

     2-56. Tracked combat vehicle operators along with the VC must make sure that bridge classifications are for
     tracked combat vehicles. Pre-mission planning should include identifying bypass or alternate routes. Use
     extreme caution when approaching bridges that are unrated. Mission commanders must seek guidance from
     combat engineers to determine if the bridge is safe to cross before the execution phase of the mission.

     2-57. Bridges may only be rated for the weight of a single tracked combat vehicle. In this case, leaders must—
          • Plan for an interval that allows only one vehicle to cross the bridge at a time.
          • Post ground guides from lead vehicle or use lead vehicle to block traffic, dependent on tactical
                situation.
          • Pick up ground guides with trail vehicle, if used.

     2-58. Soft soil poses a risk to the tracked combat vehicles due to their high ground pressure. As a result of
     the high ground pressure associated with tracked combat vehicle there is a greater risk of sinking or bogging
     down in soft soil. Operators must avoid moving too close to the edges of roads that may collapse and cause
     the vehicle to tip over.

SPEED CONTROL

     2-59. Elements in a column of any length may simultaneously encounter many different types of routes and
     obstacles. This causes different parts of the column to move at varying speeds at the same time. To increase
     safety and reduce column whipping, the movement or march order should give march speed, vehicle interval,
     and maximum catch-up speed.

EMERGENCY OR SUDDEN BRAKING

     2-60. The VC alerts the driver that the vehicle needs to stop suddenly. The VC cues this action by telling the
     driver, STOP, STOP, STOP! in rapid succession. Upon hearing the cue, the driver executes emergency or
     sudden braking procedures.

     2-61. The driver uses the recommended pumping action to prevent locking the brakes on the vehicle. During
     drivers training, after stopping the vehicle, the VC should have the driver reflect on the speed the vehicle was
     going, the time it took the vehicle to come to a complete stop, and the distance it took the vehicle to stop.
     Having the driver identify the speed, time, and distance involved in an emergency stop provides them with a
     frame of reference from which to draw.

VEHICLE HEIGHT

     2-62. Tracked combat vehicle drivers are generally unable to determine or see bridge information from their
     position in the vehicle. It is incumbent on the VC to be cognizant and aware of the vehicle’s position on the
     route and ensure that the vehicle can pass under obstacles safely. With training and time, drivers may develop
     a general idea of when a vehicle may or may not safely fit beneath a bridge without the assistance of the VC.
     However, it is still the primary responsibility of the VC to ensure a tracked combat vehicle can safely clear a
     bridge.

CHEMICAL, BIOLOGICAL, RADIOLOGICAL, AND NUCLEAR

     2-76. Chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear (CBRN) collective protection systems are organic

     collective protection systems have gas particulate filter units that remove CBRN contaminants and blowers
     which provide filtered air to crewmembers protective masks. Some provide overpressure which provides
     further protection in a CBRN environment.

     2-77. Units will train on the aforementioned systems and capabilities as part of the drivers training program.
     Proficiency should be attained in areas such as system capabilities, maintenance, operation, and use. A
     thorough understanding of not only the systems but the difficulties and levels of degradation relating to
     dexterity and vision while wearing protective equipment and operating a tracked vehicle must be reached.
     (See ATP 3-11.32 for additional information on CBRN collective protection systems in tracked combat
     vehicles.)

SECTION IV – EMERGENCY PROCEDURES

     2-78. Tracked vehicle emergency procedures must be incorporated into the unit’s driver training program
     and should be integrated into other training events such as the unit’s gunnery training. Emergency procedures
     will be conducted in accordance with platform specific technical manuals and should adhere to the following:

          • React to a vehicle fire must be practiced and assessed based on platform capabilities and by the type
                of fire (engine compartment, turret, hull, and so forth).

          • Operating the vehicle’s portable fire extinguishers and the vehicle automatic fire extinguishing
                systems in manual mode must be incorporated into the training event.

          • Reacting to multiple types of rollovers and the effects of the rollover on normal operation of vehicle
                components, such as hatches and the evacuation techniques that may differ, will be integrated into
                the emergency procedure portion of driver training and testing.

          • Removing injured personnel may be combined with the rollover portion of the training and
                assessment.

     2-79. The various platforms that are discussed in this publication have additional capabilities that require
     proficiency in the procedures to execute supplementary emergency procedures. These procedures vary by
     platform but may consist of emergency shutdown of major components, electrical systems, and operation in
     degraded modes. Vehicle technical manuals should be fully reviewed during analysis and design of unit
     driver training. Any additional emergency procedures that are specific to a vehicle or platform variant should
     be merged into the training.

                                                 Chapter 3

           Driver Selection, Training, and Supervision

    Chapter 3 outlines a system for identifying, selecting, and qualifying trainers and drivers (see
    AR 600-55 for more information). The Army vehicle driver’s contribution to combat efficiency
    is not always recognized or appreciated. Soldier performance is a critical factor in keeping a
    vehicle in operation. Even the best designed vehicles maintained by the best Army operators
    and mechanics cannot compensate for poor driving practices. Poor driver training and practices
    can result in accidents, injury to personnel, and damage to equipment.

SECTION I – PREPARATION FOR DRIVER TRAINING

     3-1. Effective driver training depends on careful planning and thorough instruction. Before instruction
     begins, the commander makes a careful and complete estimate of the driver training requirement. Based on
     this estimate and in accordance with applicable unit SOPs, installation policies, and AR 600-55 the
     commander or civilian branch chief will—

          • Develop plans and schedules.
          • Select and train license instructors (LIs) and license examiners (LEs).
          • Ensure adequate facilities and equipment are available.
          • Ensure the unit has a current file of all Army, post, and unit publications and policies that pertain to

                driver training.
          • Inform students of all regulations during the first phase of training.

     3-2. Time permitting, unit leaders or trainers should identify students well in advance of training and provide
     them with the unit SOP and driver training regulations to study on their own prior to actual training.

ESTIMATE OF DRIVER TRAINING SITUATION

     3-3. When preparing to conduct a driver training program, the commander estimates the driver training
     situation by answering these questions:

          • How many personnel require qualification (monthly, annually)?
          • How many previously licensed drivers need verification or recertification?
          • What are the capabilities and general experiences of new drivers who need qualification?
          • What are the seasonal requirements for unit location?
          • How much time is available?
          • What type of tracked-vehicle accidents has the unit experienced?
          • How many licensed instructors are available?
          • Will the training be part of unit in processing?
          • What special training do the instructors require?
          • What facilities, supplies, and equipment (including training aids, vehicles, and driving ranges with

                varied terrain) are available?
          • Does the unit have any major training events scheduled?

     3-4. The commander analyzes the answers to help develop and organize an effective plan to carry out the
     training. In organizing the training program, the commander analyzes the appropriate TSP to determine the
     following:

          • The number of instructors who need training and a schedule for their instruction.
          • The duties and responsibilities of each instructor.
          • The number of operators who require training or retraining and a schedule for their instruction.
          • How students will be grouped and how each group will be rotated.
          • What facilities, supplies, and equipment are needed and how to get them ready.
          • The standards for training required by the appropriate TSP and your unit’s needs.

     3-14. The commander develops the training strategy after assessing the strengths and weaknesses of the unit.

     and correcting its deficiencies. This requires an integrated training strategy. VCs are required to be proficient
     with all tasks performed on the vehicle, to include tasks associated with driving the vehicle. An assignment
     as the driver of a tracked combat vehicle is likely to be the initial assignment a crewmember takes when they
     are assigned to the tracked combat vehicle crew. Because driving the tracked combat vehicle is the initial
     crew assignment, all other crewmembers should have experience driving and maintaining the vehicle and
     can therefore advise and assist drivers as needed.

          • Day and night driving techniques.
          • Crew drills.
          • Emergency procedures.

     3-16. Using TADSS simulates multiple environments in which the driver can gain proficiency with driving
     and coordination skills without the added cost of using the actual vehicle. Training can also include other
     driver tasks, such as ground guide, convoy operations, and driving under unusual conditions and in
     complicated terrain. Commanders should incorporate drivers training using simulation systems prior to
     executing physical drivers training if the situation and environment allow it.

    number of days for each training event should be determined by vehicle platform and proper
    training for both the usual and unusual driving conditions. Additional training materials for the
    tracked combat vehicle family of vehicles can be found at the Unit Training Assistance
    Program website or Army Publishing Directorate website. These sites should also be reviewed
    prior to the development of drivers training to ensure that the most recent training materials
    available are being utilized for drivers training.

Report).
2. Technical manuals and DA forms associated with operating a tracked combat vehicle.

4. Operate a tracked combat vehicle under usual conditions.
5. Operate a tracked combat vehicle under unusual conditions.

Appendix C

1. Purpose: DD Form 518 is completed by the operator and given to any persons involved in an accident with
all the information that they require from you.

2. General Instructions:
          a. Fill this form out at the scene of an accident or as promptly as possible and give it to the person
                directly concerned.
          b. If an accident involves a parked vehicle and the person concerned is not present, place the
                DD Form 518 inside the vehicle or secure it on the windshield.
          c. Notify local authorities and stand by the scene of the accident until they arrive.

3. Completing DD Form 518:
          a. Any correspondence regarding the accident should be addressed to: Commanding General, your
                duty station, ATTN: Claims Office.
          b. Date of accident. Fill in the date.
          c. Make and type of vehicle. Identify your vehicle - for example, “M915 14 Ton 6x4.”
          d. Registration number of vehicle. Write the number of your vehicle.
          e. Driver. Put your last name, then your first name and middle initial.
          f. Social Security number (disclosure of SSN is voluntary).
          g. Grade. Your rank.
          h. Organization. Name of organization to which you are assigned.

Check on Learning: Conduct a Check on Learning

          1. What is the form number for the accident investigation card?
          2. What is the purpose of the accident investigation card?
          3. If the accident occurs with a parked vehicle, where do you place the accident investigation card?
          4. What entries are required to be filled out by the vehicle operator on a DD Form 518?

Review Summary: Conduct a Summary Review, allow the students to ask any questions that they have.

SF 91:

1. Purpose: Operator’s report of motor vehicle accident. It protects both you and the government against claims
and exaggerations.

2. General Instructions:
          a. Always stop and investigate any accident in which you are involved. The only possible exception

understands the appropriate technical manual.

                                                Appendix E

                         Drivers Training Testing

    Appendix E provides recommendations for the physical standards and design of the drivers
    training course. If an obstacle is not available a substitution may be used in accordance with
    AR 600-55. Commanders may modify or alter recommended designs and obstacles to suit their
    unit driver’s training program or available facilities, but courses must allow drivers to complete
    the tasks listed in the DA Form 7932 (Army Tracked Vehicle [ATV] Operators Evaluation
    Score Sheet).

OVERVIEW

     E-1. Incorporating situations simulating what drivers encounter while assigned as tracked combat vehicle
     drivers improves their ability to operate and maneuver their vehicles and prepares them to command these
     vehicles later in their careers. Encountering terrain features requiring the driver to decide whether the vehicle
     can negotiate obstacles encountered in a field environment gives them valuable knowledge of the capabilities
     and limitations of the vehicles. Drivers should encounter and negotiate various urban terrain obstacles as
     well, for instance a bridge (simulated) the vehicle must cross and an overpass or wire they may have to go
     under (simulated). Gaining this knowledge reduces accidents or injuries during future operations in real-
     world environments. Instructors should conduct these the tasks during the day as well as during periods of
     limited visibility using night vision devices.

DRIVERS TRAINING COURSES (USUAL/UNUSUAL/EMERGENCY
CONDITIONS)

     E-2. The tasks identified in tables E-1 through E-3 on page 74 are part of a driver’s training program.
     Commanders and master drivers can use the template to guide them in the development of their drivers
     training course. Prior to the execution of training trainers must consult vehicle technical manuals to ensure
     that all tasks’ drivers will complete on the course are within the safe operating parameters of the vehicles
     being operated. If any portion of the course presents unsafe conditions training must be halted until the safety
     hazard has been addressed.
