                                 Preface

ATP 2-01.4 provides foundational guidance for executing Army intelligence support to targeting at echelons
corps and below, which are integrated within a theater’s targeting process. Echelons above corps elements
use joint doctrine (see JP 3-60).

This publication focuses on intelligence professionals conducting intelligence support to Army targeting
across all intelligence disciplines. It complements and expands on intelligence support to Army targeting
discussions in FM 3-60. This publication includes techniques developed from lessons learned, current best
practices, and existing doctrine.

The principal audience for ATP 2-01.4 includes commanders, intelligence professionals, and operational staff
supporting the targeting process at echelons corps and below. ATP 2-01.4 also serves as a reference for
military personnel developing doctrine, institutional and unit training, materiel and force structure, and
standard operating procedures for intelligence activities that support targeting.

To understand the doctrine in ATP 2-01.4, readers must understand the following:

     • Intelligence doctrine described in ADP 2-0 and FM 2-0.
     • Collection management described in ATP 2-01.
     • Intelligence preparation of the operational environment (IPOE) described in ATP 2-01.3.
     • Intelligence analysis described in ATP 2-33.4.
     • Operational doctrine described in ADP 3-0 and FM 3-0.
     • Army targeting described in FM 3-60.
     • Corps and division intelligence techniques described in ATP 2-19.3.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates, with assistance from their servicing judge advocates, ensure their
decisions and actions comply with applicable U.S. laws and policy, to include but not limited to Executive

military orders, including fragmentary orders; and international treaties. Commanders at all levels ensure their

incorporates several all-source intelligence analysis tasks, all aspects of collection management, and every

    SECTION I – INTELLIGENCE

     1-1. Intelligence is 1. The product resulting from the collection, processing, integration, evaluation, analysis,
     and interpretation of available information concerning foreign nations, hostile or potentially hostile forces or
     elements, or areas of actual or potential operations. 2. The activities that result in the product. 3. The
     organizations conducting such activities (JP 2-0). Intelligence drives operations and operations enable
     intelligence.

     1-2. Intelligence is a function, a product, and a process that together enables the Army to conduct (plan,
     prepare, execute, and assess) operations. The purpose of intelligence (the function) is to provide commanders
     and staffs with timely, accurate, relevant, predictive, and tailored intelligence (the product) about the threat
     and other aspects of the operational environment (OE). Intelligence (the process) supports the conduct of
     operations. (FM 2-0 provides doctrine to assist in understanding Army intelligence and building proficiency
     in intelligence fundamentals.)

ARMY INTELLIGENCE

     1-3. Intelligence is networked, synchronized, and collaborative across the intelligence enterprise. The
     Army’s contribution to the intelligence enterprise is the intelligence warfighting function—the related tasks
     and systems that facilitate understanding the enemy, terrain, weather, civil considerations, and other
     significant aspects of the operational environment (ADP 3-0). Other significant aspects of the OE include the
     operational variables not covered by the mission variables of enemy, terrain and weather, and civil
     considerations, and other aspects depending on the nature of the operation. (See FM 2-0.)

     1-4. The intelligence warfighting function supports operations through a broad range of supporting doctrinal
     tasks, referred to as intelligence warfighting function tasks (IWFTs)—one of which is provide intelligence
     support to targeting. Army units and organizations perform these tasks via the intelligence process, shown
     in figure 1-1 on page 2, by employing intelligence capabilities (see appendix A) to support the commander
     and staff. (FM 2-0 provides doctrine on the intelligence warfighting function and the intelligence process.)

FIGHTING FOR INTELLIGENCE

     1-5. Fighting for intelligence refers to the challenge to provide effective and flexible intelligence support
     against a determined and adaptive threat—whether a peer threat or terrorist cell. This challenge also affects
     targeting as intelligence provides the commander and staff with the information and intelligence needed to
     support targeting actions. Fighting for intelligence spans the Army strategic contexts—competition below
     armed conflict, crisis, and armed conflict. (FM 3-0 and FM 2-0 provide doctrine on the Army strategic
     contexts and how intelligence relates to them, respectively.)

     1-6. Fighting for intelligence is most challenging during large-scale combat operations as the threat can apply
     operational reach, contest friendly deployments, leverage highly lethal and effective nonlethal capabilities
     across multiple domains (land, maritime, air, space, and cyberspace), and influence friendly forces across
     multiple dimensions (human, information, and physical). Maneuver units, not just military intelligence (MI)
     units, must be prepared to fight for intelligence to gain information against enemy formations, a range of
     sophisticated threat capabilities, and many unknown conditions within the OE. The commander and staff will
     have to make difficult choices in terms of accepting risk to Soldiers conducting information collection.
     Therefore, staff integration is crucial; the staff must collaborate to overcome challenges and mitigate
     information collection capability risks and system limitations by developing an integrated information collection
     plan synchronized and nested across echelons. (FM 2-0 provides doctrine on fighting for intelligence.)

                           Intelligence and Targeting

    SECTION II – TARGETING

     1-7. Targeting is the process of selecting and prioritizing targets and matching the appropriate response to
     them, considering operational requirements and capabilities (JP 3-0). Targeting, as an integrating process,
     assists in integrating and synchronizing the fires warfighting function—the related tasks and systems that
     create and converge effects in all domains against the threat to enable actions across the range of military
     operations (ADP 3-0)—with other Army warfighting functions and joint functions. (See FM 5-0 and
     ADP 3-19 for doctrine on the integrating processes and fires warfighting function, respectively.)

     1-8. A target is an entity that performs a function for the adversary or enemy considered for possible
     engagement (JP 3-60). A target’s importance is determined by its potential contribution to achieving the
     commander’s objectives or otherwise accomplishing assigned tasks or reaching an effect. Targets are
     continuously refined or adjusted as an operation unfolds.

     1-9. Targets nominated for engagement may be grouped in the following entity types: facility, individual,
     virtual, equipment, and organization (FIVE-O). (See CJCSI 3370.01D.) A single entity may be associated with
     several target systems and critical to one or more of those systems. An entity may contain functions or elements
     that are part of other target systems. A target element is specific features of a target entity that enable it to
     function (JP 3-60).

     1-10. The following provides a brief description of each entity type (see FM 3-60 for a full description):
          • Facility is a physical structure, group of structures, or area that performs one or more functions
                that contribute to a threat capability.
          • Individual is a person who provides one or more functions that contribute to a threat capability.
          • Virtual is a collection of programs, files, or codes that depend on each other to perform a function
                for the threat.
          • Equipment is an object comprising any number of devices and components, integrated and
                codependent, upon which the engagement of one component results in an effect upon the whole.
          • Organization is a group or unit that provides a function that contributes to a threat capability.

     1-11. Within the OE, targets exist in a—
          • Fixed state. The target is unable to move.
          • Stationary state. The target can move but must be in a static location from which to operate.
          • Mobile state. The target operates on the move or requires very limited time to setup and execute
                its intended function.

TARGETING PRINCIPLES

     1-12. Targeting is based on the enemy’s assets, which can limit the friendly scheme of maneuver and tactical
     plans. Therefore, its purpose is to disrupt, delay, degrade, or limit threat interference with friendly courses of
     action (COAs). The targeting process assesses the benefits and costs of engaging various targets to achieve
     the desired end state. Every target must contribute to attaining the commander’s objectives. Adhering to the
     four targeting principles—focused, effects-based, interdisciplinary, and systematic—should increase the
     probability of creating desired effects while diminishing undesired or adverse collateral effects:

          • Focused. Targeting focuses on understanding the commander’s intent and guidance to achieve the
                commander’s objectives.

          • Effects-based. Targeting seeks to create specific desired effects through lethal and nonlethal
                actions or capabilities.

          • Interdisciplinary. Targeting encompasses many disciplines and requires participation from many
                staff elements and components.

          • Systematic. Targeting creates effects systematically. If desired effects are not created, targets may
                be considered again in the process, or operations may have to be modified.

     1-13. Knowing the commander’s intent assists in clarifying the intelligence support to the targeting effort.
     The commander’s guidance on using lethal and/or nonlethal capabilities is expressed to ensure the desired
     effects. Continuous intelligence updates assist the commander in creating various windows of opportunity
     and desired effects if the threat’s actions are predicted.

TARGETING CATEGORIES

     1-15. To target the enemy effectively, friendly forces use deliberate and dynamic targeting. Deliberate
     targeting generally allows for more time and a larger resource pool from which to leverage against a target.
     Dynamic targeting is generally restricted by time, resources available, and command priorities. (See
     FM 3-60.) Each targeting category has two subcategories, as shown in figure 1-2.

                                             Figure 1-2. Targeting categories

DELIBERATE TARGETING

     1-16. Deliberate targeting prosecutes planned targets assessed to be in the unit’s assigned area for future
     operations that have a time horizon of days or weeks out. Planned targets can range from targets on target
     lists in the applicable plan or order to targets detected in enough time for placement on the air tasking order
     or integrated tasking order, mission type orders, or fire support plans.

     1-17. There are two types of planned targets:
          • Scheduled targets exist in the area of operations (AO) and are located in enough time so fires or
                other actions upon them are identified for engagement at a specific, planned time.
          • On-call targets have planned actions but not for a specific delivery time. The commander expects
                to locate these targets in enough time to execute planned actions.

DYNAMIC TARGETING

     1-18. Dynamic targeting is targeting that prosecutes targets identified too late or not selected for action in time
     to be included in deliberate targeting (JP 3-60). Dynamic targeting prosecutes targets of opportunity outside the
     24-hour integrated target order/air tasking order cycle and focuses on the current battle with a time horizon of
     hours. The commander approves targets of opportunity during the current operations’ planning and execution.

     1-19. There are two types of targets of opportunity:
          • Unplanned targets are known to exist in the AO, but no action has been planned against them.
                The target may not have been detected or located in enough time to meet planning deadlines, or
                the target may have been located but not previously considered important enough to engage.
          • Unanticipated targets are unknown or not expected to exist in the AO.

     1-20. Targets of opportunity may present themselves at any time and location on the battlefield; they are
     processed the same as planned targets. Targets of opportunity are first evaluated to determine when, or if,
     they should be engaged. The decision to engage them follows the rules of engagement and attack guidance
     and is based on factors, including but not limited to the following:

TARGET CONSIDERATIONS

     1-21. The following targets require special consideration due to the threat they pose, potential consequences
     of engaging them, and their priority on the targeting list:

          • Time-sensitive target (TST).
          • Component critical target.
          • Sensitive target.

Time-Sensitive Target

     1-22. Time-sensitive target is a joint force commander-validated target or set of targets requiring immediate
     response because it is a highly lucrative, fleeting target of opportunity or it poses (or will soon pose) a danger
     to friendly forces (JP 3-60). A TST is of such high priority—as it impinges on accomplishing the joint force
     commander’s (JFC’s) mission and objectives, or it presents such a significant strategic or operational threat
     to friendly forces or allies—that the JFC dedicates intelligence collection and attack assets to ensure success.

Component Critical Target

     1-23. An echelon commander designated as the land component commander identifies and nominates
     component critical targets. A Service component commander may designate high-priority targets that present
     significant risks to, or opportunities for, component forces and missions. These are generally targets that the
     Service component commander has nominated to the JFC TST list but were not approved as TSTs. These
     targets, known as component critical targets, may require dynamic execution with expedited cross-
     component coordination and assistance. JFC and Service component commanders should clearly designate
     these targets before executing military operations. Component critical targets should receive the highest
     priority possible, just below targets on the JFC TST list.

                                         Army Critical Targets

          Army critical targets are those high-priority targets approved by the component
          commander as crucial to the scheme of operations. These targets require immediate
          engagement because they pose (or will soon pose) a significant threat to friendly
          forces. Generally, Army critical targets have been nominated for consideration as TSTs
          but do not meet the TST criteria. However, they may still require timely cross-
          component coordination to facilitate synchronized execution with other targets. Army
          Service component commanders should identify Army critical targets within their
          targeting cycle, provide clear guidance to develop and approve the appropriate asset
          priority, and, when required, provide rapid cross-component coordination to minimize
          confusion and facilitate execution.

Sensitive Target

     1-24. Certain targets require special care or caution because failure to engage them or engaging them improperly
     could cause adverse consequences. Some examples of targets requiring special care or caution include—

          • Leader targets (high-value individuals [HVIs]) that must be handled sensitively due to potential
                political repercussions.

          • Targets located in areas with a high risk of collateral damage.
          • Weapons of mass destruction (WMD) facilities where an attack can lead to long-term

                environmental damage.

THE ARMY TARGETING PROCESS

     1-26. Targeting is a commander-driven process that can be conducted across a range of military operations.
     As an integrative process, targeting must adapt to changing circumstances and influence decisions, resource
     allocation/distribution, and critical staff actions. Army forces at each echelon, including supporting
     commands and combat supporting agencies, must understand the Army targeting process.

     1-27. The Army targeting process applies the decide, detect, deliver, and assess (D3A) methodology. (See
     figure 1-3.) The process is integrated with the operations process, joint targeting process (see appendix B),
     and the military decision-making process (MDMP). Using the D3A methodology as a foundation, the fire
     support element (FSE)—

          • Recommends targeting guidance to the commander.
          • Develops targets.
          • Selects targets for attack.
          • Coordinates, integrates, and assigns allocated joint, interagency, multinational, and organic assets

                to specific targets and target systems.

    Figure 1-3. Army targeting process: decide, detect, deliver, and assess methodology

   1-28. The Army targeting process is a complex, multidiscipline effort that requires a continuous coordinated
   effort among many elements. These collaborative elements—together referred to as the targeting team—
   include but are not limited to the fire support cell, intelligence cell, operations cell, and plans cell.
   Representatives from each of these cells are essential to a comprehensive targeting process. Other staff
   members assist the targeting team in planning and executing targeting. (See FM 3-60.)

          Note. Coordination is crucial for a successful targeting effort. Sensors and collection capabilities
          under external agencies’ control must be closely coordinated for the efficient and immediate
          reporting of fleeting or dangerous targets. Additionally, the vulnerabilities of different types of
          targets must be attacked by the appropriate means and munitions.

   1-29. The D3A methodology organizes the commander and staff’s efforts to accomplish key targeting
   requirements. It assists the commander and staff in deciding—

        • Which targets must be acquired.
        • Lethal or nonlethal engagement options.
        • Whether organic or nonorganic assets should be allocated against the target.

Note. Targeting occurs continuously throughout an operation. Corps and below units typically use

TARGET REFINEMENT

     1-31. During the Army targeting process, targets are continuously refined as intelligence is received and the
     operation evolves. During this refinement process, targets are placed on one or more target lists; for example,
     targets at the joint level can be placed on a candidate target list, joint integrated prioritized target list, and/or
     many other lists. The following are refined targets derived from targets lists:

          • High-value targets (HVTs) (any FIVE-O entity the threat commander requires for successful
                completion of the mission).

          • High-payoff targets (HPTs) (any FIVE-O entity whose loss to the enemy contributes significantly
                to the success of the friendly COA).

          • Restricted targets.

                                     No-Strike List

The no-strike list is not a target list although it is a critical part of the joint targeting
process. A no-strike list is a list of entities characterized as protected from the effects
of military operations under international law or rules of engagement (JP 3-60).
Protected entities may lose their protective status under specific situations. (CJCSI
3160.01E provides more information on no-strike lists and no-strike entities.)

     1-33. A high-payoff target is a target whose loss to the enemy will significantly contribute to the success of the

    Interrelationship Between Intelligence and Targeting

OVERVIEW

     2-1. The interrelationship between the intelligence and fires warfighting functions is integral to effective
     targeting. Commanders and staffs need timely, accurate, relevant, and predictive intelligence to support the
     targeting effort, which includes selecting, prioritizing, executing, and assessing targets. Precise intelligence
     is critical to target threat capabilities at the right time and place and to open windows of opportunity across
     multiple domains and dimensions, particularly during large-scale combat operations. The intelligence support
     to targeting effort must be prioritized, resourced, planned, and supported by a large portion of the intelligence
     architecture.

     2-2. The provide intelligence support to targeting IWFT has three subtasks:
          • Provide intelligence support to target development. (See chapter 5.)
          • Provide intelligence support to target detection. (See chapter 6.)
          • Provide intelligence support to combat assessment. (See chapter 6.)

     2-3. Characteristics that best describe intelligence support to targeting include—
          • Deliberate planning.
          • Collaboration across the intelligence enterprise.
          • Precise intelligence to target threat capabilities at the right time and place.
          • Accurate and timely assessment of military engagements.

     2-4. These combined characteristics, when well executed, open windows of opportunity to achieve positions
     of relative advantage. The effective execution of these characteristics can be especially challenging when
     locating, tracking, updating, and engaging highly rapid and mobile targets, targets in complex terrain (for
     example, subterranean, jungle, and mountainous terrain and dense urban areas), and targets across multiple
     domains and dimensions (for example, targeting threat information systems).

     2-5. Providing intelligence support to targeting occurs across most of the intelligence warfighting function.
     While all intelligence disciplines and complementary capabilities support the targeting effort, the task
     includes the work performed by more than just the intelligence targeting element. For example, the entire
     staff, led by the G-2/S-2, performs part of the provide intelligence support to targeting IWFT during the
     MDMP. The FSE coordinates with and assists the intelligence targeting element during some subtasks.
     Likewise, the entire staff, led by the fire support coordinator (FSCOORD) or fire support officer (FSO),
     performs part of this task while conducting D3A. The intelligence cell coordinates with the FSE to
     accomplish some of its supporting tasks. Each staff section, including the intelligence staff, and targeting
     working group members bring their expertise to support the targeting effort. (See appendix C for target-
     related working groups.)

             Note. Fire support coordinator is the senior field artillery commander for the theater, corps, division,
             brigade combat team who is the maneuver commander’s primary advisor to plan, coordinate, and
             integrate field artillery and fire support in the execution of assigned tasks (FM 3-09). Fire support
             officer is the operational to tactical level field artillery officer responsible for advising the supported
             commander or assisting the senior fires officer responsible for coordinating fire functions and fire
             support (FM 3-09).

PRE-MISSION ANALYSIS OF THE OPERATIONAL ENVIRONMENT

     3-1. The conduct pre-mission analysis of the OE IWFT is a continuous task driven by the commander. It is

     revised intelligence products facilitate the commander and staff’s understanding of the OE, focus information
     collection, and support the targeting effort once a mission is received. Therefore, the analytical effort must
     start as early as possible. (FM 2-0 provides detailed doctrine on pre-mission analysis of the OE.)

          • Analyzing the significant aspects of the domains and dimensions.

          • Preparing relevant mission variable information and intelligence. Intelligence overlaps directly
                with the IPOE process.
          • Completing studies.

     3-3. Intelligence officers and other staff sections begin pre-mission analysis of the OE by leveraging data,
     information, and intelligence, which is an inherent part of intelligence support to targeting.

     4-1. Intelligence preparation of the operational environment is the systematic process of analyzing the
     mission variables of enemy, terrain, weather, and civil considerations in an area of interest to determine their

             information, and intelligence; and situation development to serve as a continuous effort, with all

             assessments are necessary to maintain situational understanding of the OE in constant flux.

INTELLIGENCE REQUIREMENTS AND TARGETING

     4-4. Army intelligence staffs assist in developing requirements during the MDMP. Requirements
     development forms the foundation of the information collection plan, which also contains the targeting
     collection plan, if available. Requirements development identifies, prioritizes, and refines uncertainties about
     the OE that a unit must resolve for mission accomplishment. It ensures the appropriate collection occurs by
     specifying what must be collected, why it must be collected, where it must be collected, and when it must be
     collected and reported so a unit can successfully conduct operations. The so what of requirements
     development is ensuring the development of relevant and accurate intelligence requirements. (ATP 2-01
     provides doctrine on developing requirements.)

     4-5. An intelligence requirement is 1. Any subject, general or specific, upon which there is a need for the
     collection of information or the production of intelligence. 2. A requirement for intelligence to fill a gap in
     the command’s knowledge or understanding of the operational environment or threat forces (JP 2-0).
     Intelligence requirements primarily consist of PIRs but may also include targeting intelligence requirements
     and other intelligence requirements. After the staff determines all intelligence requirements, the commander
     must prioritize them so the staff understands the exact prioritization of all requirements. The collection
     management team and the rest of the intelligence staff manage intelligence requirements for the commander:

          • Priority intelligence requirements are the intelligence components of commander’s critical
                information requirements used to focus the employment of limited intelligence assets and
                resources against competing demands for intelligence support (JP 2-0). In Army usage, PIRs refer
                to intelligence requirements that the commander and staff need to understand the threat and other
                aspects of the OE.

          • Targeting intelligence requirements refer to those HPT-related requirements, outside of what is
                already captured in PIRs, that the commander specifies as part of the information collection effort.
                Units are not required to develop targeting intelligence requirements. However, in some situations,
                a unit may want to develop targeting intelligence requirements to conduct dedicated collection and
                focus more thoroughly on detecting and tracking HPTs and conducting BDA.

          • Other intelligence requirements refer to those commander-specified requirements that are either—
                ▪ Information requirements that do not qualify as PIRs.
                ▪ Unanticipated requirements over the course of an operation that are not characterized as PIRs.

     4-6. Once intelligence requirements are established, collection managers develop a collection management
     plan to integrate and synchronize collection assets and address intelligence requirements through proposed
     information collection tasks. Collection managers should integrate targeting assets into the collection
     management plan. Analysts use the intelligence requirements levied against these targeting assets to develop
     the targeting collection plan, which should nest within the overall information collection plan and align to
     the commander’s intelligence requirements.

WEATHER ANALYSIS CONSIDERATIONS FOR TARGETING

     4-15. Weather analysis is collecting, processing, evaluating, and interpreting relevant military aspects of
     weather; it is evaluating forecasted weather effects on operations. The G-2/S-2, in coordination with the
     USAF SWO, provides weather analysis products that primarily support the detect, deliver, and assess
     functions of the D3A methodology. Analysts should evaluate the effects of each military aspect of weather
     on enemy target activities, friendly collection assets, and friendly delivery assets. However, they should focus
     on those aspects that have the most bearing on operations and support the commander’s decision making.

             Note. It is important to provide weather updates to fire direction centers. Using precision weapons
             requires continuously updated wind speed, wind direction, barometric pressure, temperature, and
             precipitation information.

     4-16. The military aspects of weather are visibility, wind, precipitation, cloud cover, temperature, humidity,
     and atmospheric pressure (as required). They can affect collection assets’ ability to detect targets and impact
     BDA and lethal and nonlethal fires systems’ ability to achieve their desired effects on the target. Evaluating
     each weather aspect should begin with the local climatology, and analysts should refine the evaluation with
     the most current forecasts. Weather analysis has two substeps:

          • Analyze the military aspects (characteristics) of weather.
          • Evaluate the weather’s effects on military operations.

     4-17. Analysts must also consider potential space weather effects on friendly and enemy space-based
     capabilities. Solar flares or other adverse space weather may impact satellite communication links and ground
     stations, which may have cascading effects on Army ground operations. Space weather effects on satellite
     communications may have adverse effects on operations and mission success. Different solar event types can
     have different effects on equipment, and the effects may be forecasted to occur within 2 to 3 minutes or 1 to
     2 hours, or they can occur the next day. (For space weather doctrine, see FM 3-14.)

     4-21. The intelligence staff maintains civil considerations (ASCOPE) data in a file or database that organizes

     identifying restricted fire areas or no fire areas. For example, declaring a nuclear power plant as a no fire area

TARGET DEVELOPMENT

     5-4. Target development is the systematic examination of potential target systems—and their components,
     individual targets, and even elements of target—to determine the necessary type and duration of an
     engagement (JP 3-60). Target development involves the systematic analysis of threat forces and operations
     to determine HVTs, HPTs, systems, and system components for potential engagement through lethal and
     nonlethal means. Target development is a process that integrates all-source analysis (IPOE), collection
     management, and targeting functions to evaluate target systems to identify, characterize, and nominate
     targets, when engaged, and support achieving the commander’s objectives.

             Note. When supporting joint operations, CJCSI 3370.01D guidance must be adhered to on basic,
             intermediate, and advanced target development.

TARGET SYSTEM ANALYSIS

     5-5. Target system analysis is an all-source examination of potential target systems to determine relevance
     to stated objectives, military importance, and priority of attack (JP 3-60). The purpose of conducting TSA is
     to identify targeting plans that enable a commander to use targeting to create effects that accomplish
     objectives and to identify HPTs that support those strategies. Through TSA, considerations can be established
     during the evaluation of a target’s value or payoff and how much its engagement will contribute to the
     commander’s objective. TSA is a joint process used during joint targeting and target development to produce
     TSA products to support detailed targeting within the joint targeting cycle. In some situations, TSA products
     at the joint level may be inputs that assist the Army in analyzing threat systems, performing IPOE, or
     conducting TVA.

             Note. TSA is not an IWFT. It creates a requirement for the intelligence warfighting function to
             provide products and intelligence to enable the commander and staff to create targeting strategies
             to produce desired effects. The analyze threat systems IWFT is not conducted to joint standards
             and is not captured in the same format as TSA and target development at the joint level. Army
             forces working as part of or supporting the joint force conduct joint TSA and target development
             to joint standards.

ANALYZE THREAT SYSTEMS

     5-7. A system is a functionally, physically, and/or behaviorally related group of regularly interacting or
     interdependent elements that form a unified whole (JP 3-0). A system perspective provides a comprehensive,
     holistic view of the fundamental elements (nodes) and their interconnectedness (links) to each relevant
     system. A disciplined understanding of a system is crucial to viewing the OE holistically. The following are
     essential to a system:

          • The components and/or system elements.
          • The synergy among components and other systems.
          • A functional boundary separating it from other systems.

     5-8. Friendly forces cannot target threat forces and capabilities without understanding threat target systems—
     from general to detailed target elements (a macro to micro approach). This effort results in intelligence that
     is pushed from the combatant commander, the joint force, or theater army to Army echelons. Analyzing threat
     systems overlaps with developing threat models and should be conducted as early as possible before actual
     operations—preferably before the MDMP—and continuously updated. This analysis is a priority for
     organizations during the Army strategic contexts; however, it may be conducted at any time during planning.

     5-9. Analyzing threat systems is not solely an intelligence function; it requires extensive collaboration
     between intelligence, fires, and all members associated with the targeting process. Analyzing threat systems
     is a priority, primarily for theater organizations during competition and crisis. However, the task may be
     conducted at any time if the staff does not have general HVTs or it lacks an understanding of the components
     of a threat function and how that threat performs that function. Often, there are major differences in how and
     when this task is conducted to support stability operations versus offensive and defensive operations.
     Analyzing threat systems is more likely conducted at the lower tactical levels—as well as during the MDMP,
     when necessary—to support stability operations.

CONDUCT TARGET VALUE ANALYSIS

     5-16. TVA is the Army’s methodology for prioritizing potential HVTs and identifying HVT sets with a given
     tactical situation. This methodology ranks the value or worth of target sets. Initially, the intelligence targeting
     element conducts TVA with the targeting working group or fires cell (based on unit SOPs) in conjunction with
     analyzing threat systems and developing analytical products. However, TVA is a valuable tool that can be
     applied multiple times during intelligence support to target development as well as—

          • Before receipt of mission based on foundational intelligence products.
          • During step 3 of the IPOE process (evaluate the threat) to identify general HVTs.
          • During step 4 of the IPOE process (determine threat COAs) to identify specific HVTs.
          • During step 3 of the MDMP (friendly COA development) to select HPTs.
          • At any point to support subsequent target development.

     5-17. The G-2/S-2 leads TVA during initial IPOE. To complete the threat model, TVA quantifies the relative
     value of specific unit-type HVTs with each other in relation to a specific mission-type, based in part on
     conclusions reached from evaluating threat characteristics.

     5-18. The G-2/S-2, in coordination with the G-3/S-3, FSCOORD/FSO, and other staff members, uses TVA
     while war-gaming the operation to finalize staff estimations and develop a friendly and enemy decision
     support template (DST). A by-product of this is determining enemy assets critical to the success of the
     enemy’s mission and HVTs. War gaming can assist in identifying requirements for the information collection
     plan as well as when and where indicators and specific information requirements (SIRs) are likely to appear
     on the battlefield. These indicators and SIRs should be disseminated to all collection assets.

                ▪ What function (or percentage of it) will be curtailed by action on the target?
                ▪ How many targets are there?

Intelligence Gain/Loss Considerations

     5-21. Intelligence gain/loss considerations apply to information sources being exploited for combat
     information and intelligence. The G-2/S-2 must ensure the commanders and staffs understand the process of
     evaluating whether destroying a target has a higher payoff than sustaining intelligence collection against the
     target to further understand the enemy as a network/system. An example of this is a guarded frequency for
     signals intelligence (SIGINT) exploitation. The G-2/S-2 recommends to the commander to not jam or
     interfere with the frequency during ongoing exploitation. A guarded frequency is time-oriented, changing as
     the threat assumes different combat postures. This frequency can be jammed after the commander has
     weighed the potential operational gain against the loss of the intelligence exploitation.

THREAT DECEPTION CONSIDERATIONS

     6-14. Intelligence analysts must consider the possibility of threat deception capabilities when identifying and
     developing recommended targets as well as during the detect function. Many threats, especially peer threats,
     are adept at using visual, electromagnetic, physical, multispectral, and cyberspace deception techniques to
     hide their critical systems/nodes and attempt to deceive friendly forces to expend valuable munitions and
     other resources on false or irrelevant targets.

     6-15. Peer threats conceal the time and place of attack using deception operations. The intelligence staff must
     be prepared to provide support to the operations staff for deception planning. The intelligence staff
     incorporates all available collection means to achieve as much mix and redundancy as possible. Sensor
     mixing increases the probability of collection and reduces the risk of successful threat deception.

     6-25. Combat assessment is the determination of the effectiveness of friendly attack (JP 3-60). Combat
     assessment encompasses many tactical-level assessment actions and has implications at the operational level
     as well. Combat assessment, at all levels of warfare, focuses on identifying recommendations for maneuver
     operations and on evaluating the results of weapons and target engagement (with lethal and nonlethal
     capabilities), thus providing data for fires and the Army and joint targeting processes.

     6-26. To conduct combat assessment, it is important to understand the linkage between the targets and the
     objectives, targeting guidance, and desired effects. This linkage begins with targeting tasks, effects, and
     objectives, which support assigned operational tasks, effects, and objectives. It is important to understand the
     difference between operational objectives/effects, targeting objectives/effects, and munitions effects.

     6-27. Combat assessment comprises three elements: BDA, munitions effectiveness assessment (MEA), and
     reattack recommendation. Together, BDA and MEA provide the commander and staff with an assessment of
     the effects achieved against targets and whether the targeting guidance was met. Based on this information,
     the staff can recommend reattack, when necessary.

BATTLE DAMAGE ASSESSMENT

     6-28. Battle damage assessment is the estimate of damage composed of physical and functional damage
     assessment, as well as target system assessment, resulting from the application of fires (JP 3-0). BDA
     compares post-execution results with the commander’s intended effects on the target. Comprehensive BDA
     requires a coordinated and integrated effort between the intelligence staff and the operations staff, as well as
     other staff sections. BDA requirements are recorded on the AGM and translated into information
     requirements for inclusion in the information collection plan.

     6-29. BDA—
          • Enables commanders to obtain timely and accurate snapshots of the effectiveness of the targeting
                effort on the enemy based on the commander’s intent and targeting guidance.
          • Provides commanders with an estimate of the enemy’s combat effectiveness, capabilities, and
                intentions. From this information, commanders determine when or whether their targeting effort
                accomplishes their objectives.
          • Assists in determining if restrike is necessary. Commanders use BDA to allocate or redirect attack
                systems to use available combat power.

     6-30. BDA is a three-part analytical process, encompassing—
          • Physical damage assessment—the estimate of the quantitative extent of physical damage to a target
                (JP 3-60). This assessment is a post-attack target analysis, coordinated among all unit elements.
          • Functional damage assessment—the estimate of the effect of military force to degrade or destroy
                the functional or operational capability of the target to perform its intended mission and on the
                level of success in achieving operational objectives established against the target (JP 3-60). All-
                source intelligence analysts fuse information and assess the remaining functional or operational
                capability of the target or target system. The assessment—
                ▪ Focuses on measurable effects and estimates the threat’s ability to reorganize or find
                     alternative means to continue operations.
                ▪ Must include an estimation of the time required for recuperation or replacement of the target’s
                     function. BDA analysts must compare the desired effect for the engagement with the status of
                     the target to determine if the commander’s intended effect on the target was achieved.
          • Target system damage assessment—the broad assessment of the overall impact and effectiveness
                of military force applied against the operation of an enemy target system, significant subdivisions
                of the system, or total combat effectiveness relative to the operational objectives established
                (JP 3-60). All-source intelligence analysts assist the staff in assessing either the threat’s combat
                effectiveness or major threat subordinate elements or the capabilities required to accomplish a
                threat mission. Target system damage assessments are developed by compiling the functional
                damage assessments of individual targets within a system and applying them to current system
                analysis or threat order of battle. This is a relatively permanent assessment (compared to
                functional damage assessment) that can be used for more than one mission.

     6-31. BDA requirements for specific HPTs are determined during the decide function. Collection assets can
     often answer requirements for target development and target acquisition or BDA but not both. An asset used
     for BDA may be unavailable for target development and target acquisition requirements. The intelligence

          • BDA measures what is important to commanders—not make important what is easily measurable.
          • BDA should be objective. When receiving a BDA product from another echelon, conclusions

                should be verified (time permitting) to identify and resolve discrepancies among BDA analysts at
                different headquarters.
          • BDA’s reliability and credibility rely mostly on collection assets. The quantity and quality of
                collection assets influence whether the assessment is highly reliable (concrete, quantifiable, and
                precise) or has a low reliability (estimation). An effective BDA uses more than one source to
                verify each conclusion.
          • G-2/S-2s and commanders must consider enemy reinforcement and equipment resupply rates
                when calculating BDA to continue—
                ▪ Creating an accurate picture of the enemy.

     6-32. BDA is more than determining the number of casualties or the amount of equipment destroyed. The

          • Whether the targets are moving or hardening in response to the attack.
          • Changes in deception efforts and techniques.
          • Whether the damage achieved is affecting the threat’s combat effectiveness as expected.

     operational context, and mission variables (METT-TC [I]), the how and when battle damage reporting is

     dissemination of timely and accurate BDA. The lack of capabilities, personnel, or time should not hinder BDA.
     Units must create processes with available resources and leverage higher headquarters assets when possible.

          • Targets.

     A-10. Measurement and signature intelligence (MASINT) measures a characteristic, plots it against an

JOINT TARGETING PROCESS

     B-1. The joint targeting process supports the commander’s campaign plan, objectives, and scheme of
     maneuver for the employment of military force to achieve a desired military end state. The process integrates
     the capabilities of national assets, geographic CCMDs, subordinate joint forces, and multinational forces, all
     of which possess varying capabilities and requirements. Joint targeting provides geographic CCMDs with a
     list of targets supporting campaign objectives and subordinate component commander’s supporting
     objectives. The JFC sets priorities and provides clear targeting guidance.

     B-2. Components must understand the joint targeting process to—
          • Identify the right target for the right reason.
          • Be engaged with the right asset at the right time and place.
          • Produce the desired effects necessary to accomplish the JFC’s objectives while mitigating risk and
                collateral concerns. (See JP 3-60.)

     B-3. The joint targeting process includes sequential phases that are continuously operating, closely related,
     interacting, and with interdependent functions. These six interrelated phases make up the joint targeting cycle,
     which provides an essential framework to conduct joint targeting:

          • Phase 1: Commander’s objectives, targeting guidance, and intent. Summarizes—
                ▪ Targets to be engaged and under what conditions.
                ▪ Desired effects.
                ▪ Risks the commander is accepting.
                ▪ The delegation of any authority with the execution of targets, such as TSTs.

          • Phase 2: Target development and prioritization. Identifies and characterizes potential targets
                that, when successfully engaged, support achieving the commander’s objectives. These targets can
                be fully developed and validated or be candidates that are determined to support the commander’s
                objectives. Regardless, each target must be in accordance with the commander’s guidance, the law
                of armed conflict, and the applicable rules of engagement.

          • Phase 3: Capabilities analysis. Estimates the most likely outcome resulting from the use of lethal
                and/or nonlethal capabilities against specific targets to create desired effects while minimizing
                collateral effects.

          • Phase 4: Commander’s decision and force assignment. Fuses target nominations with available
                forces, sensors, and weapon systems into operationally sound target packages. It includes approval
                of the joint integrated prioritized target list; force apportionment and/or allocation to support
                lethal, nonlethal, and collection plans; and risk management. The joint targeting coordination
                board translates the commander’s planning guidance and approved targeting tasks into tasks to
                subordinate units, including what is to be achieved, where, when, and why.

          • Phase 5: Mission planning and force execution. Upon receipt of the tasking orders, subordinate
                commands conduct planning for the execution of deliberate and dynamic operations. During
                execution, planners and commanders conduct continual analysis to determine if planned targets
                still contribute to the commander’s objectives, if targets are accurately located, and how planned
                actions impact other friendly operations.

          • Phase 6: Combat assessment. Measures the progress of the targeting tasks executed and the
                desired effects achieved, including their contribution to the specified objective, intent, and the
                evaluation of future actions.

     B-4. Targeting leverages the commander’s guidance and intent and the operational objectives from joint
     planning to identify the intelligence requirements for joint IPOE (also called JIPOE) and other products of
     the intelligence process (including IPOE). Capabilities are selected during the targeting process to create
     effects and achieve objectives developed during planning. Targeting encompasses many processes—all
     linked and logically guided by the joint targeting cycle—that continuously analyze, identify, develop,
     validate, assess, and prioritize targets for engagement by the joint force.

FIND, FIX, FINISH, EXPLOIT, ANALYZE, AND DISSEMINATE PROCESS

     B-7. The find, fix, finish, exploit, analyze, and disseminate (F3EAD) process is an aggressive targeting
     model that features massed, persistent joint intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance cued to a
     decentralized all-source intelligence enterprise. Within the joint force, identity intelligence principally
     supports F3EAD; the goal is to find HVIs—for example, looking for an individual amid a mass civilian
     gathering and attempting to fix their exact location. This process can be used to conduct targeting by
     leveraging and synchronizing forces, assets, and enablers to create a desired effect.

     B-8. Considering the F3EAD targeting model from an information advantage perspective is essential. This
     model not only emphasizes the speed of removing a combatant from the battlefield, but it also creates a
     window of opportunity to gain more information on the threat. The exploit and analyze steps are often the
     main effort of the F3EAD model because they provide insight into the enemy’s network and may offer new
     lines of operations. (See JP 3-26 for doctrine on the F3EAD process.)

FIND, FIX, TRACK, TARGET, ENGAGE, AND ASSESS PROCESS

     B-9. Targets that are treated as TSTs and can be engaged use the dynamic targeting process. Dynamic
     targeting occurs when targets are prosecuted outside of the 24-hour integrated target order/air tasking order
     cycle using a condensed process known as find, fix, track, target, engage, and assess (F2T2EA). The F2T2EA
     or dynamic targeting process is integrated during phase 5 of the joint targeting cycle.

     B-10. The F2T2EA process applies to all targets and is most commonly used on targets discovered during
     current operations to support dynamic targeting that focuses on the near and current battle with time horizons
     of hours. (See ATP 3-60.1 and JP 3-60 for doctrine on F2T2EA/dynamic targeting process.)

JOINT AND UNIFIED ACTION PARTNER CONSIDERATIONS

     B-11. Commanders participating in a multinational operation develop policies and procedures for that
     operation. In most multinational operations, commanders must share intelligence with foreign forces as well
     as coordinate receiving intelligence from those forces. This requires a multilevel security system that can
     easily facilitate the sanitization and dissemination of information to U.S. and multinational commanders
     within security guidelines. (See FM 2-0, FM 3-16, and JP 3-16 for doctrine on multinational operations.)

     B-12. A technique for sharing critical intelligence with multinational partners is having U.S. intelligence
     information written for release at the lowest possible classification level (with the fewest dissemination
     restrictions) within foreign disclosure guidelines. The write for release process enables increased information
     sharing with mission partners without sacrificing information security, avoiding over classification and

     release process also describes how to share information in accordance with all published security
     classification guides and other constraints and limitations. (See ATP 3-16.02 for doctrine on write for

INTELLIGENCE SUPPORT TO TARGETING IN URBAN AREAS

     B-16. Urban operations are operations across the range of military operations planned and conducted on, or
     against objectives on a topographical complex and its adjacent natural terrain, where man-made construction or
     the density of population are the dominant features (ATP 3-06). The density of both people and buildings in
     urban areas create familiar operational difficulties for a deployed force and directly and negatively influences
     intelligence collection to support operations, including targeting. Structures and public works infrastructure
     inhibit maneuver and firepower, open and close fields of fire, and severely degrade C2 capabilities.

     B-17. Intelligence support to targeting is more demanding in urban areas due to the requirement to protect
     civilians. Although fires may destroy the threat, it may be counterproductive due to the collateral damage of
     civilian casualties. Among the people, terrain, buildings, and infrastructure in urban areas, people are the
     most challenging to analyze. As the only thinking component of an operational area, people have the capacity
     to significantly modify operations. There are several reasons why urban populations threaten operations and
     their attendant intelligence support functions. The overall population comprises several groups, each with its
     own interests. Relationships between groups might be congenial, hostile, or dependent. Understanding this
     diversity and complexity requires a lot of mental effort and flexibility.

BATTLE DAMAGE ASSESSMENT

     D-18. The 53d ID intelligence cell must conduct BDA, which provides estimates of the damage and/or
     delivered effects against targets, target critical elements, and target systems. The intelligence cell—

          • References the AGM to ensure an understanding of targeting objectives and desired effects.
          • Is notified through a fire mission report that a Tor (SA-15) is engaged using three 155-millimerter

                rounds.
          • Uses the ICSM to check which assets were assigned to track the target via engagement and reports

                battle damage. Since one asset may not provide a clear picture, the intelligence cell looks at all
                available battle damage reporting from available assets to support its assessments.
          • Determines the effects delivered to the target during engagement after completing physical
                damage, functional damage, and target system damage assessments. The cell assesses that the
                target is destroyed (80-percent confidence level)—functionally destroyed with a minimum two-
                day recuperation estimate.
          • Produces BDA reporting derived from available imagery, intelligence reports, and other reporting

                targeting working group according to unit SOPs.
          • Provides reattack recommendations, which support future targeting considerations. Based on the
                BDA and MEA for this engagement, the intelligence cell reattack recommendation is for future
                considerations. Although the desired effects were achieved against the target, the threat can
                recuperate the target to a point where reattack may be required.

                                 Target Folder

ELECTRONIC TARGET FOLDER

     E-1. A target folder is a folder, hardcopy or electronic, containing target intelligence and related materials
     prepared for planning and executing action against a specific target (JP 3-60). Target folders are built and
     disseminated to support the MDMP in preparation for combat operations and targeting. Target data must be
     in a shareable format, allowing the targeting teams to save progress, share, or database targets during the
     development process to satisfy requirements.

     E-2. An ETF is a set of web pages and/or links to metadata-tagged dynamic target materials that are stored
     and maintained in central repositories. When an entity has been identified as a potential target, an ETF is
     started. ETFs store target intelligence and operational, planning, and legal information to support target
     development. ETFs are catalogued by a unique identifier to identify the specific target though the target’s
     development stages.

     E-3. Target development generally occurs in three stages:
          • Basic. Basic target development standards are identification, location, function, target
                significance, target description, and source description.
          • Intermediate. Intermediate target development standards are more detailed/up-to-date target
                significance, target description (graphic if applicable), and source description. Intermediate standards
                also add functional characterization, expectation statement, and critical target element statements.
          • Advanced. Advanced target development standards include the following functions:
                weaponeering, aimpoint selection, and collateral damage estimation.

     E-4. In a joint role, CJCSI 3370.01D establishes target development standards for presenting an ETF to a
     validation authority. Echelons above corps organizations are critical in developing ETFs. At echelons corps
     and below, the intelligence targeting element develops and refines the target intelligence folders based on
     their requirements.

TARGET INTELLIGENCE FOLDER

     E-5. Developing target intelligence folders is optional at brigade and below levels. These echelons normally
     develop intelligence databases with specific target-related information identified before or during preparation
     for operations. Army intelligence targeting elements develop target intelligence folders based on joint TSA
     and Army target development requirements. The folder format generally follows unit requirements,
     standards, and SOPs. Folders are developed and maintained at each Army echelon (but referenced to the
     larger threat target system, component and element); they typically include a unique target intelligence
     number, which is maintained throughout operations.

     E-6. Target intelligence folders include specific intelligence products and analysis, as required, from higher
     echelons. The folders are continuously updated with information and intelligence over time. Intelligence
     targeting elements develop target intelligence folders to account for HVTs, HPTs, or systems and system
     components. Target intelligence folders usually consist of two subfolders:

          • Work folder. This folder contains raw data, working aids, and analyst notes used by individual
                analysts to form conclusions based on what the target development element presents to the ACE
                chief, G-2/S-2, and unit commander.

          • Target intelligence package (also known as intelligence target packet). This package contains
                individual target intelligence used by the targeting working group to plan targeting operations.
                (See FM 3-60.)

     E-7. Target materials are graphic, textual, tabular, digital, video, or other presentations of target intelligence
     primarily designed to support operations against designated targets by one or more weapon(s) systems
     (JP 3-60). Target materials are integral in developing target folders. They are standardized products of target
     intelligence and/or other information that support intermediate target development or are used as inputs to
     support the targeting process.

intelligence
          1. The product resulting from the collection, processing, integration, evaluation, analysis, and
          interpretation of available information concerning foreign nations, hostile or potentially hostile forces
          or elements, or areas of actual or potential operations. 2. The activities that result in the product. 3. The

          The process by which collected information is evaluated and integrated with existing information to

          A well-defined area of intelligence planning, collection, processing, exploitation, analysis, and
          reporting using a specific category of technical or human resources. (JP 2-0)

          The systematic process of analyzing the mission variables of enemy, terrain, weather, and civil
          considerations in an area of interest to determine their effect on operations. (FM 2-0)

intelligence reach
          The activity by which intelligence organizations proactively and rapidly access information from,
          receive support from, and conduct direct collaboration and information sharing with other units and
          agencies, both within and outside the area of operations, unconstrained by geographic proximity,
          echelon, or command. (ADP 2-0)

intelligence requirement
          1. Any subject, general or specific, upon which there is a need for the collection of information or the
          production of intelligence. 2. A requirement for intelligence to fill a gap in the command’s knowledge
          or understanding of the operational environment or threat forces. (JP 2-0)

intelligence warfighting function
          The related tasks and systems that facilitate understanding the enemy, terrain, weather, civil
          considerations, and other significant aspects of the operational environment. (ADP 3-0)

measurement and signature intelligence
          Information produced by quantitative and qualitative analysis of physical attributes of targets and events
          to detect, characterize, locate, and identify targets and events; and derived from specialized, technically
          derived measurements and signatures of physical phenomenon intrinsic to an object or event. (JP 2-0)

munitions effectiveness assessment
          The assessment of the military force applied in terms of the weapon system and munitions
          effectiveness to determine and recommend any required changes to the methodology, tactics, weapon
          system, munitions, fusing, and/or weapon delivery parameters to increase force effectiveness. (JP 2-0)

no-strike list
          A list of entities characterized as protected from the effects of military operations under international
          law or rules of engagement. (JP 3-60)

open-source intelligence
          Intelligence that is produced from publicly available information and is collected, exploited, and
          disseminated in a timely manner to an appropriate audience for the purpose of addressing a specific
          intelligence requirement. (Public Law 109-163)

operational framework
          A cognitive tool used to assist commanders and staffs in clearly visualizing and describing the
          application of combat power in time, space, purpose, and resources in the concept of operations.
          (ADP 1-01)

physical damage assessment
          The estimate of the quantitative extent of physical damage to a target. (JP 3-60)
