CPT Andrew Cairns/CPT Jacob Dellinger

Support for reconnaissance operations is the inherent responsibility of the combat trains
command post (CTCP). The CTCP is charged with maintaining the momentum of the operation
through supporting the maneuver plan. The squadron’s success is contingent on a CTCPs ability
to resource and maintain the squadron’s three reconnaissance troops through a task organization
inclusive of logistical, administrative, and maintenance functions.

The squadron’s ability to conduct sustained reconnaissance and security operations was heavily

reliant on the ability for the tactical logistics execution to adequately support the maneuver plan.

operations required to successfully employ the CTCP and execute sustainment operations. The
squadron was offered a fantastic opportunity to identify the routine with a training density
occurring in the summer of 2014. Prior to 5-4 CAV’s deployment to NTC 15-06, the squadron

rotation. The mission allowed the squadron to train all aspects of the armored reconnaissance
fight, to include implementing the CTCP in support of the unit’s sustainment operations. During
this exercise, the CTCP included the squadron S-1 and S-4 sections, the HHT headquarters

To ensure that the combat trains would be capable of supporting the squadron, intensive
rehearsal of the battle drills was conducted and that became the main focus of the troop. The
longest and most complex drill that required multiple rehearsals was displacement of the CTCP.
This drill consisted of tearing down the Standardized Integrated Command Post System (SICPS)
and preparing the command track (M1068) for movement while simultaneously maintaining
communication with squadron HQs and receiving reports from the troops. The most important
lesson from conducting multiple iterations was that tear down and set up of the OE-254 antenna
significantly slowed down movement. Without losing capability, the CTCP modified its
communication design to where the Quick Erect Antenna Mast System (QEAMS) was used for
the brigade administrative/logistics (A/L) net since this frequency needed to reach the furthest
(was not used at XCTC) and a COM201 was emplaced on top of the M1068 for talking to the
forward troops. Other key battle drills rehearsed included battle tracking, owning the fight when
the command post (CP) met its displacement criteria, and receiving and transmitting combat
reports via frequency modulation (FM) and Joint Capabilities Release (JCR).

The preparation of the CTCP continued when the squadron redeployed to Fort Riley. The CTCP
was able to maintain tempo in its development through the execution of several validation
exercises leading up to the NTC rotation. The squadron utilized home station training
opportunities at both the unit level and installation schools to enhance the capability of the
CTCP. Entering into this training density, there was a clear focus on digital reporting which
helped shape the training regimen. The focus would later pay significant dividends during the
training rotation due to the depth of knowledge amongst the Troopers in both the CP and the
CTCP. The NTC offered the squadron an opportunity to put stress on systems, validate training
focuses, and prepare for future operations.

A pivotal component of the CTCP was the ability to receive, process and act on information
which was reported by the troops. The emphasis on digital systems and long range
communications prior to the deployment enabled the CTCP to be successful.

During the execution of operations, the squadron would leverage the JCR system, specifically,
the chat feature, to assist in the execution of logistics synchronization (LOGSYNC) meetings
with the troops during operations. The JCR log provides a window for an open dialogue over
JCR or a chat room to aid in reporting and cut down on flash, immediate, priority, routine (FIPR)
backlog; preventing reports from being lost. Rapid and accurate reporting endured during the
mission as a result of employment of this system. Additionally, the S1 section would focus
heavily on the employment of “open office” within JCR to provide the troops a standardized and
accurate reporting tool for statuses (STATs) throughout operations. This technique would pay
dividends during sustained operations over stretched lines of communications. Furthermore, the
S1 was able to use the Microsoft Excel and Word features on JCR log for reporting of personnel
statuses (PERSTATs) and tracking of casualties and equipment. The troop 1SGs reported their
daily losses from the previous day’s battle period to maximize situational awareness for the
squadron headquarters and would allow for more responsive soldier regeneration. The chat room
created shared understanding for all troops during critical battle rhythm events. JCR quickly

emergency (PACE) plan made it so that the distance maintained between the CTCP and CP was

improper forecasting for fuel where the distance allowed for rapid response to the CP from the
CTCP.

CTCP during reconnaissance. During the initial phases of the operation, the site location
RECONs for the CTCP were not conducted effectively and lacked key personnel to ensure
feasibility of a location. Therefore, it became a tactic, technique, and procedure (TTP) for site
location RECONs to include the commander, squadron maintenance sergeant or squadron
maintenance technician, squadron medical officer and an element from the S-1 section. The need
to balance security and mission accomplishment, specifically maintenance was delicate as the
CTCP had to be positioned on terrain suitable to employ the Forward Repair System (FRS) and
the boom on M88 for vehicle maintenance. Many times the location of the CTCP was not ideal
in terms of defense, but prudent risk was assumed to ensure that doctrinal distance was
maintained and that maintenance could be conducted.

Additionally, protecting the trains was a continually evolving problem as the troop 1SG had the
responsibility to tie in all disabled vehicles awaiting parts into the security plan. The crews of
those vehicles were also integrated into the security plan not only from their mounted platforms,
but also to conduct disruption patrols and set observation points to detect any threats. Prior to
the deployment to the NTC, the squadron integrated vehicle crews from the forward support
company into Table (TBL) VI gunnery to provide qualified crews. Having additional combat
power assisted in the security of the CTCP.

A planning oversight and poor forecasting by the troop commander for Class IV materials
contributed to reduced security at the CTCP. The Class IV package which was fielded to the
CTCP was not sufficient, and the commander and 1SG had to conduct engagement area
development with a less than ideal amount of concertina wire and sand bags. Once the task
organization with inclusion of the UMCP was finalized, a standard Class IV package needed to
be identified prior to line of departure (LD) and use of these materials tied into the rehearsals
conducted. Failure to do so put the security of the command node in jeopardy. However, the
command team followed standard engagement area (EA) development procedure by “red
teaming” their position through rehearsals and reconnaissance from likely enemy avenues of
approach. Doing so, enabled the most efficient use of a reduced supply for turning and fixing
obstacles.

Furthermore, displacement inside of the CTCP was a challenge due to the terrain and the robust
size of the element. Thus, there were many times when vehicles were too close together and
were at risk to catastrophic damage and degradation of capability in the event of indirect fire
(IDF).

The security of the CTCP waned as vehicles came and went through the UMCP. At one point
the combat power was reduced to only three combat platforms. This proved to be problematic
when vehicles were pulled to support casualty evacuation (CASEVAC) from the main aid station
(MAS) as security had to be reduced to ensure Soldiers did not die of wounds, thus, degrading
combat effectiveness of the line troops. Therefore, the command team assumed risk during the
execution of CASEVAC operations at the CTCP.

With respect to the chemical threat, the CTCP employed radiac sets and M8 paper to detect
possible chemical attacks. The CTCP was successful at preparing for attacks across all of the

forms of contact and it spent much of the rotation avoiding becoming a soft target. Emblematic

Overall, security at the CTCP proved to be the most daunting task and one which required more
deliberate planning and resourcing during both the train up and the rehearsal phase.
A critical lesson learned during the rotation at the NTC was to task organize for mission early in
the training cycle. During the reception, staging, onward movement and integration (RSOI)
week, a lot of time and energy was spent integrating the different elements which would serve in
the CTCP, many of which were not organic to HHT. The problems were not insurmountable, but
the cohesion and fluidity of operations would have been enhanced if during the train up for NTC,
the CTCP had conducted operations as it had at NTC. Additionally, depth of knowledge in
critical equipment would have assisted the CTCP [i.e. only one operator for the Combat-Service-
Support Automated Information Systems Interface (CAISI) and only two personnel proficient on
HF radios. Both pieces of equipment were available at home station and not given enough of a
focus prior to the rotation.
This article has provided a way to approach the preparation of a CTCP for the decisive action
training environment in the areas of training, manning and equipping to enable the squadron to
have depth in mission command, maximize the logistical common operating picture and sustain
operations. Key lessons identified include task organize for your mission early, train multiple
personnel on low density equipment for redundancy, conduct homestation training on assuming
control of the fight from the main CP, and finally, plan and rehearse security of the CTCP using
all available assets. These techniques and lessons are applicable to all maneuver formations
(Armored, Infantry, and Stryker BCTs) that utilize CTCPs.
