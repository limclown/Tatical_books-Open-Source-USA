                                           FOREWORD

Today’s airspace environment is increasingly complex. Technological advances in air
defense systems, cruise missiles, unmanned aircraft systems (UAS), loitering munitions, and
long-range fires shape that complexity. The air domain’s vertical component, and the speed
and freedom with which forces maneuver within it, create unique challenges for commanders
seeking to exercise control in the air domain. In addition, wide-ranging variables such as
aircraft capabilities, airspace characteristics, airspace use, and various control procedures
create additional layers of complexity. As such, the objective of airspace control is to enable
military operations with the most effective, efficient, and safe use of airspace to achieve joint
force commanders (JFC) objectives and priorities.
Airspace control is extremely dynamic and situational. It needs to be flexible and responsive
to accommodate users with varied technical capabilities and to mitigate technical failure or
adversary action to optimize airspace use. Therefore, Airmen should bring air domain
expertise coupled with a joint perspective to ensure airspace control systems and procedures
promote the safe, efficient, and flexible use of airspace while increasing operational
effectiveness and facilitating emerging joint force capabilities (e.g., small UAS, loitering
munitions). Proven airspace control procedures have enduring utility, but new technologies
and platforms will force continued adaptations to match the changing operational
environment. Airmen must think innovatively to anticipate and solve future challenges.
Further, it is essential that Airmen are trained to perform deliberate planning of all-domain
effects in a distributed manner and execute the mission when isolated from decision-makers
in distributed environments. Airmen must be comfortable making decisions and operating
based on the commander’s intent and the principles of mission command.
As we build a more capable future force, doctrine must evolve to accommodate new
technologies. Existing command and control (C2) architecture, coupled with validated best
practices, provides the foundation for innovative solutions to evolving air domain challenges.
Throughout history, innovative Airmen adapted technologies and developed methods of
employment to meet our nation’s challenges. We must continue to do so.

             CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION TO AIRSPACE CONTROL

Airspace control is defined as “the exercise of delegated authority over designated airspace
and users through control procedures and coordination measures (CM) to maximize
operational effectiveness.”1 Airspace control in joint operations is a term used to describe the
activities performed and authorities executed by the Airspace Control Authority (ACA). The
ACA is the commander delegated overall responsibility for airspace control. The ACA
develops airspace control plans (ACP) and performs airspace control through an airspace
control system (ACS). The ACS is an arrangement by the ACA of the component and host-
nation airspace control elements (i.e., airspace control organizations, personnel, policies,
procedures, and facilities).2 To enable air tasking order (ATO) execution, airspace control
elements perform roles including air battle management (e.g., C2 joint air operations), air
defense (combat identification, early warning, weapons control status, target tracking,
defensive counter-air [DCA]), or surveillance and reconnaissance.

In accordance with (IAW) JFC guidance, airspace control elements manage airspace
requests, direct maneuvering aircraft, and integrate fires. Effective airspace control requires
planning and constant evaluation. Planning begins before combat operations and often
transitions through varying degrees of civil and military control across the competition
continuum. The JFC approves airspace control procedures within a joint operations area
(JOA). Airspace control aims to maximize combat operations effectiveness without unduly
restricting any Service or functional component’s capability.

Wide-ranging variables such as aircraft capabilities, airspace characteristics, airspace use,
and various control procedures add layers of complexity and difficulty to airspace control
operation. Such challenges are heightened during multinational air operations. Additional
concerns arise from host nation (HN) relationships, system interoperability, and disparate
planning processes across component and partner forces. Likewise, other governmental
agencies, civilian users, nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), and relief agencies may
require combat zone airspace. Altogether, these user demands require an integrated ACS to
enable safe, efficient use of airspace and to mitigate the risk of friendly-fire incidents and
unintended engagements against civil and neutral forces.

BASIC PRINCIPLES

Airspace is used by all components of the joint and multinational forces in the JOA. The
primary goal of airspace control is to enhance the effectiveness of joint and
multinational operations. A high concentration of friendly surface, sub-surface, and air-
launched weapon systems must share airspace to apply combat power IAW the JFC’s intent
without exceeding acceptable operational risk. The chart below is derived from Joint
Publication (JP) 3-52, Joint Airspace Control, and details basic airspace control principles
applicable throughout all joint air operations and across the competition continuum.

AIRMAN’S PERSPECTIVE

The Airman’s Perspective on airpower, as stated by Air Force Doctrine Publication (AFDP) 1-
1, is that control of the air is a necessary precondition for control of the surface. Airmen
require theater-wide visibility of the air domain. They must maintain the requisite expertise
and capabilities to control and operate across theaters, transitioning seamlessly from
operational levels to the lowest tactical level, anywhere around the globe.

Given airpower’s range and speed, Airmen operate and control aircraft across international
boundaries, in controlled and uncontrolled airspace, and from en route airspace structures
and tactically controlled operational areas during a single mission. Such capabilities may be
provided from within or outside a combatant commander’s (CCDR) area of responsibility

(AOR) or the JFC’s JOA. Airspace control activities often cross theater and regional

CONTROL OF THE AIR

Control of the air is distinct from airspace control but closely related to it. Some level of
airspace control is necessary for air operations regardless of the degree of control the joint
force exercises over the air domain. The degree of control the joint force exercises in the air
domain relative to that of an adversary at a given time and place can be described as air
parity, superiority, or supremacy. Air parity describes a condition in a contested environment
in which no force has control of the air. Air superiority describes a degree of control where
one force can conduct its operations at a given time and place without prohibitive interference
from air and missile threats. Air supremacy describes a degree of control where an opposing
force is incapable of effective interference within the operational area (OA) using air and
missile threats.3

While air supremacy is most desirable, it may not be operationally feasible. In such cases, air
superiority, even local or temporary, may provide sufficient freedom of action to create desired
effects. Airspace control procedures for localized air superiority operations should reflect the
JFC’s acceptable level of risk balanced against operational objectives. This ensures the
appropriate level of airspace control resources are employed to enable operations.

The JFACC, in pursuit of achieving counterair objectives, integrates airspace control
procedures and planning (an ACA function) with offensive counter-air (OCA), DCA (an AADC
function), and other components operations. ACPs may exercise a greater degree of
restriction on friendly and civil airspace users, particularly if certain platforms lack
communications equipment and technology to facilitate identification. ACPs accommodate a
greater variety of airspace users as joint force control over the air domain increases.

MISSION COMMAND

Vertically and horizontally integrated C2 enables airspace control inside the mission
command framework of Centralized Command–Distributed Control–Decentralized Execution
(CC-DC-DE).4 This architecture fuses component liaisons, airspace control elements, and
airspace users to enhance domain awareness and meet commander’s intent. Increased
airspace awareness fosters trust in the ACS and the ACA while mitigating airspace conflicts
and enabling joint force operations. Airspace control elements and users operate according
to commander’s intent, enabling unity of effort in contested, degraded, and operationally
limited (CDO-L) environments where communication with higher headquarters may be
intermittent or impossible.

JOINT ALL-DOMAIN OPERATIONS
Airspace control is inherently joint and affects every warfighting domain. All joint force
components have airspace mission requirements that should be integrated, coordinated,
prioritized, and deconflicted within the ACS. The ACS should be arranged and employed to:
 Maximize the effectiveness of operations conducted in, from, and through the air.
 Prevent friendly fire incidents, reduce the risk of collateral damage, and prevent unintended

    engagements against friendly, neutral, and civil aircraft.
 Facilitate joint fires and enhance air and missile defense (AMD).
 Enhance and support movement, maneuver, and employment of land component forces.
 Facilitate fleet defense and power projection from naval surface forces.
 Integrate and synchronize all-domain operations to create synergistic effects and achieve

    JFC objectives.

              CHAPTER 2: AIRSPACE CONTROL FUNDAMENTALS

Airspace control is essential to accomplishing the JFC's objectives. Effective airspace
control allows all users to access airspace and aims to accommodate and deconflict
competing users. As an operational environment (OE), the air domain encompasses a variety
of conditions, circumstances, and influences that affect employment and the decisions of
commanders. The air domain OE may be categorized as either hostile, uncertain, or
permissive based on the following characteristics:6

 Hostile: A condition where freedom of movement is contested. Risk to air operations and
    air assets should be considered high in hostile air environments.

 Uncertain: A condition in which the joint force’s freedom of movement may be contested.
    Risk to air operations and air assets may be considered moderate or significant in
    uncertain air environments.

 Permissive: Uncontested conditions in which joint forces have freedom of movement.
    Risk to air operations and air assets should be considered low in permissive air
    environments.

AIRSPACE CONTROL METHODS

An ACS should adapt to changing requirements and priorities as operations progress while
accounting for dynamic OE conditions and changes in Joint Forces’ degree of control over
the air domain. The ACS should incorporate joint force and HN airspace control capabilities
while remaining functional in situations where adversaries deny, degrade, or disrupt the ACS.
The capabilities of the airspace control element executing control over a given volume of
airspace and the categorization of airspace (hostile, uncertain, or permissive) normally
determine the ACS’s characteristics and methods of control within airspace. The control
system’s structure should encourage close coordination among joint force components,
allowing a rapid concentration of combat power. Airspace control methods may vary across
the JOA from positive control to procedural control, or any combination of the two.

Positive control relies on identification, tracking, and communicating with aircraft within the
airspace, conducted with electronic means by an airspace control element. Positive control
requires locating and identifying airspace users in real time while maintaining continuous
communications to pass control instructions. It is normally conducted via electronic means by
an airspace control element having the authority and responsibility for airspace management
within an assigned airspace volume. Typically, airspace control elements should be equipped
with radar, identification friend or foe interrogators and receivers, beacons, track processing
computers, digital data links, and communications equipment to perform positive control. In
the event positive control systems become degraded or unavailable, predetermined
procedures to transition to procedural control should be established. Such procedures should
account for the differences between civil and military communications and surveillance
systems.

Procedural control relies on a combination of previously agreed-to and promulgated orders
and procedures. It relies on common published procedures, designated airspace, and
instructions by an authorized airspace control element to deconflict and activate CMs such

COST VERSUS RISK

When considering procedural and positive control, level of effort, resources, and risk (i.e., risk
to personnel and air assets, risk to force, and/or risk to mission) need to be addressed (see
“Notional Airspace Control Continuum, Cost vs. Risk” figure). The minimum requirements for
surveillance, identification, and communications equipment can vary by theater and
operation, but are likely to be driven by a combination of military and civil aviation regulations
and the varying levels and types of risk the JFC is willing to accept. Assuming a constant air
traffic volume, uncontrolled airspace exerts a small drain on resources however, carries
increased risk to personnel and air assets. On the other hand, this arrangement may decrease
risk to mission by capitalizing on airpower’s inherent flexibility. For that same airspace,
standing airspace procedures incrementally increase control and resources required, reduce
risk to personnel and air assets, but may increase risk to mission. Full military or civilian
positive control provides the least risk to personnel and air assets, but significant airspace
control resource requirements may increase risk to force (i.e., the ability to generate and
sustain operations), and risk to mission. Commanders may desire that the entire airspace
control area be under positive control, however, limited resources or other factors, such as
terrain, may make this goal unrealistic. Airspace planners should establish positive control in
areas where the JFC’s risk tolerance regarding personnel and air assets is lowest and the
need for efficiency is highest (e.g., enabling a large diversity of airspace users and uses). In
areas where positive control is not feasible, procedural control is often the minimum standard
for airspace control. Procedural control measures can also form a crucial backup in the event
positive control capability is diminished.

AIRSPACE CONTROL MECHANISMS

To execute airspace control effectively, the ACA translates Air Operations Directive (AOD)
guidance into airspace use and deconfliction procedures through the ACP and ACO. The
ACA also provides airspace use inputs to the ATO and SPINS.

Air Operations Directive (AOD). The AOD provides the JFACC’s guidance for the planning
and execution of each ATO period. It conveys JFC intent concerning acceptable levels of risk
and priority of use for airspace, usually with mission-type orders.

Airspace Control Plan (ACP). The JFC-approved ACP provides specific planning guidance
and procedures for the ACS in the JOA. The ACP implements AOD operational guidance and
may be distributed as a separate document or as an annex to the joint air operations plan
(JAOP). Refer to chapter 4 and appendix A for topics to consider when developing an ACP.

Airspace Control Order (ACO). The ACO implements the ACP and translates general
guidance provided in the ACP into specific control procedures for defined periods. The ACO
provides details of approved CMs while defining and establishing airspace for military
operations as coordinated by the ACA. The ACO is normally disseminated as a separate
document, published and effective for one twenty-four-hour period coinciding with the ATO

day. Like the ATO, changes to the ACO are published during execution, on an as-needed
basis, or at set intervals, to meet the needs of dynamic, high-tempo operations.

Air Tasking Order (ATO). The ATO assigns aircraft to perform missions under the control of
the JFACC during a specified period IAW the AOD and the master air attack plan (MAAP).
The ATO may also include theater air missions not under the control of the JFACC for
visibility, coordination, deconfliction, and air defense amongst all airspace users. Airspace

Special Instructions (SPINS). Along with the ACO, SPINS provide airspace users with
directions to balance combat effectiveness with safe, orderly, and efficient use of airspace.
SPINS provide operational and tactical direction at the appropriate levels of detail and are
updated as frequently as required.

Aeronautical Information Publication (AIP). The AIP is a publication issued by, or with the
authority of, a state (country) and contains aeronautical information that is essential to air
navigation. It is intended to be a manual containing details of regulations, procedures, and
other information pertinent to aircraft operations. AIPs relevant to the JOA should be
referenced when developing the ACP.

          AI and human-machine teaming (HMT) have the potential to increase the
          speed, efficiency and effectiveness of airspace control elements. Airspace
          control applications may include:

           Predicting airspace conflicts and provide automated collision avoidance
               instructions to airspace users.

           Analyzing flight data to predict traffic patterns and reduce congestion.

           Processing joint force, coalition, and civil requests for airspace and

           Anticipating weather patterns to help controllers route airspace users more
               effectively.

           Increased resilience of ACS C2 networks by managing multiple

               operationally limited environments.

               guidance documents such as the AOD and JAOP.

           Increasing the density of air assets that can be effectively controlled by a

from peacetime operations to combat operations and back to peacetime.

AIRSPACE DECONFLICTION

The air operations center (AOC) plans airspace deconfliction at the operational level. The
AOC’s combat plans division usually resolves airspace conflicts during ATO or ACO
production and dissemination, while the combat operations division handles ATO execution.
Tactical level deconfliction is executed by ACS elements and is achieved by directing time,
position, altitude, and airspace CMs. Tactical ACS elements performing this function include
CRC, airborne C2, ASOC, joint air-ground integration center (JAGIC), Marine direct air
support center (DASC), Navy TACC, tactical air control parties (TACP), ATC, and other
component C2 nodes. These ACS elements represent “decentralized execution” of airspace
control operations.

Tactical ACS elements will likely require enhanced air domain awareness to support planning
and integration of emerging joint force capabilities (e.g., UAS, loitering munitions). Effective
control and deconfliction of these systems at scale and concurrent with traditional air
operations will challenge existing TTPs and have the potential to overwhelm current ACS
capabilities. Detailed planning and the correct balance of airspace control resources and
weight of effort is crucial to the integration of these capabilities in support of JFC objectives.

COORDINATION MEASURES

CMs are employed to facilitate planning and efficient execution of military operations and fires
while simultaneously providing safeguards for friendly forces. The JFC uses CMs to integrate
and deconflict users and uses of airspace to allow aircraft and fires to operate within, or transit
through the air domain. Centralized airspace planning and coordination are essential to
facilitate joint force operations.

The ACA, in conjunction with component and multinational partners, develops airspace
control procedures and associated CMs for JFC approval and publication in the ACP and
ACO. Once approved, CMs are promulgated to joint and multinational forces via the ACO and
SPINS. Their implementation should include consideration of risks associated with fires and
air operations. CMs in the ACO provide a physical description of the operational area.
Commanders use CMs to assign responsibilities, synchronize forces, impose restrictions, or
establish guidelines to regulate airspace use among various users. Categories include:

 Airspace coordinating measures (ACM). Measures employed to facilitate the efficient
    use of airspace to accomplish missions and simultaneously provide safeguards for friendly
    forces.

 Air defense measures (ADM). Restrictive measures employed to facilitate identification,
    detection, and tracking to engage enemy air and missile threats as directed by the AADC.

 Air reference measures (ARM). Measures, to aid C2 coordination, used to define a point
    over the ground or a volume of airspace for reference. (Does not require coordination to
    pass over or through).

 Air traffic control measures (ATCM). A measure established by civil (including HN) or
    military ATC for expeditious and safe movements of aircraft. ATCMs are directive and
    restrictive and are established in conjunction with civil and military ACA and International
    Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), as appropriate.

                       CHAPTER 3: COMMAND AND CONTROL

Airmen, in conjunction with joint and multinational partners, are responsible for planning and
integrating the ACS IAW JFC guidance. The ACS should maximize the combat effectiveness
of all forces while reducing the risk of friendly fire incidents and collateral damage.

JOINT FORCE COMMANDER

The JFC is responsible for military-directed airspace control within the JOA. Executed by the
ACA through the ACS, airspace control procedures and authorities within the JOA are codified
in the ACP and ACO. These documents express how airspace is to be used to support
mission accomplishment and establish airspace priorities among users (including non-military
airspace users) and missions.

COMMANDER, AIR FORCE FORCES

The Commander, Air Force Forces (COMAFFOR) provides the USAF theater air control
system (TACS) and airspace control expertise and resources to the JFC. Normally, the
COMAFFOR has the preponderance of airspace control resources and the capacity to C2
joint air operations via the Joint AOC (JAOC) or other distributed C2 entities. When the
COMAFFOR is designated as the JFACC, ACA, and AADC, the TACS typically forms the
nucleus of the ACS for the JOA. If JFACC, ACA, and/or AADC are designated from another
component, the COMAFFOR may still be expected to provide the TACS.8

JOINT FORCE AIR COMPONENT COMMANDER

The JFC normally designates a JFACC to establish unity of command for joint air operations.
The JFACC is typically also designated as the ACA and AADC due to the close coordination
required by these three functions. JFCs normally designate the commander with the
preponderance of taskable forces and the ability to C2 joint air operations as the JFACC.
Vesting JFACC, ACA, and AADC roles in one commander ensures unity of effort for air
operations within the theater of operations.

AIRSPACE CONTROL AUTHORITY

The ACA is typically the commander with the preponderance of airspace control and
management capabilities in the JOA and who has been delegated overall responsibility for
airspace control.9 The ACA provides and operates an effective and adaptive ACS. In addition,
the ACA plans, coordinates, and develops airspace control procedures for all military airspace
users and airspace control elements operating in the JOA. The ACA does not have the

AREA AIR DEFENSE COMMANDER

The AADC is the component commander with the preponderance of AMD capabilities and
the required command, control, and communications capabilities assigned by the JFC to plan
and execute integrated air defense operations.10 Because ACA and AADC authorities are so
integral to air operations, and due to the USAF’s ability to C2 these activities, the JFACC is
normally assigned both ACA and AADC responsibilities.

The AADC is responsible for DCA operations. The AADC develops the AADP with JFC
approval and ensures it is promulgated. The AADC should identify specific volumes of
airspace and CMs that support and enhance DCA operations, identify required airspace
management systems, establish procedures for systems to operate within the airspace, and
ensure they are incorporated into the ACS. The AADC may also designate regional or sector
air defense commanders (RADCs/SADCs) to facilitate C2 of airspace based on the size and

operation of all available air, land, and maritime-based AMD systems. Chapter 5 of this

functions.

In joint operations where ACA and AADC authorities are assigned to different commanders,
close coordination is essential for unity of effort, prevention of friendly fire and unintended
engagements against civil and neutral aircraft, and joint air operations deconfliction.

                                                National Airspace

    International law recognizes two types of airspace—national and international.
    National airspace is any airspace subject to the sovereignty of a State. This includes
    airspace above the territorial seas, internal waters, archipelagic waters (for
    archipelagic States), and land territory of a State. Consistent with international law,
    the US Government recognizes territorial sea claims up to a maximum distance of 12
    nautical miles from a coastal State’s baseline drawn IAW international law.
    International airspace is not subject to the sovereignty of a State. This includes all
    airspace seaward of coastal States’ national airspace, including airspace over
    contiguous zones, exclusive economic zones, and the high seas. International
    airspace is governed by international law and treaty (e.g., Chicago Convention) and
    may further be governed by international agreements. See Department of Defense
    Instruction (DoDI) 4540.01, Use of International Airspace by U.S. Military Aircraft and
    for Missile and Projectile Firings, and DoD Law of War Manual, for additional
    information.

The ACS is designed to meet the needs of a wide variety of airspace users (e.g., civil, and
military ATC, military aircraft, and indirect fires). Flexibility and simplicity should be
emphasized to maximize the effectiveness of forces operating within the system. Airspace
control should be capable of supporting 24-hour operations in all weather and environmental
conditions. As it is likely to be a prime target of attack, it should be integrated, survivable,
sustainable, and redundant. The design, responsiveness, and procedures of the ACS
employed in the combat zone should support the rapid massing of combat power, including
emerging systems such as small UAS and loitering munitions. Therefore, the structure of the
ACS should be responsive to emerging threats and the evolution of ongoing operations.
Emphasis should be placed on simple, flexible airspace management procedures. Provisions
should allow for control capability degradation. In this manner, flexibility and battlespace
responsiveness are preserved.

ACS FUNDAMENTALS

A common ACS facilitates accurate and timely coordination of airspace requirements among
friendly forces.12 Common equipment, understanding of Service and joint doctrine, and
familiarity with procedures through joint exercises and training can enhance airspace control
within an AOR or JOA. Standardized airspace procedures rely on a mix of identification and
control measures. Airspace control identification requirements should be integrated with
those for AMD. Airspace control, AMD, ATC, supporting C2 procedures, equipment, and
terminology should be compatible, mutually supporting, and integrated to enable common
procedures for airspace users and control agencies. Airspace control agencies should
establish agreements and communication links to facilitate coordination.

The ACA normally executes airspace control via distributed elements of the ACS. The ACA
distributes an ACP designating joint force component airspace control elements to be part of
the ACS. These elements may be required to provide ICAO-approved traffic and separation
standards. In this regard, ACS elements may be governed by HN rules and regulations
pertaining to airspace control during military operations.

However, as operations transition between peacetime and combat operations, peacetime
airspace rules and organizations change. These changes vary with some ACS elements
executing dual roles for air battle management and airspace control of assets. For example,
a CRC, tasked with airspace control duties, may also be a RADC/SADC responsible for a
portion of theater AMD.

The ACS requires the timely exchange of information through reliable, secure, and
interoperable communications networks. ACS systems include, but are not limited to,
sensors, communications, data processing, and common operating databases. It is critical that
these systems are protected by cybersecurity programs such as communications security,
physical security, emissions security, and defensive cyberspace operations. Implementing
operations security practices is imperative when developing communication policies and
procedures.

The ACS’s supporting systems (e.g., satellite ground stations, air navigation, surveillance and
weather radars, and voice radio communications) rely on the EMS. These systems are
susceptible to electromagnetic interference (EMI) from intentional (e.g., frequency jammers)
and unintentional (e.g., friendly transmissions on adjacent frequencies) emissions,

THEATER AIR CONTROL SYSTEM

The TACS is the USAF C2 system by which the JFACC executes joint air operations,
including the tasking of joint air forces made available by other components. It provides the
JFACC with the means to distribute control and decentralize execution of air operations.
TACS elements may be employed in garrison, deployed for contingencies, or deployed to
augment theater-specific systems. Though they may be configured differently across various
theaters, basic functions performed by the TACS are the same. The JFACC/ACA/AADC
executes the ATO, ACP, ACOs, and the AADP via the TACS. As an organic USAF system,
the TACS remains under the operational control (OPCON) of the COMAFFOR.

When the COMAFFOR is designated as the JFACC, the TACS becomes the nucleus of the
TAGS, which incorporates other components and HN airspace control elements. The JFACC
exercises centralized command of joint air operations in pursuit of JFC objectives through the
JAOC.

The TACS is composed of dedicated elements, dynamic elements, and supporting systems.
Dedicated elements include both airborne and ground-based battle management units.
Dynamic TACS elements are any other weapon system or entity that has a primary role other
than C2 but may, with appropriate certification or delegated authority, augment, or become a
substitute for dedicated TACS elements. Support systems include organic or opportunistic
surveillance, communications, and decision-making capabilities that improve and extend
TACS capabilities. To fully understand the relationship of the TACS to the ACS, planners
should know each component's system, composition, and structure.14

Air Operations Center. The AOC is the senior C2 element of the TACS where centralized
planning, direction, control, and coordination of assigned air forces occur. An AOC is
organized under a commander, whereas a combined or joint AOC (C/JAOC) is organized
under a director (for joint forces) and a commander (for USAF forces). The AOC commander

divisions: strategy division, combat plans division, combat operations division, intelligence,
surveillance and reconnaissance (ISR) division, and air mobility division, along with multiple
support and specialty teams. Each division integrates numerous disciplines in a cross-
functional team approach to planning, execution, and assessment.15

In the AOC, airspace management expertise is organic to the combat plans and combat
operations divisions. Additional airspace management expertise may reside in the strategy or
air mobility divisions, multiple support and specialty teams, and other Service or functional
component liaisons (e.g., battlefield coordination detachment [BCD], naval and amphibious
liaison element [NALE], and special operations liaison element [SOLE]).

manage real time AOC airspace coordination issues per AOD prioritized objectives and tasks.

change to enact required CMs. In such cases, airspace control elements should be prepared
to interface with the AOC and airspace users to deconflict CMs with short notice and in real

OTHER USAF ACS ELEMENTS

WING OPERATIONS CENTER (WOC)

An installation’s WOC provides a standardized, functional organization to facilitate
installation-level C2 across the spectrum of operations for all wing-assigned units and
organizations. The WOC provides a single, consolidated C2 center to monitor mission
execution of installation commander assigned or supported missions, including tenant, joint,
and combined missions. It interfaces with the AOC and Air Force forces staff and is the key
C2 center that connects operational planning with tactical execution.

The WOC is scalable and tailorable at the installation commander’s discretion to provide the
necessary C2 capability required for the unique location, mission, and operational situation.
As a baseline, it consists of the following functional areas: operations control, maintenance
coordination, aerial port coordination, reports, and incident response (including base defense
and force protection). It provides experts to receive, schedule, plan, and direct execution of
the ATO. When required, the WOC can connect with TACS elements and is capable of
coordinating with HN representatives, tenant organizations, and joint and multinational forces
as required.

In addition to the command post function, the WOC may include provisions for a battle staff,
mission planning, operations planning and execution monitoring, maintenance operations, a
logistics readiness center, and an emergency operations center. It is linked to group and
squadron unit control centers, on-base support facilities, and off-base C2 nodes, including
major command C2 centers, USAF component headquarters, AOC, and civil emergency
operations centers.

With appropriately delegated authorities, coupled with the capability and capacity to execute
the C2 process (i.e., plan, prepare, execute, and assess air operations), a wing commander
exercising C2 via a WOC may be considered a “Distributed Control” node in CC-DC-DE
Mission Command Framework.

The JFACC plans and tasks joint air operations with the TAGS. The JFACC is normally

ARMY AIR-GROUND SYSTEM (AAGS)

The AAGS is a vital part of the Army C2 system that connects to the TACS to enable
air-ground operations. AAGS is the Army’s control system for synchronizing, coordinating,
and integrating air operations with the ground commanders’ operational objectives. The
AAGS provides the framework for initiating and processing component air support requests,
collection requirements, airspace integration, joint fires, AMD, and liaison exchanges. The
combined effort of the AAGS and TACS enhances air-ground operations for their components
by increasing situational awareness and understanding of both air and land operations.

COMPOSITE WARFARE COMMANDER/NAVY TACTICAL AIR CONTROL SYSTEM
(CWC/NTACS)

Naval aviation doctrine is distinguished from USAF airpower doctrine in that the primary
responsibility of naval airpower is to support the maritime commander. There are two distinct
applications of naval airpower: one, an organic integral extension of naval sea power
employed by the maritime commander, and two, the tactical, operational, and strategic
capabilities under the unified command of a JFACC in support of a JFC. Maritime C2 of naval
air forces is designed to support both applications of naval airpower, integrate allocated joint
air support assets, and align with joint planning and execution systems (such as the joint air
tasking cycle and the TAGS). For example, within the same maritime AOs there may be naval
aircraft supporting an amphibious landing, naval aircraft supporting a war at sea strike against
enemy ships, and other aircraft provided to the JFACC supporting the land component
commander. Each of these separate employments of naval airpower have specific C2
processes that should be integrated. For instance, naval aircraft supporting a war at sea strike
are integrated within the CWC C2 process while naval airpower supporting an amphibious
landing integrate with the amphibious force NTACS.

The NALE is the key point for integration of naval and joint force air operations within the
JAOC. The NALE coordinates airspace requests from naval air control centers with the ACA
for inclusion in the ACO. Once approved, naval control centers (e.g., Naval tactical air control
center [NTACC], air resource element coordinator [AREC], supporting arms control center
[SACC]) conduct battle management of allocated and assigned air assets in support of
component and joint force objectives.

SPECIAL OPERATIONS AIR-GROUND SYSTEM (SOAGS)

Special operations (SO) air forces are typically organic to the joint special operations
component commander’s (JFSOCC) formation. SO air assets are inherently joint and can be
employed in concert with, or independently from, conventional force (CF) operations. In any
case, SO integration with the TAGS requires extensive planning and coordination. SO may
provide their own air support, require CF air support, or make SO air assets available for use
by CF. The SOAGS is scalable and tailorable depending on the mission and JFC
requirements. The principal functions supporting SO air operations within the SOAGS may
include a joint special operations air component commander (JSOACC), special operations
task groups/units (SOTG/SOTU), SOLE, Special Tactics Teams (STT), SO Joint Fires
Element (JFE), and JTACs.

The SOLE within the JAOC is the key point of integration for SO and CF air operations. The
SOLE coordinates airspace requests from SOAGS elements with the ACA for inclusion in the
ACO, such as special operations force areas. The SOLE may also coordinate CAS and time-
sensitive target support (in support of SO forces or when SO aircraft support CF), facilitate
airspace deconfliction actions, and coordinate ISR requirements for the SO component. In
cases where CF air assets are required to support SO, the JFACC may be required to
establish a capability similar to an ASOC at the SO task force level to facilitate C2 of CF air
assets.

                   CHAPTER 4: AIRSPACE CONTROL PLANNING

Commanders, planning staffs, and airspace control professionals should be involved from the
outset in planning and executing C2, ATC, and airspace management. This ensures airspace
requirements are coordinated and approved by the proper agencies.

GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS

Airspace control planning begins well before the onset of armed conflict. Airspace control
professionals should develop understanding, partnerships, and integrated processes and
procedures to facilitate cooperation and transitions between civil and military ACA in each
JOA. Furthermore, such planning should be adaptable and scalable to account for various
airspace control scenarios and requirements.

Establishing relationships with the host and key neighboring nations’ ATC organizations is
critical. Planners should consider establishing an ATC cell to liaise with HN ATC
representatives. Establishing an aircraft diplomatic clearance process should be
accomplished as early as possible during the planning process. Key items include:

 Identifying key personnel and their contact information.

 Identifying existing agreements (e.g., AIPs and site surveys).

 Identifying rules, regulations, and existing international, multilateral, or bilateral
    agreements or arrangements governing proposed operations (e.g., ICAO, Federal
    Aviation Administration (FAA), regional organization, or HNs). For planning purposes, this
    information is in the DoD Foreign Clearance Guide, DoD Directive 4500.54E, DoD Foreign
    Clearance Program. Planners and operators should consult with their servicing legal
    advisors to ensure active international agreements are reflected in DoD guidance.

 Identifying special operating rules or waivers needed for certain aircraft or operations
    within HN airspace (e.g., rules for unmanned aircraft system [UAS]).

 Establishing requirements to integrate liaison officers, equipment, processes, and
    functions.

To preserve and maximize airpower’s inherent speed, range, and flexibility, potential ACS
modifications should be anticipated, considered, and planned for across the competition
continuum and during all operational phases (i.e., shape, deter, seize the initiative, stabilize,
and enable civil authority) to enable joint and combined force effectiveness. The ACP should
be consistent with and support specific operation plans (OPLANs) and operation orders
(OPORDs) developed by the JFC and component commanders. Planning for airspace control
should ensure control responsibilities, activities, systems, documents, and liaison
requirements are aligned across the range of actions associated with a typical major operation
or campaign. Typically, operational phasing delineates a shift in the operation’s nature, focus,
or objective. Associated shifts in airspace control requirements should be accounted for and
planned.

Transferring airspace control from civil to military control, adapting it to JFC or HN needs
throughout the transition from competition to armed conflict, and eventually returning it to civil
authority are complex tasks requiring joint military, diplomatic, and interagency efforts. The

JOINT PLANNING PROCESS (JPP)

The JPP is an orderly, analytical process that consists of logical steps to analyze a mission,
select the best course of action (COA), and produce a campaign or joint operation plan or
order. To enable joint air operations, support JFC plans, and account for joint, HN, and civil
airspace requirements, airspace control planners should be integrated throughout the JPP.

JOINT PLANNING PROCESS FOR AIR (JPPA)

The JPPA is an air-centric application of the JPP used by the JFACC to guide the employment
of joint air capabilities. The JPPA drives the production of the JFACC’s joint air operation plan
(JAOP), COMAFFOR’s operation order, and other plans and orders. Joint airspace control
planning is normally conducted using the JPPA. The JPPA is ideally suited for Airmen at or in
concert with the JAOC.25

AIR FORCE PLANNING PROCESS (AFPP)

The AFPP is a planning method developed for use by all USAF units. Airmen employ AFPP
when the situation and mission do not necessitate planning for the joint force or joint air
operations. Localized airspace control planning may be accomplished using the AFPP.26

CONSIDERATIONS FOR OPERATIONS DURING COOPERATION AND COMPETITION

In addition to ensuring the continuation of routine DoD flight operations, USAF airspace
planners should establish effective relationships with key airspace authorities in the AOR,
work with the ACA to develop specific ACPs in preparation for future operations and build
airspace planning expertise. Regular DoD or joint force interaction with HN authorities and
participation in regional airspace conferences establishes relationships with the HN. This can
speed issue resolution, enable effective coordination of airspace requirements, and facilitate
deliberate crisis planning in the event of armed conflict.

JOA-specific airspace control expertise, for ACS design and establishment of procedures, is
as crucial for operations in cooperation and competition phases as in any other operation.
Airspace managers should receive formal training before arriving at a USAF component
headquarters or AOC, preferably at the AOC formal training unit. Additionally, exercises may
provide key opportunities for airspace control planners to practice joint C2 procedures and
familiarize themselves with the basic operations plan. Bilateral or regional exercises with host
and partner nations provide opportunities to improve cooperation with, and understanding of,
HN airspace control agencies and capabilities.

ACPs should integrate known international or HN ATC and AMD capabilities. Primary
planning considerations include identification of airspace required for joint force operations
and the proposed coordination process for obtaining that airspace. Joint planning should
consider procedures to transfer civil airspace control from the HN to the joint force and the
ACA. This includes rerouting of airways, ACA responsibilities for continuity of civil aviation
operations, and HN notification of ACA areas of control through notices to Airmen or AIP
entries.

CONSIDERATIONS FOR TRANSITION TO ARMED CONFLICT

Prior to initiating large-scale combat operations, the JFC should ensure a fully operational
TAGS is in place. TACS and other ACS elements should be able to move with surface
elements, and since airspace control is a critical C2 function, elements of the TAGS and ACS
should be given appropriate movement priority. Once combat operations commence, a
reliable and jam-resistant communications system should be available to cover the entire
JOA. While the JFACC/COMAFFOR (in the ACA role) is normally responsible for most of this
system, other Service component contributions can significantly increase its capability. The
ACP, ACO, ATO, and SPINS should be updated, including responsibilities and authorities
(including special operations and multinational forces) for the following:

 Unassigned areas within the joint force land component commander’s (JFLCC) AO.

 Procedures for forward operating bases and airfields.

 DATCALS, ATC, and air traffic service at forward operating bases.

 ATC or air traffic service at captured airfields.

 Placement of the CRC, Marine tactical AOC, and AMD radars to provide coverage and
    sharing of coverage pictures, if required.

 Area AMD and short-range air defense integration behind the FSCL and forward line of
    own troops (FLOT).

 UAS (all categories), loitering munitions, and rotary wing deconfliction methods.

 Continued integration of the ASOC, TACPs, and DASC with the rest of the ACS.

OPERATIONS AND RISK

Joint doctrine recognizes each Service and functional component’s need to maximize the
availability and use of airspace within JFC-established acceptable levels of risk. For all
airspace users—including fires—risk acceptance criteria should be delineated in the ACP,
including a risk matrix that is agreed upon by components before commencing operations. At
a minimum, this matrix should include the types of risk shown in Appendix B, Notional JFC
Airspace Control Risk Guidance Matrix.

In general terms, low-risk acceptance by the JFC prioritizes resource preservation. Moderate
risk acceptance by the JFC seeks to balance mission accomplishment with resource
preservation. Lastly, high-risk acceptance prioritizes mission accomplishment above all other
concerns. The assumption of risk is a command decision. Airspace control planners should
assess risk throughout the planning process. Upon approval by the JFC, the ACP risk
guidance matrix translates risk assessments into specific criteria that inform commanders
where risk factors may exist, provide mitigation measures, and when to apply them. Risk
guidance for civil aviation (if present in the JOA) should be added to the matrix.

Control procedures should be modified to keep pace with increases in airspace users and
keep operations within JFC acceptable risk levels. The ACP should specify areas where high
numbers of airspace users are projected and plan for increased control capability.

COMMUNICATIONS AND INFORMATION

Though airspace C2 infrastructures have not changed significantly over time, most
communications networks are significantly improved, enhancing the reliability, security, and
timeliness of information. Tactical information that may have terminated with an airborne C2
element or CRC is now passed to the AOC, enhancing situational awareness. In addition,
broad-bandwidth communications using spacecraft and internet protocol communications
have substantially increased ACS C2 coverage and reach-back capabilities. Along with newer
technologies, secure voice systems (ultra-high, very high, and high frequencies) offer reliable
means of communication. Such technologies provide the JFACC with a variety of options to
execute airspace control. Despite advances in communications technology, JFACCs should
plan for degradation, intermittency, or complete loss of communication with other C2
elements, particularly in CDO-L environments.

DIGITALLY ENABLED OPERATIONS

TDLs are standardized communication links suitable for the transmission of digital information
between C2 nodes and link-equipped aircraft. Each Service uses these links for situational
awareness to support battle management, C2, and combat airspace management. Link-
equipped TACS airspace control elements, including AMD nodes, leverage TDLs to enhance
the situational awareness and effectiveness of the ACS.

Gateways. Gateways translate and forward tactical data to facilitate interoperability and
information exchange among disparate TDL systems. With the debut of ASOC Gateway
machines, ASOCs have increased situational awareness and the means to digitally
communicate combat airspace information to compatibly equipped aircraft.

Joint Interface Control Officer (JICO). The JICO works with the JAOC’s plans division, C2
elements, and component liaisons to develop and publish the theater’s integrated data link
architecture and operations guidance. Circumstances may require the delegation of some
data link management functions to lower echelons. CJCSI 6610.01F, Tactical Data Link
Standardization and Interoperability, provides a detailed description of data links. AFTTP 3-
2.17, MTTP for the Theater Air-Ground System (TAGS), and AFTTP 3-2.31, MTTP for AMD,
provide further information on data links and the key role they play in enabling effective
airspace control and AMD.

COMMUNICATIONS PLANNING

Comprehensive communications enable airspace control throughout a theater. Its availability
cannot be taken for granted or treated as a foregone conclusion. Detailed, deliberate
planning, including radar and radio signal analysis, ensures surveillance and communications
systems provide appropriate coverage within the airspace by employing a combination of
fixed and mobile systems. TACS communication capabilities include line of sight, beyond line
of sight, and spacecraft-based systems. Principal transmissions should be through secure
and jam-resistant radio equipment. Planners should ensure radio relays are considered to
enhance over-the-horizon radio communications.

Networking technologies may also increase the capabilities of C2 nets and net enabled
communication to disseminate information. However, planners should consider the unique
requirements, specific configurations, protocols, and tactics, techniques, and procedures
(TTPs) to ensure the availability, reliability, resiliency, and effectiveness of these capabilities.
Specific procedures for the use of internet relay chat, or other non-voice communication
systems, should be included in the ACP. If communications cannot be secured through
technical means, procedures to meet operations security requirements should be applied to
unsecure communications.

A detailed analysis of joint network and joint infrastructure requirements is crucial to enabling
systems integration across components and allied operational capabilities. These needs are
normally met by installing a combination of organic and commercial communications systems
prioritized to meet mission requirements, maximize use of military capabilities, increase
capacity and reliability, and generate greater freedom of action. Communications planners
should perform an operations security vulnerability analysis to determine procedures that
protect information from exploitation.

ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM (EMS)

Airspace control relies on equipment using the EMS (e.g., PNT systems, radio navigation,
ATC radars, weather radars, and voice). The use of spectrum-dependent technologies is
constrained by frequency availability and EMS congestion. The rapid growth of sophisticated
weapons systems, as well as increasing bandwidth requirements for intelligence, operations,
and communications systems, greatly increases the demand for EMS access. Lack of proper,
pre-planned EMS coordination and consideration of environmental electromagnetic effects
may have an adverse effect on the safe, efficient, and flexible use of airspace. As an additional
consideration, international law protects a nation’s sovereign right to utilize and regulate the
EMS within borders. Generally, HN approval (and potentially frequency clearances and
landing rights) is required before conducting EMS operations in foreign locations.

CONTINGENCY PLANNING

The operation plan serves as the foundational employment concept for an operation and its
associated AO(s). The JFC’s plan typically involves some form of phasing, moving from one
to the next as an operation progresses and achieves necessary objectives towards an end
state. The JFC may follow the traditional six phase model (i.e., shape, deter, seize the
initiative, dominate, stabilize, transition to civil control), modify it, alter it entirely, or adapt it to
the situation as required. In any case, airspace planners should account for unique airspace
control requirements for each phase, anticipating operations may not transition smoothly
between phases or phases may overlap.
Operations may start, stop, or transition unexpectedly along the competition continuum
depending on the operation’s nature, strategic objectives, and JFC intent. Transferring ACA
from civilian to military control, adapting the ACS to the JFC’s needs during each phase, and
eventually returning an ACS to civil authority are complex tasks requiring joint military,
diplomatic, and interagency efforts. Since a crisis may occur unexpectedly, airspace control
and management activities should be a part of planning from the beginning. For instance,
moving ACS equipment (e.g., CRCs or ATC facilities) is a time-phased force and deployment
data consideration. Since much of this equipment is subject to airlift or other mobility
constraints, a comprehensive plan is required to ensure critical ACS capabilities are available
at the appropriate time and phase of the operation.

                  CHAPTER 5: AIRSPACE CONTROL EXECUTION

Airspace control execution supports JFC operations, activities, and investments across the
competition continuum. However, the composition of the ACS and locus of ACA may vary
widely as military force is employed through cooperation, competition, and conflict. The ACP
and the ACS should be flexible and adaptable to changing environments, objectives, and
circumstances while facilitating transitions between operations within JFC notional
operational phases (e.g., shape, deter, seize the initiative, dominate, stabilize, and enable
civil authority).

COOPERATE

Shaping activities are designed to achieve objectives by creating perceptions and influencing
the behavior of adversaries and allies, developing allied and friendly military capabilities for
self-defense and coalition operations, improving information exchange and intelligence
sharing, and providing US forces with peacetime and contingency airspace access. Airspace
control shaping activities during cooperation are characterized by normal military
airspace activities designed to deter adversaries and ensure or solidify relationships
with allies and partners. Shaping entails various joint, multinational, and interagency
airspace activities that are executed to enhance international legitimacy and gain cooperation
in support of defined military and national strategic objectives.

During the shaping phase, airspace control and aerial navigation services are likely to remain
under the sovereign control of the HN. In such cases, the JFC may appoint a lead agent to
coordinate airspace control issues with the joint force and HN instead of an ACA. The lead
agent is typically the commander with the preponderance of air assets and the ability to C2
within the theater.30

Proper coordination with civil air operations is important during transitions into or out of
wartime status or during non-wartime periods of heightened tensions. Political constraints,
national and military ACS and procedures, and the capabilities and limitations of these
systems are important considerations in planning for joint force airspace control. Applicable
information from the ACP should be distributed to joint and multinational forces as well as
HNs, allies, and international organizations such as ICAO.

In addition to building JOA airspace expertise and establishing effective relationships with
allies and partners, airspace planners should develop specific ACPs in preparation for future
operations during the shaping phase. Planners should assess and integrate HN ATC,
airspace control, and AMD capabilities into the ACP and identify airspace required for future
joint operations, along with the procedures for obtaining access to that airspace. Airspace
planning should also anticipate and establish procedures for the transition of ACA from the
HN to the joint force.

COMPETE

While the shaping phase of a joint operation entails normal activities may have deterrent
value, deterrence operations typically initiate upon the issuance of an OPLAN or OPORD and
entail activities designed to demonstrate joint force capabilities and resolve to prevent

ARMED CONFLICT/WAR

The JFC's initiative or an enemy attack can trigger the transition from adversarial competition
to armed conflict. Airspace rules and organizations are altered during combat operations, and
the ACP should provide clear instructions for transitioning from peacetime to combat,
including airspace control concepts and procedures for potentially degraded operations.
Airspace planners should be involved in the development of the MAAP to enable adequate
airspace to be allocated for combat operations and an effective transition from peacetime to
combat airspace control.

At the start of combat operations, civil aviation operations should be excluded from the
affected JOA. Notifying adversaries of impending changes to airspace control should be
avoided, so the timing for airspace transition to potential combat should be carefully
considered. Advanced or open notification of airspace changes should be integrated into
information operations for operations security or military deception purposes.

During the initial stages of combat, effective airspace control requires an understanding of
the OPLAN, JFC intent, airspace environment, and requirements for effective control. The
JFACC is typically the supported commander for the theater-wide counterair effort, integrating
OCA and DCA (as the AADC) to gain control of the air. More control of the air domain may
be necessary for simultaneous operations against enemies with a credible AMD threat, and
efforts to achieve control of the air may progress sequentially in the early stages.

Operating airborne systems with limited identification and communications equipment can
increase the risk of enemy air and missile attacks and friendly fire incidents. The ACP may
restrict friendly military and civil airspace users until air superiority is achieved. Specific
airspace measures are required for mission-essential systems with limited identification or
autonomous sense-and-avoid capability.

Once the desired degree of control of the air is achieved, the JFC may allow increased
operations by airborne systems with limited identification and communications capabilities. A
theater-wide common operational picture (COP) can contribute significantly to AMD and
airspace control. The JFC's ACP may employ fewer restrictions on certain systems visible on
the airspace COP. Other systems with less capable identification and communications
equipment may require more restrictions and coordination.

of the TACS and NTACS should accomplish C2 functions until the full TAGS is established.

INTEGRATION WITH JOINT FIRE SUPPORT

Planning airspace control for combat operations should fully integrate fires from all friendly
and multinational forces. Failure to integrate fires in initial planning significantly increases the
potential for friendly fire incidents and may delay execution or decrease the effectiveness of
combat operations. Early integration of airspace and fires prevents conflicts that result when
two or more components plan to use the same airspace, at the same time, for different
missions. Airspace managers and airspace control elements should coordinate with the
JFLCC’s senior fires element, naval gunfire liaison company, SOF, and cruise missile
planners to identify conflicting airspace requirements and quantify risk.

The JFACC, working with the JFC and other components, should identify the risk of combining
fires and aircraft operations (manned and unmanned). Such concerns are heightened during
the initial stages when airspace is typically congested by a higher density of air and land
operations. These risks should be codified in the ACP, along with the level of risk the JFC is
willing to accept for each phase. The ACP should establish guidelines for procedural control
and coordination of fires. These procedures should also include real-time deconfliction
measures for missions in the ATO, ACO, and SPINS.

INTEGRATION WITH INDIRECT FIRES

During large-scale combat operations, airspace control elements should expect a significant
increase in indirect fires in the JFLCC and JFMCC AOs. Indirect fire systems are considered
airspace users and should be integrated with other airspace users to meet air and ground
component commander objectives and avoid friendly fire incidents. Airspace planners should
minimize the number of CMs (especially FSCM) so as not to overly restrict fires. In the same
manner, fires planners should understand there are some areas in which the JFC cannot
accept the risk of mixing fires and aircraft. When possible, fires planning should be done early
to enable air planners to build their routes around planned restricted airspace due to surface-
to-surface missile systems (SSMS) and other FSCMs. Numerous deconfliction measures are
available to address these issues and generally involve some sort of spatial or temporal
separation of fires and aircraft, allowing both to operate simultaneously without undue risk.

INTEGRATION OF EXPEDITIONARY AIRFIELDS

As an operation flows through its various phases, expeditionary airfields normally open and
close as forces reposition. These airfield changes should be integrated and synchronized with
ongoing airspace control and RAMCC procedures. An AFFOR airfield operations cell
normally stands up to facilitate the opening of new airfields. Their key actions include
installation of required airfield systems, sourcing of personnel, and the development and
inspection of flight procedures. The timely establishment of all-weather instrument procedures
is crucial for base logistics and operations.34

INTEGRATION OF UNMANNED AIRCRAFT (UA)

UA deconfliction is critical in all phases and requires detailed planning and coordination. UA
compete for airspace with manned aircraft, particularly in the vicinity of high-value targets. To
minimize risk and maximize the effectiveness of UA, ACP and SPINS should direct the
deconfliction of service, joint, and multinational UA platforms operating at all altitudes. Unlike
their manned counterparts, the vast majority of UA do not have onboard sense-and-avoid
capabilities. Therefore, other means of aircraft separation should be employed to reduce the
risk of mid-air collisions.

complete. However, continued joint force support and involvement with the HN and other

desired end state.

             CHAPTER 6: HOMELAND OPERATIONS AND THE LAW

The US homeland represents a complex political-military environment different from any other
CCDR’s AOR. Within the US, the FAA is granted statutory authority over managing airspace
by Title 49, § 106 of the U.S. Code (USC), FAA. The administrator establishes security
provisions to encourage and maximize the use of navigable airspace by civil aircraft
consistent with national security and in consultation with the Secretary of Defense (SecDef).
In the interest of national defense, the administrator establishes airspace areas by regulation
or order and restricts or prohibits certain flights of civil aircraft in those areas. Title 5, USC., §
551-559, Administrative Procedure, requires public notice before the FAA can carry out
certain airspace management actions, including military actions. However, the DoD may
direct and implement emergency security control of air traffic in certain specified
circumstances IAW Title 32, Code of Federal Regulations, Part 245, Plan for the Emergency
Security Control of Air Traffic (ESCAT). Additionally, during wartime, the President may
transfer FAA responsibilities to the DoD IAW Title 49 USC, § 40107, Presidential Transfers,
and Executive Order 11161, as amended.

NORAD, USNORTHCOM, CONR, AND AFNORTH

Complicating the FAA’s control of US airspace, two separate military commands share the
responsibility of defending the US. The operational missions of NORAD and USNORTHCOM
and its assigned AOR overlap within the homeland. The continental US NORAD region
(CONR) and 1st Air Force (Air Forces Northern [AFNORTH]) combine to function as part of
the US TACS.37 Additionally, AFNORTH works with other service components and
commands collaborating within a civilian interagency environment composed of government
and civilian organizations (e.g., FAA, Federal Emergency Management Agency, US Secret
Service), to execute air missions prior to and after crises or disasters. Unity of effort is
achieved by ensuring a clear division of geography and labor with a spirit of mutual support
and cooperation among these commands and the National Guard.38

NATIONAL GUARD

The National Guard normally operates under the command authority of a state’s governor
and the Adjutant General in full-time National Guard duty status (Title 32 USC, National
Guard). AFNORTH uses an approved legal model based on memoranda of understanding
with governors to receive ANG volunteers for short notice federal missions. AFNORTH
achieves unity of effort with ANG leadership by providing air component planning,
deconfliction, and coordinating capabilities to state ANG organizations through the AOC. This
coordination also provides the USNORTHCOM commander and the JFACC (usually the
AFNORTH commander) situational awareness of the affected air, space, and cyberspace
domains even before National Guard units are federalized under authority of Title 10, USC,
Armed Forces.

A1. SCOPE: Information in this plan does not replace airfield or airspace local operating

A2. DEFINITION OF AIRSPACE CONTROL

A2.1. OBJECTIVE: To enhance air, land, maritime, and special operations force effectiveness
in accomplishing joint force commander (JFC) objectives. This is accomplished with the
maximum allowable freedom to airspace users consistent with the JFC's airspace control risk
guidance. Airspace control includes coordinating, integrating, and regulating airspace to
increase operational effectiveness, however, the airspace control authority (ACA) does not
have the authority to approve, disapprove, or deny combat operations.

A2.2. TYPES OF AIRSPACE CONTROL: Control of airspace will be accomplished by two
primary means: procedural control and positive control.

A2.2.1. Procedural control is a method of airspace control which relies on a combination of
previously agreed on and promulgated orders and procedures.

A2.2.2. Positive control is a method of airspace control that relies on positive identification,
tracking and direction of aircraft within an airspace, conducted with electronic means by an
agency having the authority and responsibility therein.

  APPENDIX C: REGIONAL AIR MOVEMENT CONTROL/COORDINATION
                                              CENTER

Safe operations in the airspace control area may require coordination among a multitude of
users and air traffic facilities, beyond those the AOC normally directs or considers in an ATO,
ACO, or ACP. Non-AOC directed users may include military airlift, special operations, and
other Services, along with United Nations or other peacekeepers, humanitarian relief
organizations, HN, or multinational aircraft, and scheduled commercial air services. Many of
these users frequently transit airfields in the airspace control area.

After initial military strikes, combat, combat sustainment, and non-AOC directed airlift
missions may increase dramatically. Civil entities conducting humanitarian or commercial air
operations may further congest airspace and airfields. This can lead to increased hazardous
air traffic reports and increased airfield operational complexity that undermine the operation
unless integrated air traffic management measures are implemented.

The RAMCC42 is typically established to help prevent congestion and airspace conflicts when
significant military forces operate in an area with an inadequate ATC infrastructure. Under
these circumstances, the ACA deconflicts aircraft directed by the joint or combined AOC with
other aircraft participating in the contingency or during disaster relief operations (e.g., United
Nations, Red Cross, NGOs, and commercial operators). This becomes even more critical
when joint force air operations are conducted in an ICAO flight information region within a
sovereign nation. Additionally, the RAMCC may prove useful in the transition of airspace back
to civil authorities and may remain for several years after a conflict.

The RAMCC serves a function distinct from the combat plans division or the air mobility
division by dealing with a wider range of users, deconflicting terminal operations and
sometimes being involved in current operations (e.g., en route deconfliction). Its members
work closely with AOC divisions, but its members are not spread across them.

The ACA may elect to establish airfield and airspace scheduling, granting authority to the
RAMCC to issue or deny slot times at airfields and control points to deconflict aircraft in the
airspace control area or ICAO flight information region. Slot times are normally based on an
assessment of an airfield’s limitations (onload-offload rate, parking capacity) and, as
applicable, ATC separation capability. With RAMCC scheduling in effect, all US military
aircraft operating in the control area should participate and adhere to slot times to ensure
adequate margins of safety. Slot times also aid in managing ramp capacity at theater airfields
by ensuring the maximum capacity for parking aircraft at an airfield or the maximum on ground
capability for aircraft is not exceeded.

RAMCC is the centralized information source providing the AADC with the identity of civil and
other military aircraft operating in the control area. Despite gaining procedural control through
these advanced scheduling schemes, most aircraft movements are still conducted under
visual flight rules (i.e., “see and avoid”), unless ATC separation is instituted.

42 Different DoD doctrine publications define the RAMCC as both a “control center” as well as a “coordination
center.” In the interest of multinational relations and to prevent concerns regarding ownership and sovereignty
of foreign airspace, overseas RAMCCs typically use the “coordination center” nomenclature.

ORGANIZATION
The RAMCC organization is typically subdivided into long-range plans, current operations,
airfield operations, and a mission support section. The long-range planning cell manages
activities beyond 24 hours of execution including military and civilian users’ arrivals and
departures at airfields as well as aircraft transiting through the ACA-controlled airspace. The
RAMCC director is normally a senior officer (O-6) because of the scope of responsibility and
seasoned leadership expertise required.
Ideally, the RAMCC should have a wide variety of specialties, along with Service and
multinational representation to reflect the user base of the organization. Specialties include
but are not limited to, rated aircrew, air traffic controllers, transportation and aerial port
specialists, C2 specialists, communication and computer technicians, and administrative
support. Diverse manning during multinational operations allows the RAMCC to execute its
mission as an honest broker, given the broad range of RAMCC customers.
A RAMCC was used in Operations ALLIED FORCE, ENDURING FREEDOM, IRAQI
FREEDOM, and Operation UNIFIED RESPONSE.
