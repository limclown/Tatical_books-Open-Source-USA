                                         FOREWORD

Today's global landscape presents complex security challenges, demanding adaptable
approaches to foreign internal defense (FID). This publication provides the United States
Air Force (USAF) framework for assisting host nations (HNs) against an evolving array of
threats, including subversion, insurgency, and terrorism, by integrating all instruments of
national power.
USAF FID activities support a state's right to maintain internal order and are conducted
with the mutual consent of both the United States (US) and HN governments. The core
principle is working "by, with, and through" our foreign partners to build their security
capacity. All FID operations require appropriate legal authority and a clear exit strategy
that leaves a legitimate government in place with sufficient means to address its own
security threats.
Effective FID necessitates a whole-of-government approach that incorporates diplomatic,
economic, and informational initiatives, not just military intervention. Therefore, Airmen
must understand the full operational environment-including the political, economic, and
cultural factors that influence stability-to ensure our involvement reinforces good
governance and avoids unintended consequences.
While existing security cooperation structures provide a foundation, this doctrine must
evolve to meet emerging challenges. This publication guides innovative Airmen in
embracing adaptability and collaboration to support our partners effectively. By mastering
these principles, Airmen will be prepared to contribute to the success of FID missions and
advance US national security interests.

                             CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

Foreign internal defense (FID). is defined as “participation by the civilian agencies
and military forces of a government or international organizations in any of the
programs or activities undertaken by a host nation’s (HN’s) government to free and
protect its society from subversion, lawlessness, insurgency, violent extremism,
terrorism, and other threats to its security.”1 Though FID has recently been most
visibly employed in counterinsurgency (COIN) efforts, FID is a broad mission involving
varying levels of United States (US)-provided diplomatic, economic, informational, and
military support to enable HN internal defense and development (IDAD) programs.

The National Security Council provides initial strategic guidance for FID, the Department
of State (DoS) is normally designated as the lead agency for execution, and the
Department of War (DoW) provides personnel and equipment to help achieve military FID
objectives. DoW support to FID is typically executed through routine SC activities as part
of a combatant commander’s (CCDR) theater campaign plan.

FID applies across the competition continuum and involves all instruments of national
power. Moving across the thresholds that separate one level of engagement from the next
carries serious legal and policy implications for the US. US Government (USG)
engagement in HN IDAD programs is initiated and advanced through a HN request, a
shared HN and USG goal, or USG proposition to an HN to improve its governance and
security. US Air Force (USAF) FID actions entail working by, with, and through
foreign aviation forces, foreign law enforcement, counterintelligence, and security
agencies to achieve HN IDAD and/or US strategic and operational objectives.

FID ACTIVITIES

FID operations are divided into three major categories: indirect support, direct support
(not involving combat), and combat operations. The “Integrated Security Cooperation
Activities” figure shows typical security cooperation (SC) activities aligned to major FID
categories. Although various capabilities, programs, and activities within these categories
may occur simultaneously, the categories represent significantly different levels of US
involvement.

Airpower can serve as a force multiplier in countering subversion, lawlessness, and
insurgency (including terrorism) by enhancing the survivability and effectiveness of
internal security operations. However, government success in a FID context is predicated
on a complex set of variables. The application and effectiveness of military force,
including airpower, is only one of these variables. A government that can provide
adequate services and is slowly reducing violence may succeed. In contrast, a
government that achieves quick military victories but fails to make fundamental reforms
to address the root causes of internal threats may ultimately fail. Nonetheless, early and
effective USAF activities to develop or improve HN capabilities can also reduce the need
for higher forms of direct support or intervention.

USAF FID may reinforce the HN government’s security and defeat internal threats,
but it also has the potential to undermine HN stability sought in US policy. For
instance, overt US involvement in FID operations may create perceptions of weak or
compromised HN governance, thereby aggravating the underlying causes of instability.
Further, airpower may concentrate the use of force into a small number of government
officials who can use it to attack perceived rivals or reform organizations. USAF FID
efforts should always support the broader political reform process necessary to address
the underlying threats to internal security, consistently promoting US interests in the HN
or region.

THE ROLE OF AIRPOWER IN FID

FID air operations are founded on the basic tenets of airpower. These tenets, which apply
equally to US and HN forces, include mission command, flexibility and versatility,
synergistic effects, persistence, concentration, balance, and priority.5 Specific methods,
applications, and expectations may vary according to the nature of the conflict. The HN’s
IDAD strategy provides the basis for determining appropriate airpower roles and
objectives. Accordingly, USAF FID assistance should be designed to support and
reinforce the HN’s IDAD strategy.

Many critical elements of a HN’s IDAD strategy involve “soft power” initiatives that
combine tangible actions, operations in the information environment (OIE), coalition
building, and subjective perceptions. The military instrument of power, particularly
airpower, may offer attractive and relatively simple solutions to internal defense problem
sets. Still, commanders must recognize that relatively small miscalculations or missteps
involving military force may have disproportionate negative impacts on the HN’s overall
IDAD strategy. Airpower operations are most successful when their resources and
methods support the total range of IDAD functions.

IDAD STRATEGY

IDAD is defined as “the full range of measures taken by a nation to promote its growth
and to protect itself from subversion, lawlessness, and insurgency.”6 The principal
functions of an IDAD strategy include balanced development, mobilization, security, and
neutralization.

 Balanced Development refers to a HN’s attempts to achieve national objectives in
     a way that allows all individuals and groups in society to share in the rewards of
     development, thereby potentially alleviating frustration.

 Mobilization provides organized manpower, resources, and activities to motivate
     and organize popular support for the HN government.

 Security includes all efforts to protect the HN populace from violence and provide a
     safe environment for national development and mobilization.

 Neutralization encompasses HN law enforcement and military efforts that render

As the HN government brings together all four functions of the IDAD strategy, its airpower
resources should facilitate synergies among defense and development initiatives. Using
airpower to support the full range of IDAD initiatives enhances the host government’s
ability to focus on political and economic solutions to a crisis.

BALANCED DEVELOPMENT AND MOBILIZATION

Airpower’s role in development and mobilization is especially useful where ground lines
of communication are untenable due to terrain or threats. Aerial logistics and
communications networks establish critical links between the government and the
population by carrying information, supplies, and services to civilians. Initiatives such as
healthcare infrastructure development contribute to regional stability and government
legitimacy while improving rapport with and understanding of HN populations. These
efforts can provide tangible and enduring HN governance benefits to underserved or
marginalized groups that would otherwise be vulnerable to internal threats and influence,
while mobilizing them to support the legitimate HN government at crucial times.

SECURITY AND NEUTRALIZATION

Airpower can contribute most effectively to security and neutralization when it functions
as an integrated component of the overall IDAD effort. It is least effective when employed
unilaterally as a substitute for ground maneuver or long-range artillery. The effectiveness
of airpower efforts can be enhanced by including intelligence, surveillance, and
reconnaissance (ISR), C2, and air mobility support. Properly employed airpower can
demonstrate to the population that a legitimate government is in control.

UNDERSTANDING THE OPERATIONAL ENVIRONMENT

The operational environment is the composite of conditions, circumstances, and
influences that affect the employment of capabilities and bear on the commander's
decisions. The political, economic, and physical terrains of HNs can present significant
challenges to both US and HN air forces during FID operations.

The greatest FID operational challenge is understanding the HN’s operational
environment (including the HN’s governmental structure, information environment,
social and cultural factors, and other variables) and the nature of internal security
threats that can impact the HN’s, US’s, and partners' national security interests.
The first step should be to identify the USG, CCDR, and HN goals for the FID effort. An
in-depth assessment of the HN IDAD situation and needs, coupled with an understanding
of the nature of internal threats, typically provides important context and parameters for
the FID effort. Threat analysis should include the administrative and financial mechanisms
that enable threats, as well as the motivating beliefs and ideologies that drive them.

A crucial part of understanding and shaping the operational environment is conducting an
accurate assessment of how internal and external audiences may perceive US
involvement in HN IDAD efforts and how those perceptions shape relevant actors'
attitudes and behaviors. As such, threat assessments should include target audiences'
sentiments and perceptions. Additionally, commanders should apply the same effort to
cyberspace operations (CO), OIE, and strategic communication as to other FID
operations, since they contribute to achieving mission objectives in concert with other
operations, activities, and investments (OAI). USAF information warfare (IW) capabilities
(e.g., public affairs [PA], CO, electromagnetic spectrum operations [EMSO], intelligence,
and weather) shape perceptions at the tactical, operational, and strategic levels.
Collectively, FID operations and OIE can play a significant supporting role in USG efforts
to communicate policy and demonstrate US commitment.7

The lessons derived from the classic paradigms of insurgency, small wars, and terrorism
are crucial for recognizing and analyzing internal security threats. Studies and analyses
should address the characteristics of current and emerging threats, including their origins,
histories, and prior OAIs within the HN. While historical analysis is critical for
understanding internal threats, commanders should not plan their FID initiatives entirely
based on past behavior.

SOCIAL AND PHYSICAL CONDITIONS

Major environmental factors affecting FID planning and execution include physical and
psychological pressures from hostile elements, social fragmentation, political instability,
and economic constraints. Difficult terrain, seasonal weather patterns, physical isolation
of population groups, and poorly developed infrastructures often impede HN IDAD efforts.
Airpower, because of its inherent flexibility, can overcome many of these obstacles. FID
activities may be required in nations where public services, industrial infrastructures, and
air support facilities are being developed to modern standards. Austere terrain, adverse
climate, and the scarcity of improved airfields affect the tempo, scale, and character of air
operations and restrict the types of aircraft that can be employed. However, such
obstacles also present opportunities to effectively employ airpower in rural administration,
logistics, and tactical mobility.

THREAT FORMS

Subversion, insurgency, lawlessness, and terrorism manifest themselves in a variety of
forms capable of challenging the authority and survival of HNs. Strategic and operational
level planning should address the degree to which each of these forms manifests in the
overall threat to HN’s internal security.

SUBVERSION

Subversion is an action designed to undermine the military, economic, psychological, or
political strength of a governing authority. Subversion may weaken government authority
by normalizing extortion, bribery, or corruption, and physical threats. In other cases, it can

provoke widespread civil disorder, eventually leading to political and economic crises,
ethnic and religious confrontations, and armed disputes among rival political factions.
Dissident groups may use propaganda and terrorism to dramatize political causes or to
extort concessions from HN governments. Civil disorder can cause violence and social
upheaval, precipitating the collapse of a HN government.

INSURGENCY

Insurgency is the organized use of subversion or violence to seize, nullify, or challenge
political control of a region. Insurgencies often develop because of internal perceptions
that a host government is unable or unwilling to solve important domestic problems. An
insurgent aims to attain legitimacy through popular support, which fuels the political
mobilization required to generate workers, fighters, money, and weapons, while denying
the government the same. A HN’s ability to preemptively address critical economic and
political issues is central to countering insurgencies and should generally take
precedence over military force. Insurgency often assumes an ideological foundation with
social, economic, political, or religious components, the ability of force to affect the central
issues underlying an insurgency warrants special consideration in planning.

Insurgencies have individual characteristics that commanders should consider when
initiating FID. Commanders must understand the nature of the conflict. The essential
elements of information (EEIs) typically include cultural and political identity and
motivation. A failure to critically analyze an insurgent conflict and determine its root
causes and nature at the beginning of a confrontation can waste resources and potentially
fuel further insurgency.8

LAWLESSNESS

Lawlessness often occurs when a government has marginal or ineffective control over its
population or territories. Lawlessness may also exist when the government lacks the
resources and mechanisms required to monitor and control the formation and activities
of subversive organizations. Local dissident groups may use terrorism against
government agencies or against other ethnic or religious groups to demonstrate and
reinforce their claims of autonomy. The inability of a government to extend its
administration and influence into outlying regions often leads to the emergence of
ungoverned territories. This is often the result of not having the technological means to
provide physical presence and persistence in forward areas.

Subversion, insurgency, illegal drug production, and narco-terrorism all go together with
lawlessness. Illicit drug production and trafficking can function subversively when
international drug cartels seek permissive environments and resort to intimidation and
violence to suppress government interference. Partnerships involving exchanges of
drugs, weapons, and money between insurgents and criminal organizations such as drug
cartels may also support subversive activities, terrorist organizations, and revolutionary
movements. Terrorist organizations, insurgents, and criminal organizations often share
common infrastructures.

TERRORISM
Terrorism is the unlawful use of force or violence against persons or property to intimidate
or coerce a government or civilian population in furtherance of political or social
objectives. Threat groups may employ terrorism to pursue religious, political, and
ideological goals such as the replacement of a government that encourages changes to
a society unwanted by the threat group. Terrorism may also be used to gain political or
economic concessions from a HN government. When political mobilization and legitimacy
form the critical centers of gravity, terrorism is often employed to demonstrate the inability
of regimes to defend themselves and their populations.

          CHAPTER 2: SUPPORTING HOST NATION AIR FORCES

USAF FID activities generally focus on support to HN aviation forces. The scale of military
airpower operations in developing nations is usually small in terms of force size, total
sortie potential, resource consumption and availability, and overall support costs. Despite
these limitations, the potential contributions of HN aviation forces can be vital to the
success of its IDAD strategy. When HN governments possess only a few aircraft, airframe
availability, maintenance timelines, flight safety, and sortie generation rates are critical.

Aircraft sustainment and supportability are difficult problems in small fleets. Funding
constraints and supply shortages in HNs can amplify logistics problems. The grounding
or combat loss of relatively few aircraft can degrade or destroy an air effort.

The USAF organization model may not offer a workable solution for the HN, nor may a
complex organization be necessary if the HN’s air force size is small. Detailed
organizational models featuring extensive specialization and methods of authority
delegation may not be appropriate for a smaller, less sophisticated fleet of aircraft.
Training and advising should be tailored to the HN's needs and the operational
environment while incorporating appropriate elements of the USAF model.

Commanders conducting FID should anticipate and account for differences between US
and foreign organizational structures and personnel policies when providing FID training
and advisory assistance. US-centric organizational leadership models, such as
empowering noncommissioned and junior officers with significant authority and
responsibility, may not be acceptable to air forces in some partner nations.

In some cases, HN aviation programs are dependent upon FA for major weapon systems,
aviation support equipment, aircraft spares, training, advice, technical services, survival
equipment, specialized clothing, munitions, and even consumables.

AIRCRAFT

HN military aircraft available for internal defense operations may be older-generation
aircraft. A lack of commonality or availability with US platforms and parts inventories
complicates FID activities in many countries. The cost and complexity of modern aircraft
systems usually rule out new procurement and magnify life-cycle support problems.
Older-generation, non-standard aircraft may be the only viable option for HNs.

A nation might possess few or no precision-guided munitions. A HN may be unable to
conduct close air support (CAS) at night. Many air forces are capable of area engagement
but not precision engagement.

A nation may lack an effective strike capability. Some nations in the developing world
possess weapons delivery platforms but lack the operations and maintenance (O&M)
funding to field them, leaving aircraft unavailable for operational use.

FID efforts in such environments typically need to prioritize aircraft availability, sortie
generation, aircrew training, and lifecycle management to ensure a viable air power
capability is on hand to support HN IDAD priorities.

AIR FACILITIES

Aerial port facilities vary. Major airports serve most capital cities, however, conducting
military air operations from civilian airports is often impractical due to traffic congestion,
space restrictions, and political sensitivity. Outside capital cities, civil and military aviation
support facilities may be limited. Military aviation units often have access to only a few
main operating locations with hard surface runways.

Forward operating locations may include short, unimproved airstrips with limited approach
or runway lighting, intermittent or unreliable electrical power infrastructure, and passive
defense capabilities. Modern navigational aids may also be limited, with non-directional
beacons being more prevalent despite being less reliable. There are generally no terminal
approach aids outside international airports except for navigation aids found at air
installations occupied by US military forces. Consequently, military flying operations rely
extensively on visual flight rules procedures or global positioning system navigation.

FID efforts must include an assessment of air facility constraints and restraints throughout
planning and execution. In some cases, air facility capacity and capability limitations may
need to be corrected, improved, or mitigated before effective support to HN IDAD
operations can be accomplished. For example, airworthiness assessments conducted by
USAF personnel at HN air facilities may be used to simultaneously assess HN
capabilities, develop air facility improvement initiatives, build rapport, and foster a spirit of
cooperation between US and HN authorities.

INTELLIGENCE

Intelligence capabilities (including the collection, processing, exploitation, and
dissemination of information or intelligence, analysis, counterintelligence activities, and
other related skills) will vary across nations. Among the multiple intelligence disciplines,
human intelligence (HUMINT) is often the primary source for many HNs. Additionally,
counterintelligence activities against internal and local threats are often the HN’s best
capability to neutralize and exploit intelligence information received through HUMINT.
However, even these capabilities may be limited by the lack of all-source analysis and
fusion, as well as by uncertain source credibility. Many HNs have limited capability to
conduct imagery intelligence and signal intelligence.

The lack of efficient procedures for the timely dissemination of tactical intelligence often
degrades overall mission effectiveness. HN’s intelligence capabilities may also be limited
by existing information sharing agreements with allied nations and coalition partners and
may require revision to improve access and cooperation. Aviation units may lack the
capability to incorporate intelligence products into mission planning. Therefore, FID efforts
should emphasize the use and significance of basic intelligence analysis skills. HN’s
acquisition of technical intelligence collection capabilities should be accompanied by the
development of analytical skills and dissemination systems that will turn the collection into
actionable intelligence.

Airpower can help commanders maintain situational awareness through ISR capabilities.
Platforms equipped with signals or geospatial intelligence capabilities enable the

AIR MOBILITY OPERATIONS

Air mobility can increase the HN government’s capacity to govern and administer through
presence and persistence into otherwise inaccessible regions of the country, and by
physically extending the reach of public policy, public works, security, and information
programs.

 Airlift can carry specialists and trainers to remote regions to provide on-site technical
     training and assistance in areas such as public services management, sanitation and
     hygiene, agronomy, agribusiness management and technology, veterinary medicine,
     ecology, environmental protection, and school administration.

 Air mobility can also support DoS developmental initiatives by delivering construction
     equipment, supplies, and personnel to build rural housing projects, power generation
     plants, hydroelectric facilities, bridges, and other public works.

 Air mobility can support security and neutralization by deploying, sustaining, and
     reinforcing civil law enforcement agencies as well as military and paramilitary surface
     elements. Air mobility can also effectively advance political goals by extending the
     electoral process to rural areas.

SUSTAINMENT

USAF sustainment forces play a dominant role in SA and form the backbone of indirect
USAF FID assistance. These forces also support US force deployment and specific joint
and multinational operations with more direct forms of support. Information developed
through site surveys, joint and multinational exercises, mission analysis, and other
assessments supporting SA activities may be significant to planners.

The HN’s IDAD strategy should be understood, and its aviation requirements and
capabilities should be defined before detailed sustainment planning commences.
Accordingly, sustainment planning for significant force structure improvements should
focus initially on what the HN intends to do, or can do, for itself. Specifically:

 How the host government intends to employ its resources to reach its IDAD goals.

 Immediate and long-range priorities assigned to IDAD objectives.

 How the HN intends to employ airpower to support both military and non-military
     objectives contained in the overall strategy.

 What the HN’s capacity is to receive, store, operate, and maintain both present and
     projected air, space, and information resources.

Sustainment operations in FID should be designed to enable HN airpower capabilities.
Focus areas of HN aviation forces’ sustainment capabilities should include
maintenance, readiness, logistics, communications, air base defense, medical
functions, and airpower infrastructure. Commanders should consider the following
sustainment factors when supporting FID operations:

 Operating in austere environments with limited infrastructure.

 Increased combat readiness for surviving and operating in increased threat
     environments, to include chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear environments.

 Increased security and force protection (FP) requirements.

 Extended logistical lines.

 Communications limitations.

 Multiple distributed operations.

 Medical service availability.

HN forces’ sustainment capabilities should be assessed, and training and education
developed to ensure full mission capability. Resource priorities should align with
objectives identified in the IDAD plan, along with strategy-to-task FID planning.
Sustainment planning should account for the limited logistics infrastructure often
prevalent in developing nations and for social, economic, and political factors that could
enhance or impede operations.

COUNTERLAND/COUNTERSEA OPERATIONS

The FID counterland/countersea mission includes air interdiction (AI) (on land or sea) and
CAS in support of the friendly surface scheme of maneuver. Counterland/countersea
efforts generally emphasize precision engagement and minimization of collateral damage
risk. AI neutralizes or degrades enemy combat power before it can be employed against
friendly surface forces. CAS is focused on suppressing or destroying enemy combat
power operating near friendly ground forces. Counterland/countersea operations for
security and neutralization occur when hostile elements openly commit their forces during
assembly and attack or when their C2 centers and logistics elements are exposed and
identified.

Counterland/countersea operations should be planned and executed on a scale
commensurate with the required effects. When countering certain forms of lawlessness
(e.g., illicit narcotics production and civil disorders), surface operations are generally
aimed at controlling territory or maritime areas, making arrests, and seizing contraband
rather than inflicting casualties. CAS, if required, should be limited to protecting ground
forces by using tactics and munitions designed for suppression, shock, and intimidation
rather than maximum lethality.

PERSONNEL RECOVERY OPERATIONS

Personnel Recovery (PR) operations can be employed in virtually every aspect of FID air
operations. Reliable combat search and rescue (CSAR) and casualty evacuation
(CASEVAC) capabilities, especially at night, historically improve the willingness and
ability of HN ground forces to engage in operations they may otherwise be less motivated
to perform. Organic and capable PR also provides HN governments with additional
capabilities to respond to humanitarian and disaster relief situations.

OPERATIONS IN THE INFORMATION ENVIRONMENT

The informational and psychological effects of using air assets can have significant
consequences in FID contexts. OIE ensures these consequences are fully analyzed and
integrated with FID priorities and HN IDAD objectives before acting. Effective planning
and the employment of information capabilities can influence relevant actors’ decision-
making, contributing to the desired set of behaviors.

 Airpower supports psychological operations (PSYOP) by delivering specific
     information to target audiences through radio, television, loudspeakers, print, and
     other media, and often by using air mobility to establish the presence of HN
     government functions in isolated locations.

 Air forces possess capabilities that may produce influential effects by demonstrating
     superior mobility, responsiveness, and firepower. Influencing target audiences'
     behavior through air operations can weaken enemy resistance, capture public
     support, or both. Airpower may also yield indirect OIE benefits through humanitarian
     and civic assistance (HCA) activities.

 USAF PA, in coordination with the responsible combatant commands, can develop,
     create, and disseminate information both domestically and abroad to bolster HN,
     multinational, and US operations, as well as complement other OAIs in the IE.
     Capabilities may include engagements with the local and international press, local
     communities, and key government stakeholders, as well as information released
     through US and multinational websites, traditional news media, and social platforms.

 Electromagnetic warfare (EW) is conducted to secure and maintain freedom of action
     in the electromagnetic spectrum (EMS). Military forces rely heavily on the EMS to
     sense, communicate, strike, and dominate offensively and defensively across all
     warfighting domains. EW is essential for protecting friendly operations and denying
     adversary operations within the EMS.

 Cyberspace capabilities provide an indirect or direct role to support or extend effects
     and provide defense-in-depth options in the face of increased probing and attempted
     cyber actions against friendly networks. In the FID context, planners should
     coordinate cyber support with interagency partners and sustainment agencies.

SPECIAL OPERATIONS
Special operations forces (SOF) offer extended military capabilities and tailored options
providing great flexibility, stealth, surgical execution, speed, and surprise. SOF aviation
is especially useful in situations where conventional solutions are not effective. The
development and maintenance of a HN special operations aviation capability is
particularly important to countries that must deal with such internal asymmetric threats as
insurgency, terrorism, criminal subversion, and illicit drug production and trafficking. US
FID efforts should be organized and executed to establish or increase HN special
operations aviation in such circumstances.

surface forces with conventional aviation assets. As with US forces, indigenous
capabilities should be adaptive, fluid, and responsive to asymmetric threats and

                CHAPTER 3: PRINCIPAL USAF FID ACTIVITIES

FID operations take place within the broader context of US strategies to shape and
influence the operational environment. The decision to conduct FID activities is based on
US and international laws, however, the culture and norms of the affected nation influence
how FID is conducted. Operations should be tied to specific objectives and may be
conducted over extended periods.

INTEGRATED AGENCY OBJECTIVES

The first requirement for USAF FID activities is to ensure commanders clearly articulate
military objectives based on HN and US objectives. USAF FID activities should be
sensitive to HN needs while integrating plans and operations with other participating
departments, executive agencies, and USG organizations.

Commanders should ensure USAF FID-related indirect and direct support activities
function as integrated elements of the overall US FID effort. USAF commanders and their
assigned or attached forces should be prepared to function as part of a joint-interagency
team with mutually supporting programs and objectives. Relationships between
command agencies should be clearly defined. USAF FID activities will often fall under the
purview of non-DoW agencies.

USAF FID ACTIVITIES

USAF FID activities primarily aim to develop and enhance HN airpower capabilities. FID
activities can take a variety of forms and should address the overall needs and capabilities
of HN aviation forces' employment and sustainment. Principal USAF FID activities are
typically conducted through operational advising (OA), which includes equip, assess,
train, advise, and assist functions:

 Equip HN aviation forces by facilitating the transfer of US defense articles and
     services under the SA Program to eligible foreign government aviation units
     supporting HN IDAD programs.

 Assess foreign military aviation capabilities and provide direction or
     recommendations towards improving HN airpower employment and sustainment
     methods. Aviation assessments are carried out primarily in support of CCDR
     requirements and for other key USG agencies and departments. Assessments focus
     on foreign aviation capabilities and limitations, specifically: aircrew capability and
     safety, aircraft airworthiness, critical resource availability, resource sustainability, and
     operational potential.

 Train foreign military forces to operate, sustain, and improve indigenous airpower
     resources and capabilities. Training normally includes operational tactics, techniques,
     and procedures (TTP) in such areas as C2, CSAR, communications systems support,
     air-space-ground interface, aerial insertion, extraction, resupply, intelligence
     production and processes, ISR activities,10 CAS, AI, and airlift operations. Training

force integration into multinational operations. USAF personnel bring all other key
tasks (equipping, assessing, training, advising, and assisting) together in a coordinated
effort to achieve interoperability with and integration of HN forces.

USAF FID activities may be a cost-effective means of helping HNs deal with internal
problems before they assume regional or global dimensions. This assistance can also
build enduring relationships with foreign officials who may provide or facilitate access to
important resources, capabilities, and basing. Bonds of trust between advisors and
foreign military leaders forged during FID activities have often overcome critical access
problems and acted as force multipliers during crises and contingencies.

THE FID CONTINUUM

The objective of USAF FID activities is to encourage and support HN solutions to
subversion, insurgency, lawlessness, and terrorism, and to reduce the possibility of direct
US military involvement. USAF support is usually applied indirectly through SA functions,
joint and multinational exercises, and logistics support. Alternatively, the USAF can

INDIRECT SUPPORT

SA, supplemented by joint and multinational exercises and other joint initiatives,
constitutes the primary USAF contribution to indirect support of FID activities. The
Department of the Air Force (DAF) International Engagement Plan (IEP) defines USAF
SC efforts, and SA is one of the tools available to support SC and FID. The principal
objective of SA in FID is to establish secure environments in which foreign governments
can pursue social, economic, and political initiatives to relieve tensions in their nations
and to prevent internal threat organizations from establishing a safe haven. Although SA
encompasses more than FID, it is a component of other FID activities, such as logistics,
training, and advisory support.

USAF SA efforts should focus on defense equipment and services that reduce foreign
security forces' dependence on in-country supervision and US support. Recipient nations’
personnel should be trained to operate and maintain the systems provided. In some
cases, they should also be shown how to employ these systems for specific operations.
As HN proficiency in air operations improves, developing internal training capabilities
within HN aviation units can reduce dependence on in-country USAF assistance. To
overcome sustainability problems inherent in maintaining older generation aircraft and
systems, USAF SA efforts may also include administrative and logistics support for
nonstandard items.

The DoS has the overall responsibility for military and economic SA. The DoW
administers the military assistance portion under the direction of the Assistant Secretary
of Defense for International Security Affairs. The USAF is responsible, as an
implementing agency, for those SA programs assigned by the Defense Security
Cooperation Agency (DSCA). Of the several SA programs involving the USAF, the main
programs typically include:

 Foreign Military Sales (FMS) is a non-appropriated program through which foreign

HN capabilities. USAF direct support activities normally focus on civil-military operations
(CMO) support,11 intelligence cooperation, communications, cybersecurity support, SFA,
PSYOP, logistics support, and other “stand-off” support functions.

ISR, attack, and forward air control assets provide capabilities in support of US and HN
ground and maritime forces. USAF EW platforms can also provide support, including
communications jamming.

                CHAPTER 4: PLANNING FOR FID OPERATIONS

USAF FID primarily entails airpower enablement, i.e., helping foreign aviation
forces employ, sustain, and defend their resources. As their US counterparts, allied
and partner commanders should employ airpower as mutually supporting, integrated
systems that function synergistically with surface operations. USAF planners should tailor
FID efforts to achieve the desired level of HN capability, self-sufficiency, and action.

FID planning should be incorporated into theater SC plans, strategies, theater campaign
plans, and FID contingency actions. FID planning should account for the DAF IEP along
with current DAF and national disclosure policies. The IEP defines objectives pursuant to
the national strategy and provides commanders and planners a methodology for
evaluating partner nation capabilities and willingness to work with DAF forces. The IEP
also provides a framework for synchronizing DAF SC efforts in support of CCDR
objectives. CCDRs base force employment guidance on an assessment of employment
opportunities and an analysis of the relative strengths and weaknesses of friendly and
hostile forces.

FID plans provide joint forces with a concept of operations and establish resource
requirements, priorities, channels of communication, and basing modes. Plans for indirect
and direct support are extensions of theater strategy but vary in purpose and execution.
FID operations can be planned and carried out independently of US military actions,
performed in conjunction with significant joint and multinational actions, or carried out
before or after major campaigns aimed at securing specific military objectives.

PLANNING INDIRECT SUPPORT

The principal function of the US military's indirect FID support is to secure US strategic
interests. Planning should be directed at specific sustainment capabilities, such as aircraft
maintenance, fuels, and training, as well as advisory support to create and sustain HN
military capabilities. HN military commanders deploy and employ forces and orchestrate
tactical events to accomplish objectives established in the HN IDAD plan.

Joint planning for indirect FID support should guide US military commanders and other
agencies charged with SA and joint-multinational exercises. These plans for indirect FID
support serve US strategic interests by supporting HN IDAD programs where such
assistance is politically feasible, legally permissible, strategically sound, and logistically
supportable.

PLANNING DIRECT SUPPORT

Direct support planning for FID primarily involves the employment and logistical support
of US forces. Direct support planning guides US military activities and concurrently
complements and supports HN IDAD programs. Direct support planning typically does
not include training or equipment transfers to the HN. However, it may include combat
advisors to help HN forces execute tactical operations and remain connected to
multinational joint forces at the tactical level. Airpower priorities and tasks established in
the plan involve allocating and apportioning USAF aircraft, aircrews, and support

resources. Planning may include mobility and employment considerations for allied
foreign forces when multinational operations are involved.

PLANNING REQUIREMENTS

USAF FID planning requires a clear statement of airpower objectives in the HN,
specifically, what major role airpower should play and what it should accomplish in
furthering the HN’s IDAD strategy. These objectives, derived from priorities the IDAD
plan's priorities, should serve as the basis for planning various forms of airpower support
to the host government to achieve desired effects. USAF planners, SC and SA personnel,
and foreign disclosure officers should be involved in the FID planning process.

Operational-level airpower planning for FID generally requires extensive cooperation and
coordination within the joint, multinational, and interagency arenas. In most cases,
CCDRs and their staffs function as focal points for coordinating both direct and indirect
forms of assistance. In many cases, the planning process requires the direct participation
of the supported government and its military forces. Requirements for effective FID
planning include, but are not limited to, the following:

 Plans that establish guidance and objectives for all types of direct and indirect
     support, compatible with host-government IDAD initiatives.

 Planning should determine the appropriate airpower tools for the operational
     environment and local conditions. For example, transferring available air platforms to
     the HN should account for and be compatible with that nation's technological and
     financial resources.

 USAF FID planning should be flexible. Periodic revisions may be necessary to
     accommodate alterations in HN planning, unanticipated restrictions on US
     assistance, and shifts in conflict intensity or requested levels of US support.

 USAF FID planning should be conducted jointly, or, where appropriate, coordinated
     with joint US military Services and with US civilian agencies exercising control,
     supervision, and oversight of FID activities in the recipient nation.

 All forms of direct and indirect FID support are coordinated with and approved by the
     HN. Joint and multinational planning gives participants a means to negotiate and
     agree on such issues as mission objectives, force levels, force composition, support
     arrangements, C2 relationships, and rules of engagement.

 USAF FID planning should be carried out according to US procurement and SA law.
     Allowable FID activities vary within the USG, the DoW, and the USAF. To ensure
     compliance with legislative constraints and funding procedures, FID planners should
     involve knowledgeable judge advocates early and often in the planning process.

STRATEGY TO TASK FID PLANNING

The FID planning process begins with a mission analysis to identify the critical capabilities
and vulnerabilities of both friendly and adversary forces. Planners must focus on the HN's
internal security threats and how to counter them within its financial and technological

means. This analysis helps the HN develop its IDAD strategy by defining the ends
(desired results), ways (methods), and means (resources) required to achieve its security
objectives.

Next, planners determine the role of military force within the IDAD strategy and prioritize
airpower's contribution. A qualified USAF assessment team then evaluates the HN's
existing aviation capabilities to identify gaps between what it has and what it needs.

Based on this assessment, a detailed plan is formulated that establishes the
requirements, priorities, and goals for recruiting, organizing, training, and equipping the
HN's aviation forces. This culminates in a formal FID plan with a concept of operations,
phased timelines, and clear objectives for US actions, all of which must be reconciled with
the HN’s broader IDAD strategy.

SUPPORT TO FID

Although the most desirable FID course of action is often through indirect support (i.e.,
minimizing US presence and participation), mission analysis may show that HN aviation
forces alone cannot prevail under the timelines and conditions required to bring their units
up to operational status. The operational design and resulting plan may require USAF
airpower to be introduced alongside enabling FID initiatives aimed at helping the HN
counter, survive, and prevail against threats to internal stability. When FID actions
involve the direct use of USAF forces, there is a critical sequence of initiatives to
incorporate into the planning process:

 USAF direct support operations are carried out in accordance with the joint force
     commander’s (JFC) guidance and performed in concert with other joint and
     multinational military forces and civilian agencies.

 HN airpower is supported through training, advising, assisting, and material support.
     HN airpower should become functional as soon as practical and, where possible, be
     applied simultaneously with the USAF direct support effort.

 USAF advisors facilitate multinational operations with HN forces, followed by a
     transition to a HN-only operation.

 USAF direct support forces and operations are phased out of the campaign or
     contingency.

 The USAF supports HN air operations with ongoing sustainment initiatives, including
     specific sustainment capabilities and advisory assistance, until the HN attains a
     successful conclusion to the conflict.

situation. FID planning should begin with an analysis of the operational environment.
Intelligence requirements are based on the commander’s information needs. They can
include risk assessments, analyses of enemy forces and infrastructures, endemic health

SPACE SUPPORT TO FID

Space support should be integrated into FID at all stages of engagement. Space systems
and personnel, for example, provide commanders with ISR, C2, and position, navigation,
and timing (PNT) support. Proper command relationships, normally direct liaison
authorized (DIRLAUTH), should be established between in-country teams and national

intelligence data and analysis.

communications (SATCOM) support centers in the areas where they operate to provide
them with SATCOM capabilities. Similarly, DIRLAUTH, with the mission management
center for friendly force tracking (FFT), allows management and use of FFT capabilities.

Some space-based communications capabilities and services can be made available to
HN forces. In some cases, intelligence derived from space-based assets may also be
shared with the HN subject to the appropriate authorizations. USAF FID planners should
take appropriate measures to ensure foreign disclosure, security, and classification
guidelines are in place and followed.

Operations Center (CSpOC) allows in-country FID teams to access DoW space support.
The SpOC/CSpOC can also request and coordinate reachback to many non-DoW space
service providers that offer non-DoW, foreign, and commercial space support. The
SpOC/CSpOC provides connectivity to many space service providers that offer ISR,
SATCOM, and PNT capabilities. Depending on where the FID effort is taking place, the
HN may have better relations with foreign space service providers than the US does. In
those cases, the FID team should provide the HN with information that will enable them
to request services directly from sources that may not want to support US efforts.

accomplishment, however, CO may also be used to produce stand-alone tactical,
operational, or strategic effects to achieve objectives. CO are key force multipliers,
mission assurance providers, and risk mitigators for operations.

Health issues contribute to FID struggles for legitimacy and influence, and USAF medical

health infrastructure. Similar to regional area specialists, IHS professionals are proficient

                        CHAPTER 5: USAF FID EXECUTION

The geopolitical environment and US force structure reductions have increased the US’s
reliance on HNs’ ability to defend themselves and to function as viable coalition partners.
There is increased emphasis by planners and JFCs on creating multinational and joint
battlefields. That task, which involves facilitating the availability, reliability, safety, and
interoperability of foreign forces, falls to US military trainers and advisors.

USAF FID activities can establish a US presence, build rapport, achieve integration of
forces, and build a foundation for future regional cooperation. If necessary, commanders
can employ a greater range of capabilities and resources in more direct forms of FID
support when HN aviation units are inadequately sized or structured to make timely
contributions to their defense effort.

COMMAND AND CONTROL

Specific C2 systems and processes for indirect and direct support vary according to the
conflict situation, HN requirements, US objectives, and the level of US involvement. C2
systems and processes for SA and most forms of direct support (excluding combat)
include both civilian and military elements. Non-governmental organizations and
commercial consortia can provide much of the C2 infrastructure that enables FID. USAF
SA activities overseas are organized within SA offices under a military command
structure, but both civilian and military elements control and supervise these activities.
Military authorities command, control, and supervise tactical operations.

Security cooperation organizations (SCO) report to two command channels: one through
the US diplomatic mission and the other through the CCDR. All USG personnel
performing SA duties in a foreign country are under the policy direction and supervision
of the US CoM. Commanders and senior officers assigned to SCO positions in foreign
countries should receive specialized training on the limits of “policy direction and
supervision” and appropriate reporting instructions in military channels when guidance
may not be consistent with DoW policy.

C2 INDIRECT SUPPORT

The USAF provides indirect support in accordance with statutory authorities, executive
orders, and directives, regulations, and manuals issued by the DoS and the DoW. Military
personnel temporarily assigned within a foreign nation serve under the policy direction,
coordination, and supervision of the CoM unless deployed to perform duties directed by
the CCDR. Forces temporarily deployed overseas to accomplish SA functions within a
CCDR’s area of responsibility should normally be attached to that commander. The
supported CCDR normally exercises operational control of these forces.

SA-funded mobile training teams (MTTs) accomplish tasks and objectives established in
SA case letters of offer and acceptance by the DoS, the Secretary of the USAF, and the
SA community, rather than a CCDR or special operations command. MTTs fall under SA
case management and oversight of the SCO, or US Military Group, in the American
embassy. The MTT performs contractual functions, with a training “product” upon

completion. Air Force Special Operations Command (AFSOC) MTT deployments,
however, should be coordinated with the theater special operations component (TSOC)

C2 DIRECT SUPPORT (NOT INVOLVING COMBAT)

Direct FID support (not involving combat) is authorized and directed on a case-by-case

with respect to C2 responsibilities of the CCDR in whose AOR the operation is conducted.

Deployed Air Force special operations forces (AFSOF) trainers and advisors are normally

foreign forces. This legal provision gives AFSOF greater flexibility than other FID and
military coalition support activities. Their inherent flexibility makes AA forces an attractive
and cost-effective way for the US to establish important foreign relationships.

The CoM can request certain forms of indirect and direct support, requests are generally
subject to their approval. Coordination of mission requirements, operational constraints,
and tasking should be conducted with the JFC or other designated joint force agencies.
The CoM normally exercises in-country policy supervision and oversight of these
activities.

C2 OF COMBAT OPERATIONS

USAF forces employed in combat operations in support of FID should be organized,
commanded, and controlled based on guidance established in joint and USAF doctrine
and directives. C2 structures may require tailoring to fit specific operational environments,
force allocations, and US-HN agreements on command of multinational forces.

When deployed, AFSOF are normally attached to a joint task force as part of a
subordinate joint special operations task force (JSOTF) commanded by a joint force
special operations component commander. However, AFSOC personnel performing FID
missions may be attached directly to a conventional air expeditionary task force (AETF)
under a JFACC. If FID operations expand into larger combat operations, these AFSOC

JOINT AND INTERAGENCY OPERATIONS

FID activities frequently involve joint and interagency efforts supporting unified action.
The success of these operations depends on effective communications for tasking,
coordinating, monitoring, and reporting among the various US civilian and military
agencies. USAF C2 structures should be tailored to specific environments, missions, and
joint force compositions, and adaptable to varying scenarios. These structures should
connect in-country, joint, and interagency elements and should establish links between
in-country elements, the CCDR, and US command and supervisory elements. Deployed
USAF teams may function as elements attached to other Service units or attached, under
special arrangements, to interagency organizations.

MULTINATIONAL OPERATIONS

USAF elements play a key FID role by helping to develop and support multinational C2
capabilities for airpower operations. Multinational activity is inherent in all forms of military
FID assistance, and all these forms require links between US and HN C2 elements.
Command relationships and responsibilities for multinational exercises and contingency
operations are established in accordance with US-HN agreements.

Connectivity is essential, particularly when US and HN forces function in mutual support
during combat operations. Interoperability issues should also be considered considering
the USAF’s need for information assurance.

AIR ADVISORS

As the JFC’s primary contact with foreign counterparts, AAs represent the airpower
component of the advisory effort. Teams must be sized to address major deficiencies in
the HN air force. Advisors are expected to recognize potential airpower applications within
an internal conflict and understand how to integrate them with the HN's social, economic,
and political initiatives.

AAs exert significant influence by helping the JFC integrate foreign forces into
contingency planning as force multipliers, keeping coalitions connected at the tactical
level. They advise on airpower application and sustainment methods to support
multinational objectives. This core "assess-train-advise-assist" mission is performed
across all levels of FID. When supporting an HN conducting COIN, it is specifically called
"support for COIN."

Aviation FID supports HN programs like COIN, counterterrorism, and counternarcotics,
complementing ground and maritime advisory operations. Ideally, FID training should be
used as a shaping operation to encourage HN-led solutions and prevent wider conflict.
Commanders must anticipate the specific training HNs require for these missions. For
example, counterterrorism operations may demand that foreign air units shift tactics,
focusing not just on which platform to use, but how to use it effectively. Achieving
"mission-ready" status often requires major improvements in the HN's ability to generate
and apply detailed intelligence during mission planning and execution.

OPERATIONAL ADVISING MISSION SET FOR OPERATIONS, ACTIVITIES, AND
INVESTMENTS

OA encompasses a wide range of OAI focused on improving a HN's air force
capabilities and integrating them with their surface forces. OA is centered on assess,
train, advise, assist, and equip activities using the HN’s own equipment. Since HN
forces have varying levels of experience, OA bridges the gap between individual
training and true operational capability under the HN's unique conditions.

The demand for these advisory skills exceeds the number of specialized forces
available, so commanders must consider all options for enabling foreign aviation forces.
This support is conducted at the operational and tactical levels, with USAF personnel
deploying to collocate with HN units. Generally, "training" occurs in non-operational
environments, while "advising" and "assisting" missions take place in operational
environments where the risk of confrontation is higher.

The mission follows a critical sequence. All flying-related advisory efforts must be
preceded by a thorough assessment of the HN unit's airworthiness and safety. This
establishes a baseline for risk management and interoperability. Following the
assessment, tactical training may be required to bring HN forces to a level where they
can be effectively advised on airpower applications that support their national strategy.

Historically, FID has focused on tactical flying skills. However, the most significant
challenge is often institutional: senior HN leaders struggle to effectively organize, train,
and create operating regulations for their own forces. Therefore, commanders should
elevate advisory efforts to address higher-level military leadership on foundational
issues like infrastructure, organization, C2, and logistics. The goal is to build a lasting,
self-sufficient defense capability, not a temporary one that erodes when external
support leaves.

This institutional advising is a significant challenge that often requires the experience of
the broader USAF community, beyond specialized special operations resources.
Success is ultimately contingent on the advisors themselves, for whom language ability,
cultural intelligence, and geopolitical expertise are indispensable tools.

TRAINING VERSUS ADVISING

There is an important distinction between training and advising. Training is a form of
indirect support that provides a doctrinal foundation for military operations. These
instructional programs build capabilities ranging from technical flying skills to combat
TTPs and sustainment methods, all tailored to the HN’s financial and technological
limitations.

Advising, conversely, is direct support involving the practical, real-world application of
doctrine within the host country. Because advising can directly implicate the US in an
HN’s contingency, it crosses a critical political threshold that often requires presidential
approval. Therefore, commanders must provide all personnel with clearly defined rules
of engagement.

Advice is most effective when directed at the highest levels of the host government.
Guidance on strategic matters and operational planning has a more lasting effect when
acted upon by senior military leaders. At the field level, advisors support subordinate
commanders in tactical planning, air base security, resource conservation, and technical
assistance with US equipment. Due to their close contact with in-country operations,
USAF advisors are best positioned to identify requirements for further assistance,
coordinate direct support, and help analyze US-provided intelligence.

EMPLOYMENT IN DIRECT SUPPORT OPERATIONS

The USAF can provide direct support to enhance or supplement a HN's aviation
programs when indirect assistance is insufficient. The key is maintaining a balance:
direct support should prevent conflict escalation without undermining the HN's will or
capacity to achieve its own internal solution.

This support bridges the gap between indirect assistance and full combat operations,
especially when a US combat role is inappropriate or politically infeasible. Examples
include intelligence sharing, logistics airlift, and communications support, which can
often be conducted as stand-off options. While this does not eliminate all risks, it offers
specialized assistance without directly exposing US personnel to hostile fire. For
sensitive or clandestine operations, commanders should consider using AFSOF.

In multinational settings, commanders must use advisors to resolve differences in TTPs
between US and HN forces, tailoring procedures to fit all operational and legal
constraints.

Even when combat operations are required, the US role remains strictly supportive. The
central objective is to protect vital resources and alleviate threat-group pressure, but the
HN retains full responsibility for the strategic offensive. This preserves the HN’s
legitimacy and ensures all air support tasks can be transferred back to them as soon as
practical.

          APPENDIX A: JOINT AND MULTINATIONAL EXERCISES

Concurrently with testing and evaluating mutual capabilities, joint and multinational
exercises may include training and construction, as well as HCA projects, within a host
country. Although the exercises may be conducted only between USAF and HN air force
units, maximum utility is realized when they involve joint and multinational operations.

INTEROPERABILITY TRAINING

The purpose of interoperability training is to ensure that US and HN forces can function
as mutually supporting entities during combat operations. Training to achieve
interoperability should include doctrine, tactics, individual skills, weapons familiarization
(particularly if US and host forces use different weapon systems), maintenance, and C2
procedures.

Interoperability training assumes comparably proficient units are involved. The training
experience allows US commanders to learn how other forces conduct IDAD operations
and to develop the most compatible methodology, consistent with US FID policies and
objectives, for operating together.

If the foreign force is not proficient enough to conduct multinational operations with US
forces, SA efforts should be conducted to equalize the foreign force before multinational
exercises are undertaken. Joint-multinational exercises are not to be used to provide
training to foreign military personnel if that training is normally provided under SA.

SAFETY TRAINING

Safety training reduces the risks inherent in conducting military operations involving two
or more forces that differ widely in language, culture, geographic origin, technology, and
practical experience. Mutual safety is improved not only through training in the use of
specific weapons but also through standardization of TTPs.

CONSTRUCTION

Construction related to multinational exercises is permitted under two sets of rules, one
for Joint Chiefs of Staff (JCS) exercises and another for non-JCS exercises.

Set-aside funds contained in the unspecified minor construction account of each
US military Service pay for all exercise-related construction during JCS exercises.
Set-aside funds cover only material, supplies, nonmilitary labor costs, overhead (except
planning and design costs), and DoW-funded costs applicable to O&M of equipment.
O&M funds may not be used for construction related to JCS exercises.

During non-JCS exercises, O&M funds may be used to construct or improve facilities
under US control if each construction project results in a usable facility for US units to
take part in the multinational exercise. Strict project rules and precise funding limits are
established for such construction.

          APPENDIX B: HUMANITARIAN AND CIVIC ASSISTANCE

US Armed Forces personnel participate in HCA activities to create strategic, operational,
and tactical effects that support CCDR objectives in theater SC or designated contingency
plans while concurrently reinforcing skills required for the operational readiness of the
forces executing the HCA mission. HCA fulfills US military unit training requirements
while creating humanitarian benefits for the HN civilian population. HCA programs
and projects may not be provided directly or indirectly to any individual, group, or
organization engaged in military or paramilitary activity.

AUTHORITIES

HCA activities should be distinguished from similar types of projects the USAF may
undertake to accomplish a military operation that results in incidental benefits to the local
population. Purpose and intent are the key factors in determining whether Title 10 covers
a specific activity, USC, § 401, “Humanitarian and civic assistance provided in conjunction
with military operations.” For example, if an USAF unit digs wells, clears land, and cuts a
road through the jungle to service a base camp for the benefit of that unit, those activities
would not constitute HCA even if they result in ancillary and unintended benefits to a local
populace.

Expenses incurred as a direct result of providing HCA (other than minimal cost HCA) to
a foreign country is paid for with funds specifically appropriated for such purposes
(specifically included in USAF’s operation and maintenance account). Funding for HCA
activities is provided by the annual National Defense Authorization Act and obligated for
incremental expenses such as costs for consumable materials, supplies, and services, if
any, that are reasonably necessary to execute HCA activities. Funding does not include
costs associated with the military operation (e.g., transportation, personnel expenses,
petroleum, oils, and lubricants, equipment repair), which would likely have been incurred
regardless of whether the HCA was provided.

FORMS OF HCA

HCA is limited to authorized activities and minimal cost HCA, as defined by DoD
Instruction 2205.02, Humanitarian and Civic Assistance (HCA) Activities. Commanders
contemplating participation in HCA activities should seek legal advice on the form of HCA
that is most appropriate for the operation or exercise being conducted, especially with
respect to rules governing HCA limitations and funding.

HCA Activities performed in conjunction with authorized military operations include:

 Medical, surgical, dental, and veterinary care provided in rural or underserved areas
     of a country. Activities may include education, training, and technical assistance
     related to the care provided.

 Construction of rudimentary surface transportation systems.

 Well-drilling and construction of basic sanitation facilities.

            APPENDIX C: USAF SECURITY ASSISTANCE TEAMS

This appendix assists commanders in identifying and selecting appropriate FID options
by providing a brief survey of teams that perform functions under SA. One of the key
operational-level objectives of USAF advisory operations is to help integrate HN airpower
into the multinational and joint arenas. Commanders should refer to current directives and
publications for guidelines and specific policies on assistance provided under the USAF
SA program. This appendix also discusses functions, goals, and special constraints
associated with USAF advisory support to HNs. Additional information on SA teams can
be found in DoD 5105.38-M, Security Assistance Management Manual.

Legislative guidance limits training and advice by permanently assigned SCO personnel.
Also, SCOs are not sufficiently sized or configured to advise and train or perform technical
assistance duties that extend beyond primary (and essentially logistical) SCO functions.
Outside assistance may be required in specific instances.

Besides SCOs, several teams and organizations may perform limited SA functions for
specified periods on a temporary duty or permanent change of station (PCS) basis. These
teams include technical assistance teams (TAT), technical assistance field teams
(TAFTs), MTTs, extended training service specialists (ETSSs), quality assurance teams,
language training detachments, site survey teams, and defense requirement survey
teams.

Commanders for FID activities can employ these teams, and others assembled for
specific purposes, such as health service support teams. In certain instances, temporarily
deployed USAF teams may be called on to advise foreign personnel on operational
matters directly related to the use of host-nation resources in specific conflict situations.

     country or, when prohibited or deemed impractical because of inadequate local
     facilities, conducted out of the country. TATs should not be used to provide technical
     training or instruction except for incidental over-the-shoulder technical assistance in
     conjunction with their primary duties.

 Technical Assistance Field Teams. Where ongoing aviation support requirements
     cannot be met through the combined efforts of the HN air force, the SCO, and

ADVISORY SUPPORT TO HN FORCES

When specifically authorized and directed, deployed USAF SA teams advise host military
personnel on using airpower systems and related support capabilities. Advisory functions
are subject to legislative restrictions and DoW directives on levels and types of assistance
provided. Commanders and SCO personnel requesting this type of assistance must
ensure that advisory activities fall within current policy guidelines and legal parameters.

COMMAND ADVISORY FUNCTIONS

USAF advisory assistance may be required to facilitate HN air force support of IDAD
objectives and encourage a satisfactory correlation between US SA goals and the
recipient’s use of SA assets. Advisory assistance teams accomplish these tasks by
advising central command elements of the host military on the capabilities, limitations,
and correct use of airpower in conflict. Command advisory functions focus on operational
planning that can bridge the gap between IDAD strategy and tactical employment.

FIELD ADVISORY FUNCTIONS

USAF advisory assistance conducted at the field level (operational flying units, aviation
support elements, and army maneuver units) focuses primarily on improving host military
TTPs for airpower operations.

USAF advisory functions include mission-related advice on intelligence collection and
analysis, maintenance, logistics, communications, and administration, as well as tactical
operations. Field advisory support also has important applications in medical and MCA
programs, military construction, and influence operations. Its principal objective is to
provide guidance fostering self-sufficiency in the use of airpower resources for the conflict
at hand, not to supplant HN capabilities.

Field advisory functions should be closely linked to operational-level objectives supporting
overall US and HN strategic goals. This entails such field advisory functions as testing
and verifying HN airpower employment and sustainment capabilities, focusing HN
aviation resources on appropriate roles and missions, and facilitating the availability of
safe, reliable, and interoperable aviation support as a force multiplier for the US JFC.

Field advisory functions are not automatically linked to direct participation in host military
operations. Advisory functions that expose US personnel to hostile fire represent a
significant increase in US commitment with profound legal and political implications.
Personnel performing defense services may not perform any duties of a combatant
nature, including any duties related to training and advising, that may engage US
personnel in combat activities outside the US in accordance with federal law.
