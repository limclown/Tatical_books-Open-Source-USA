 9 (HD) as the DoW's paramount mission and outlines the increasingly complex threat
10 environment, encompassing traditional nation-state actors, asymmetric threats, and the
11 growing potential for attacks on US interests both at home and abroad, recognizing that
12 the homeland is no longer a sanctuary.

13 HD outlines the evolving threat landscape, emphasizing the need for deterrence and a
14 global perspective on homeland security. It details how the USAF will leverage its core
15 functions-air superiority to deny adversaries freedom of action, global precision attack
16 capabilities for rapid and decisive response, robust global intelligence, surveillance, and
17 reconnaissance (ISR) for early warning and threat assessment, rapid global mobility to
18 swiftly deploy airpower, and resilient command and control to synchronize defense across
19 all domains. This publication describes the command relationships and authorities unique
20 to HD, including interactions among United States Northern Command, United States
21 Indo-Pacific Command, North American Aerospace Defense Command, and non-DoW
22 agencies. It emphasizes the importance of clear command arrangements, especially in
23 scenarios involving dual-status commanders and forces operating under different legal
24 statuses.

25 Finally, HD stresses the criticality of mission command as a philosophy of leadership and
26 discusses some of the nuances of command and control of air operations in the
27 homeland. It highlights the need for seamless integration with interagency partners,
28 recognizing that success hinges on effective collaboration among federal, state, local,
29 and private sector entities.

30 This publication serves as a vital resource for all Airmen involved in homeland operations,
31 providing a common frame of reference for planning, preparing, executing, and assessing
32 operations to protect the nation and its interests. It encourages commanders and staff at

34 remains ready to meet the challenges of a rapidly changing security environment.

              CHAPTER 1: AIRPOWER IN HOMELAND DEFENSE

Homeland defense (HD) is the military protection of United States (US) sovereignty
and territory against external threats and aggression or, as directed by the
President, other threats.1 The greatest responsibility of the federal government, and by
extension the United States Air Force (USAF), is protecting the American people.

   “The U.S. military’s foremost priority is to defend the U.S. Homeland.”

                                       –Department of War 2026 National Defense Strategy

The USAF plays a crucial role in HD, contributing air power to achieve joint force
commander (JFC) objectives by leveraging its core functions: air superiority,
global precision attack, rapid global mobility, global intelligence, surveillance, and
reconnaissance (ISR), and command and control (C2), to protect the nation from a
wide array of threats. Many of the USAF capabilities that support HD are equally
applicable to defense support of civil authorities
(DSCA) operations. The legacy term “homeland
operations” encompasses the USAF’s role in both
the HD mission and DSCA. An important distinction
between the two lies in the supported and supporting
relationship. While the Department of War (DoW) is
the lead federal agency for HD, it operates in support
of another federal agency when conducting DSCA.2
While not the primary purpose of this publication,
specific information regarding DSCA can be found in
Chapter 4.

As the rapidly evolving operational environment (OE)
affects homeland operations, the authorities and
laws associated with HD and DSCA require careful consideration and clear prioritization.
This publication focuses on airpower’s role in homeland operations and its primary
objective, defending the United States from attack.

THE HOMELAND

The US homeland is the physical region that includes the continental United States
(CONUS), Alaska, Hawaii, territories in the Pacific (i.e., Guam, American Samoa, the
Northern Mariana Islands), as well as territories in the Caribbean (i.e., the US Virgin
Islands, Puerto Rico, and the surrounding territorial waters and airspace). The United
States also enjoys exclusive sovereignty 12 nautical miles out to sea and exercises
responsibilities extending 200 nautical miles from the coast.3

CHALLENGES TO HOMELAND DEFENSE

HD represents a complex and evolving challenge for the USAF. It demands a strategic
approach that leverages all core functions to deter aggression and maintain a secure and
resilient posture. As competition with adversaries intensifies across all domains,
commanders and their staff should recognize that HD is not confined to simply protecting
our national borders. Maintaining air superiority over the homeland in a contested
environment will require enhanced global precision attack capabilities, resilient C2
systems, and strong working relationships with all levels of government. Furthermore,
bolstering global ISR to provide early warning and accurate assessments of adversary
intentions and capabilities is essential for maintaining an advantageous position in the
modern threat environment.

USAF HD operations support the joint function of protection. Protection encompasses
the JFC’s efforts to secure and defend the effectiveness and survivability of mission-
related military and nonmilitary personnel, equipment, facilities, information, and
infrastructure deployed or located within or between the boundaries of a given operational
area (OA) to maintain mission effectiveness.4 For the past two decades, HD has been
seen primarily as a crisis response to natural disasters and anti-terrorism actions when
local or regional civilian authorities have been overwhelmed. Given the growing multi-
domain threats and increasingly capable US adversaries, HD requires the greatest
effort and attention. Achieving the DoW’s top priority is becoming increasingly important,
as the homeland is no longer a sanctuary from enemy attack. HD is vital to ensure the
protection of the American people, the sovereignty of the United States, and the ability to
project power beyond the homeland.

THREATS TO THE HOMELAND

HD should address all external threats as directed by the President, to facilitate a broad-
based, globally integrated defense-in-depth. Threats planned, promoted, caused, or
executed by external actors may take place inside the homeland, with or without
attribution. The persistent presence of both traditional nation-states and asymmetric
threats requires a proactive, comprehensive, and disciplined approach to HD.

Weapons of Mass Destruction (WMD). Adversaries have and continue to seek WMD
and the means to deliver them to enhance their influence and achieve greater strategic
leverage against US advantages. They may use the weapons to conduct an attack on US
citizens, infrastructure, and other vital interests and to exploit US power projection,
sustainment, and force protection (FP) vulnerabilities.

Missile Threats. Our competitors continue to develop long-range and novel missile
capabilities to threaten the US homeland---including traditional ballistic missiles, aero
ballistic and cruise missiles, hypersonic weapons, and novel nuclear and conventional
warhead delivery systems. Their goals are to exploit defense gaps, affect our ability to
generate surge forces, raise the risks and costs of intervention abroad, and introduce a
destabilizing threat to the general populace.

Electromagnetic Warfare/Cyberspace Operations (CO). Adversaries have invested in
capabilities to exploit US and allied satellites and networks through electromagnetic
warfare and CO. Additionally, adversaries have demonstrated the ability to infiltrate
critical homeland infrastructure cyberspace networks that, if acted upon, could cause
significant damage to both the military and civilian sectors. The DoD Cyber Crime Center
(DC3) is a key resource for analyzing these intrusions and identifying vulnerabilities.

Malign Influence. Adversaries will likely expand the use of malign influence to pursue
their objectives in an increasingly aggressive and hostile manner. This includes actions
to deny access to information or spread propaganda and deception. Malign influence
activities can also leverage social media and artificial intelligence to interfere with
democratic processes and incite domestic terrorism or civil unrest.

Conventional Military Forces. Adversaries have frequently tested America’s border
sovereignty using conventional forces. For example, North American Aerospace Defense
Command (NORAD) regularly conducts fighter intercepts of Russian Tu-95 Bear bombers
and IL-20 reconnaissance aircraft flying near Alaskan and Canadian airspace. More
recently, Russia conducted a bilateral mission with People’s Liberation Army Air Force
(PLAAF) H-6 bombers in the Alaskan Air Defense Identification Zone (ADIZ). NORAD
has executed its aerospace control mission over 2,000 times since 9/11.

Unconventional Hostile Forces. The threat to USAF installations in the homeland does
not only originate from sovereign nations overseas. Porous national borders have
enabled the undetected invasion of unvetted individuals, many of whom originated from
adversary nations or designated terrorist organizations openly expressing hostility

Espionage. The Air Force Office of Special Investigations (AFOSI) works with the
Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) and the Defense Counterintelligence Security
Agency (DCSA) to execute counterintelligence missions. One such mission is protecting
intellectual property (IP) related to the Defense Industrial Base (DIB).5 Because DIB is
predominantly composed of commercial companies, IP theft is a constant risk, and
protecting IP from exploitation is paramount to maintaining US technological superiority.

Sabotage. Adversaries have historically conducted sabotage to disrupt military
operations and critical infrastructure. More recently, Russian sabotage activity against
European infrastructure, likely aimed at fracturing Allied cohesion, demonstrates that
sabotage remains as relevant today as in past conflicts. AFOSI conducts criminal and
counterintelligence investigations with interagency partners to provide early warning and
actionable intelligence to defend the homeland.

USAF HOMELAND DEFENSE ACTIVITIES

Within the United States, the National Strategy for Homeland Security envisions
circumstances under which the DoW would be involved in defending national security.
Specific instances where the USAF could play a key role in HD, based on USAF core
functions, include:

AIR SUPERIORITY

Airpower’s primary role in joint air operations is to achieve control of the air.6 Historically,
control of the air has been a prerequisite to success for modern operations. It facilitates
freedom of action and freedom of movement and prevents enemy air and missile threats
from effectively interfering with the operations of friendly air, land, maritime, space,
cyberspace, and special operations forces (SOF). Dominance of the air cannot be
assumed, even in the homeland.

Offensive Counterair (OCA). OCA operations seek to dominate enemy airspace and,
more importantly for HD, prevent the launch of threats, resulting in greater freedom from
attack, increased freedom of action, and the ability to project power. OCA can provide
support to HD by projecting power against an adversary to deter them from threatening
the homeland. OCA is typically grouped with the joint function of fires and is often
associated with countering an adversary’s anti-access and area denial capabilities.

 On-call Missions. OCA operations are employed in a fast-moving, dynamically
    changing OE. A frequently used best practice is to task on-call aircraft. Armed
    appropriately, aircraft sit in alert status on the ground or loiter while airborne, awaiting
    the call to strike a target that could threaten the homeland.

Defensive Counterair (DCA). Integrated Air and Missile Defense (IAMD) integrates

GLOBAL PRECISION ATTACK

Global precision attack is the ability to hold a target at risk or strike rapidly and
persistently, with a wide range of munitions, to create swift, decisive, and precise effects
across multiple domains. The global precision attack function includes strategic attack,
nuclear operations, and counterland operations in the form of air interdiction (AI) and
close air support (CAS).

Strategic Attack. Strategic attack is an offensive action against a target, whether military,

Nuclear Operations. Nuclear operations are activities conducted across the competition

strategic attack, nuclear operations serve to deter adversaries from threatening the
homeland. The employment of nuclear weapons is a form of strategic attack that supports
the joint function of fires.

Air Interdiction. AI is defined as air operations conducted to divert, disrupt, delay, or
destroy the enemy’s military potential before being brought to bear effectively against
friendly forces or to otherwise achieve the JFC’s objectives. AI can channel movements,
constrain logistics systems, disrupt communications, force urgent movement, and destroy
enemy fielded forces. AI can be used to project power and strike targets that could
threaten the homeland.

Close Air Support. CAS is defined as action by aircraft against hostile targets in close
proximity to friendly forces and requires detailed integration with the fire and movement
of those forces. CAS supports firepower in offensive and defensive operations to destroy,
disrupt, suppress, fix, harass, neutralize, or delay enemy targets as an element of joint
fire support. The speed, range, and maneuverability of airpower enable CAS assets to
attack targets and support the ground scheme of maneuver. Both AI and CAS are USAF
counterland operations that support the joint functions of movement & maneuver and
fires. Should an adversary strike in the homeland, CAS would be a means to deliver fires
against those forces, as well as to protect friendly forces and the civilian populace.

attack, protect, and manage the electromagnetic operating environment.10 These actions
include and impact all joint force transmissions and receptions of electromagnetic energy.
The goal of EMSO is to achieve electromagnetic spectrum superiority, contributing to
gaining and maintaining information advantage and decision advantage to achieve the
JFC’s objectives. All aspects of EMSO can be used to support HD. Electromagnetic attack

Cyberspace Operations. The DoW seeks to preempt, defeat, or deter malicious cyber

regardless of whether that incident would impact DoW’s warfighting readiness or

RAPID GLOBAL MOBILITY

The USAF provides mobility forces with global reach to employ and sustain military forces
that enable JFCs to conduct decisive operations across the competition continuum. The
USAF fulfills the rapid global mobility function through air mobility, which is the rapid
movement of resources to, from, or within a theater by air.13

Airlift. Airlift is the movement of personnel and materiel via mobility air forces (MAF) to
support strategic, operational, and tactical objectives. Airlift provides rapid, flexible, and
secure transportation for the joint force. Personnel and cargo, categorized as
requirements, are transported to meet the JFC’s objectives and priorities. The ability to
rapidly transport cargo across the homeland and globally supports the joint force and the
American people in homeland operations.

Mobility Support. The USAF, either through conventional forces, the Civil Air Patrol
(CAP), US Air Force Auxiliary (AFAUX), or the civil reserve air fleet, can rapidly transport
personnel, equipment, and supplies across the globe-a crucial mission in homeland
operations.

Air Refueling (AR). AR is the in-flight transfer of fuel from a tanker aircraft to a receiver
aircraft in support of strategic, operational, and tactical objectives. AR expands options
available to commanders by increasing the range, payload, persistence, and flexibility of
joint and coalition receiver aircraft. AR can support almost all homeland operations.

GLOBAL INTELLIGENCE, SURVEILLANCE, AND RECONNAISSANCE

ISR is an activity that synchronizes and integrates the planning and operation of sensors,
assets, processing, exploitation, and dissemination systems, and analytic systems or
capabilities to directly support current and future intelligence operations. ISR operations
comprise the primary activities that feed data and information into the joint intelligence
process supporting the JFC in homeland operations. ISR is critical for HD, providing
essential data for threat detection and analysis.14 Persistent ISR operations enable
preemptive threat identification and rapid response against threats to the homeland.

Airborne ISR. One of the most visible representations of USAF intelligence operations is
airborne ISR. Airborne ISR can provide a unique, taskable, and visible presence,
delivering real-time, tailored information during mission execution. Airborne ISR provides
the information the JFC needs to best defend the homeland. When lawful and authorized
to do so, USAF intelligence forces may be tasked to conduct ISR within the homeland.

Targeting. Intelligence operations are a primary contributor to targeting and directly
influence the joint targeting process. Targeting is the process of selecting and prioritizing
targets, and matching appropriate responses to them, while considering operational
requirements and capabilities. Having appropriate targets nominated prior to the start of
operations would allow the JFC to coordinate actions across combatant commands
(CCMDs) and project power while conducting HD.

Operations in the Information Environment (OIE). OIE includes military actions
conducted to influence, disrupt, corrupt, or attack adversaries’ information environment
while simultaneously protecting our own. For HD, OIE can be utilized against adversaries
overseas to identify potential attacks by monitoring communications, assessing hostile
propaganda, and analyzing online behavior, thus enabling preemptive actions to prevent
these threats from materializing.

COMMAND AND CONTROL
C2 is the exercise of authority and direction by a properly designated commander over
assigned and attached forces in the accomplishment of a mission. In addition to the
commander, the C2 function encompasses the act of exercising authority (the C2
process), the framework that guides it, and the systems that support it. C2 is the central
joint function that integrates all the other functions and enables operations.

Tactical C2. Tactical C2 (TAC C2) encompasses elements that conduct battle
management, such as Air Operations Centers (AOCs), Airborne Warning and Control
Systems (AWACS), and Battle Control Centers (BCCs). Battle management is C2 in
execution-the management of operational activities in accordance with the commands,
direction, and guidance given by an appropriate authority. Battle management is required
in HD but carries the added consideration of integration with the Federal Aviation
Administration (FAA).

OTHER ACTIVITIES AND CONSIDERATIONS
The USAF is uniquely suited to provide quick response in support of civilian agencies
by providing forces and capabilities during an emergency, such as an attack or natural
disaster. USAF assets and capabilities are also leveraged in limited scope missions
where other agencies have primary responsibility for security, such as at national special
security events like the Olympics or the State of the Union address.

Border Security. A secure national border is a key aspect of HD. The DoW and the USAF
have supported civilian law enforcement agencies’ border security activities since the
early 1990s. Border security missions in which the USAF participates include Operational
Support, Detection and Monitoring Support, Crisis Response Force Support, and Air
Support. Operational Support involves motor transport operations, light maintenance, and
heavy equipment operations. Detection and Monitoring Support has service members
manning mobile surveillance cameras to observe and report activity to Customs and
Border Protection (CBP). Crisis Response Force Support, at CBP's request, includes FP,
medical, aviation, and engineering support. Air Support missions include tracking foot
traffic signs, transporting CBP agents into remote areas, monitoring for illicit activity,
casualty evacuation, and transporting equipment.

                               DoW Activities at the Southern Border

     declaring a national emergency at the Southern Border and clarifying the
     military’s role in protecting the territorial integrity of the United States. The EO

     the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) in obtaining full operational control
     of the Southern Border. Absent an exception, military personnel in Title 10 status

exception to the Posse Comitatus Act that limits the president’s deployment of the military

Though all combatant commanders (CCDRs) contribute to the defense of the homeland,
US Northern Command (USNORTHCOM), US Indo-Pacific Command

USNORTHCOM. USNORTHCOM’s mission is to “defend our homeland-deter, detect,
deny, and defeat threats to the United States, conduct security cooperation activities with
allies and partners, and support civil authorities.” USNORTHCOM conducts HD within its
assigned area of responsibility (AOR), which includes CONUS, Alaska, Canada,
Greenland, Mexico, parts of the Caribbean, and surrounding water out to approximately
500 nautical miles. In addition, it conducts DSCA in CONUS, Alaska, Puerto Rico, and
the US Virgin Islands. It may conduct the related mission of foreign disaster response in
Mexico, Canada, and the Bahamas.18

USAF forces permanently located within the boundaries of the USNORTHCOM AOR are
typically service retained. The multitude of commands operating within the US drives the
need to carefully deconflict roles, responsibilities, and missions. Doing so requires
consistent collaboration when planning operations, particularly when determining support
relationships and organizational policies, roles, and responsibilities. For example, it is
important to understand that CCMD FP policies take precedence over the FP policies of
a DoW component deployed in that CCMD’s AOR.

NORAD. NORAD is a separate command from USNORTHCOM, though they operate via
an integrated staff for mutual support during planning and execution of assigned missions.
Like USNORTHCOM, NORAD is charged with the critical mission of defending the United
States and North America. NORAD is a binational command responsible for providing
aerospace warning, aerospace control, and maritime warning of the North American
continent, specifically the CONUS, Alaska, Canada, Puerto Rico, and the US Virgin
Islands. This also includes the air defense identification zones, the air approaches,
maritime areas, internal navigable waterways, and the maritime approaches thereto. The
missions of aerospace warning, control, and defense in North America are collectively
known as Operation NOBLE EAGLE (ONE). USNORTHCOM’s AOR also encompasses
a defined portion of North America with a broad spectrum of missions assigned in the
Unified Command Plan (UCP). Deconflicting the NORAD, USNORTHCOM, and
USINDOPACOM missions within the overlapping geographic areas is accomplished by
SecWar orders and the UCP.

HOMELAND AUTHORITIES RELATED TO AIRPOWER

Authoritative duties, the legal framework, and complexities that are specific to the
homeland that the air component commander should consider include the following:

Airspace Control Authority. Unlike other AORs, where the air component commander
is normally delegated ACA by the JFC, the term airspace coordination authority is used
in homeland operations. The airspace coordination authority is responsible for
coordinating and deconflicting air traffic. The FAA almost always retains this
responsibility. All airspace planning is coordinated with and approved by the FAA. The
FAA provides liaisons to USNORTHCOM and USINDOPACOM to collaborate and
minimize conflicts during mission execution. If the FAA cannot fulfill these responsibilities,
the air component commander should be prepared to assume their role. The president
may transfer any function of the FAA through EO to the DoW in a time of war.

Area Air Defense Commander (AADC). The AADC is responsible for DCA operations,
including the integrated air defense system for the operational area. DCA and OCA
operations constitute the counterair mission, designed to attain and maintain the degree
of control of the air desired by the JFC. The AADC develops, integrates, and distributes
a JFC-approved joint area air defense plan in coordination with the component
commanders.

Incident Awareness and Assessment. Various organizations, such as the FBI, National
Counterterrorism Center, DHS, and state and local law enforcement, can provide much
of the required information. When authorized by the SecWar to conduct incident
awareness and assessment (IAA), Services should coordinate with these agencies before
collecting data independently.

OPERATIONAL AND ADMINISTRATIVE CONTROL

When conducting HD or DSCA under CCDR authority within the homeland, policy,
doctrine, and law apply the same as they do for operations in any other AOR regarding
command relationships, authorities, and responsibilities. Additionally, the ANG may
conduct state-level operations in the homeland under the executive authority of a
governor, with commensurate state command authorities.20

With several CCMDs, NORAD, the Department of the Air Force, and each state’s ANG
all operating within the homeland, it is important to understand command relationships
for homeland operations. Unity of command of USAF forces, whether in Title 10 or Title
32, USC, or State Active Duty (SAD) status in homeland operations, is maintained
through the presentation of forces to the appropriate JFC at the CCMD, JTF, NORAD,
incident command system, or state level. For purposes of unity of command and effort,
forces conducting DSCA or HD in the homeland are normally assigned or attached, with
specification of operational control (OPCON), to the commander of USNORTHCOM,
USINDOPACOM, or, for specified missions, NORAD. When personnel are attached to a
USAF Service component for homeland operations, detailed administrative control
(ADCON) authorities to be exercised by the gaining commander should be specified in
appropriate orders. The ADCON requirements for Air Reserve Component forces are
complex and require legal review.

the two CCMDs in support of HD missions. Proper execution of CAAs is paramount to
provide unity of effort in support of civil authorities across various jurisdictions. To ensure

principles:

 The use of attached forces should be limited to specific missions or operations.

 Force roles, functions, and uses to accomplish missions or operations should be

COMMAND OF USAF FORCES IN DIFFERENT LEGAL STATUSES

Airmen accomplish HD and provide support to civil authorities under multiple laws and
authorities, which may enable or restrict Airmen’s actions. The status of Airmen is
commonly defined by the legal authority that authorizes them to perform their assigned
missions. These statuses are commonly grouped as:

 Title 10, USC Regular Air Force, Air Force Reserve, and Air National Guardsmen in
    federal active-duty status operate in Title 10 status. In addition, CAP is a volunteer
    civilian AFAUX while operating under Title 10 status. Forces operating under Title 10
    are under the authority of the President as Commander in Chief.

 Title 32, USC Air National Guardsmen may also be requested to accomplish federal
    activities, including DSCA and HD, while remaining under the control of the governor.
    This circumstance is referred to as Title 32 status. They are under the authority of the
    state governor, they are funded by the federal government.

 State Active Duty. When the governor of a state mobilizes the National Guard, state
    Air National Guardsmen are typically in SAD status. The governor exercises command
    over forces in SAD status through the state’s Adjutant General (TAG). Funding is
    provided by the state. SAD forces conduct state missions in accordance with state
    needs and within the guidelines of state laws and statutes.

ANG forces can be classified into three categories within the law. Except for a dual-status
command scenario discussed below, forces can be in only one status at a time, which is
critical to preventing legal violations and maintaining clear lines of authority. The
coordinating authority allows the state governor to direct ANG forces in accordance with
a Title 10 commander's orders. In such an arrangement, Guard forces remain under the
authority of the governor, but coordination between the ANG commander and Title 10
commander (i.e., regular USAF officer) is required to achieve unity of effort. Coordination
challenges can be overcome using CAAs.

DUAL-STATUS COMMANDERS

A unique command relationship is usually established when federal and state forces
operate together. The following information describes circumstances in which regular
USAF and National Guard officers may be appointed to command both federal and state
forces pursuant to a CAA promulgated as a memorandum of agreement (MOA) between
a governor and the SecWar (via delegated authority from the President of the United
States). The intent of dual-status command is to improve unity of effort for operations
focused on a specific, temporary mission in which forces are in different legal statuses,
and when having a single commander directing operations is advantageous.

 National Guard Dual-Status Commander. Title 32 USC § 325 authorizes a federally
    recognized National Guard officer to exercise command on behalf of, and receive
    separate orders from, both a federal Title 10 and state chain of command
    simultaneously. If the President authorizes such service in both duty statuses, and the
    governor of the National Guard state or territory (or the Commanding General of the
    District of Columbia National Guard) consents to such service in both duty statuses,
    the National Guard officer is not relieved from duty in the National Guard.

 Regular USAF Dual-Status Commander. Title 32 USC § 315 authorizes a
    commissioned regular USAF officer to exercise command on behalf of, and receive
    separate orders from, both a federal Title 10 and state chain of command
    simultaneously. The SECAF details the regular USAF officer to duty with a state
    National Guard unit. With the President’s permission, the officer may accept a
    commission in the National Guard without prejudicing his or her rank and without
    vacating the regular commission.

Upon approval of dual-status, the officer may be appointed to command both state and
federal forces through distinct, separate chains of command. Before a dual-status
command can be established, an MOA must be signed by the governor and the President

HOMELAND COMMAND RELATIONSHIP COMPLEXITY

The C2 of HD air operations in the USNORTHCOM AOR is complex. Where the NORAD

normally retains authority for aerospace control and aerospace warning missions.

   CHAPTER 3: HOMELAND COMMAND AND CONTROL ACTIVITIES

Operational C2 is conducted by a commander through the four major activities of the C2
process: planning, preparing, executing, and assessing. These activities are both
progressive and continuous. However, conducting these C2 activities in the homeland
presents some unique challenges. Commanders will need to anticipate the necessity of
interagency coordination and adherence to domestic policies or legal constraints.
Additionally, when planning homeland operations, commanders must consider the
prioritization dilemma: whether to devote limited resources to power projection or to
dedicate the few standing organizations to conduct HD.

PLANNING

Planning is the broad term to describe how Airmen address and solve problems. It is
generally the first C2 activity conducted by a commander and their staff. The outputs of
planning provide foundational guidance for the remainder of the C2 process. Design and
planning are discussed separately, but they are inextricably linked. Design is part of
planning, and planning informs design.21

C2 of HD is complex and requires careful consideration in Alaska. USAF forces there are
typically assigned to USINDOPACOM, but the defense of Alaska is a USNORTHCOM
and NORAD responsibility. ALCOM is the sub-unified command responsible for the
AKTO. Similarly, while Hawaii, Guam, and other US territories and commonwealths in the
Pacific are a part of the homeland, the commander of USINDOPACOM is responsible for
those locations, with some exceptions. One such exception is that CDRUSNORTHCOM
provides ICBM defense of Hawaii while CDRUSINDOPACOM provides all other missile
defenses of Hawaii.

It is paramount that commanders and their staffs at all levels understand their superior
commanders' intent and convey it clearly to subordinate commanders. HD operations are
particularly vulnerable should combat erupt elsewhere around the globe, as any
operations within the USNORTHCOM AOR could effectively become a second front in
that conflict. This creates a prioritization dilemma, one which is typically associated with
conflict against a peer or near-peer adversary. HD operations could drive decisions that
would impact other AORs, given the limited resources within the DoW and the USAF.
Commanders and planners will need to understand the supporting/supported
relationships to avoid confusion should an OPLAN or an integrated plan set (IPS) be
implemented.

Aerospace defense operations within the homeland pose unique concerns for

 Control of Airspace. US airspace is under the control of the FAA. Civilian control of

 ROE. The airspace over the homeland is congested, with up to 5,000 aircraft in the

 OA. The OA includes the portions of the homeland that fall within the USNORTHCOM

        CHAPTER 4: DEFENSE SUPPORT OF CIVIL AUTHORITIES

Many USAF capabilities can provide a rapid response to support civil authorities during
domestic emergencies and disasters. Planning for and responding to these events should
be a Total Force effort.

Installation commanders should plan for situations that may require assigned units to
assist local authorities. They should be aware of the various industries or other facilities
in the surrounding community and should assess potential hazards or threats these
industries and facilities may pose to the installation. They should also determine what
type of assistance each may require in an emergency.

Risk management is integral to any planning related to homeland operations. Installation
commanders should also be aware of critical dependencies within the surrounding
community. They should work with involved agencies to ensure the installation can
continue to perform the minimum essential functions in an emergency. Coordinated
planning between the installation and the community is critical to a successful
emergency response and should account for the need to preserve the installation’s
ability to perform its mission and protect its forces when and where needed. What
occurs in the community may affect the installation’s ability to perform its mission. In
addition to providing support, USAF installations should have plans and procedures to
receive aid and assistance from DoW or civil agencies when needed.

MOAs, MOUs, and Intergovernmental Support Agreements37 with surrounding
communities can clarify issues such as response procedures, capabilities, and cost
reimbursement. Establishing and exercising such agreements prior to an incident enables
a mutual understanding of risk, requirements, and authorities. Governors may also
establish EMACs.

considerations and should be the primary reference in developing MOAs/MOUs. Refer to
the NRF and consider the following when drafting an MOA/MOU:

                  APPENDIX A: NATIONAL POLICY AND LAW

The USAF examines US policy, domestic law, and international obligations when
planning homeland operations. There are two general points regarding homeland
operations and the law:

First is the overall legal framework affecting the application of airpower in the homeland.
There are restrictions on using the military to collect intelligence in the homeland. In
addition, there are restrictions on using the military for civil law enforcement purposes,
with exceptions. Some examples include the President’s ability to invoke the Insurrection
Act if needed and legislation permitting some use of the military in law enforcement roles
to counter the influx of illegal narcotics.

Second is the law and its impact on potential financial reimbursement to the DoW. When
managing the consequences of an event, states normally exercise primacy over domestic
incidents. Only when they ask for federal assistance, or in extraordinary circumstances,
will the federal government get involved. The request process is key for the military
because, except for immediate emergencies, a formal request by the state followed by
Presidential approval is necessary for the military Services to be reimbursed financially.

Because of the legal and policy complexities, prompt and frequent consultations
with military legal experts are among the most important considerations in
planning for and employing military assets. The homeland legal environment is very
complex and dynamic. The following is a nonexclusive list of legal sources for homeland
operations:

Anti-Deficiency Act, 31 USC §§ 1341-42, 1511-19. This act generally prohibits the
obligation or expenditure of appropriated funds in advance of, or in excess of, an
appropriation by Congress.

Defense Against Weapons of Mass Destruction Act, 50 USC Chapter 40. Requires
DoW coordination with WMD response agencies. Authorizes DoW support to the
Department of Justice when the SecWar and Attorney General jointly determine that a
WMD threat exists, and civil authorities lack sufficient capabilities.

Economy Act, 31 USC § 1535. This act governs the transfer of material between
executive branch agencies within the federal government. The Economy Act does not
apply to the transfer of material to non-federal law enforcement agencies. Reimbursement
to the DoW from civilian law enforcement agencies is authorized under 10 USC § 277 to
the extent such would be authorized under the Economy Act.

National Emergencies Act, 50 USC §§ 1601-1651. This act establishes a process for

Posse Comitatus Act, 18 USC § 1385. The PCA prohibits using members of the Army,
Navy, Marine Corps, Air Force, or Space Force, while in Title 10 status, for law
enforcement purposes, except as authorized by Congress or the United States
Constitution. Prohibited direct support includes arrests, searches, and seizures. In
addition, any form of indirect support that would subject civilians to a regulatory,
prescriptive, proscriptive, or compulsory use of DoW power is prohibited. The PCA does
not apply to National Guard units in non-federal status.

 Military Purpose. The PCA does not prohibit direct support to law enforcement
    agencies if the primary purpose is to further a US military or foreign affairs function.
    Actions that serve a primarily military purpose include investigations taken pursuant
    to the Uniform Code of Military Justice or other military administrative proceedings and
    actions taken pursuant to a commander’s inherent authority to protect military
    personnel, property, or guests or maintain order on an installation.

 Emergency Authority. The PCA does not prohibit direct support in emergencies
    when the action is taken under the inherent right of the US to preserve order and carry
    out government operations. During sudden or unexpected emergencies, responsible
    DoW officials or commanders may approve the use of military forces in a law
    enforcement capacity to prevent loss of life or wanton destruction of property or
    restore governmental functioning or order. This authority should be used with great
    caution and in extremely unusual situations.
