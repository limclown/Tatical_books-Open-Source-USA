                                 Preface

The influence process task technical manual series is an evolution of Special Text (ST) 33-01 and provides
techniques and procedures that, in their whole, enable the conduct of influence activities by Army
Psychological Operations units. Each TM in the series provides unique content; however, the TMs are
interconnected and codependent. TM 3-53.10 will provide an overview and summary of the
interconnectivity between the TM series and higher echelon publications. The remaining TMs address
specific activities of the influence process. The influence process activity series includes
TM 3-53.11, Target Audience Analysis; TM 3-53.12, Plan; TM 3-53.13, Prepare; TM 3-53.14, Execute;
and TM 3-53.15, Assess.

       Note. TM 3-53.11 has already been published. TM 3-53.10, TM 3-53.12, TM 3-53.13, and
       TM 3-53.14, are currently in the publication process; therefore, the publication dates are still
       unknown, and they are excluded from the References section of this publication.

Developing separate technical manuals provides more detail and depth within each step of the influence
process enhancing Psychological Operations Soldiers’ ability to conduct influence activities. The target
audience analysis (TAA) was considered the highest priority doctrinal gap within the process and is the
task most interconnected to all activities in the process. Subsequently TM 3-53.11 was developed and
published. Each TM is published separately, as opposed to concurrently, to better support the force with
critical information as expeditiously as possible. ST 33-01 will be rescinded once the entire TM series is
published.

TM 3-53.15 focuses on assess, the continuous activity that affects the accuracy, effectiveness, and
efficiency of all influence activities. This TM codifies techniques and procedures for Psychological
Operations forces conducting the assess activity in support of military objectives and related influence
objectives.

TM 3-53.15 contains numerous acronyms, abbreviations, and terms. Users should refer to the Glossary at
the end of this manual for their meanings. This manual uses joint terms where applicable. Select joint and
Army terms and definitions appear in both the glossary and text. For other definitions shown in the text, the
term is italicized, and the number of the proponent publication follows the definition. TM 3-53.15 is not the
proponent publication for any terms.

The principal audiences for TM 3-53.15 are Psychological Operations Branch personnel and supported
commanders and their staffs, regardless of Service.

This TM is the primary reference for Psychological Operations Soldiers and units executing the assess
activity of the influence process, including conducting research requisite to the assess activity, recording
and reporting findings consistent with the Army operations process, and developing and conducting related
unit training.

This publication applies to the Active Army, the Army National Guard, and the United States Army
Reserve, unless otherwise stated.

The proponent of TM 3-53.15 is the U.S. Army Special Operations Center of Excellence, United States
Army John F. Kennedy Special Warfare Center and School, USAJFKSWCS. The preparing agency is the

                              Introduction

TM 3-53.15 is a new publication that supplements Army Service doctrine in FM 3-53.

TM 3-53.15 updates Psychological Operations doctrine to include applying the hierarchy of psychological
effects model to the influence framework and subsequent assessment process. The following is a summary
of information by chapter.

Chapter 1 provides an overview of the influence process activity assess.

Chapter 2 describes assess within the influence process to include the assessment process and assessment
planning.

Chapter 3 details the application and stages of the hierarchy of psychological effects model.

Chapter 4 identifies the various types of research that support military processes.

                                                 Chapter 1

                                   Overview

    The influence process is the foundation for conducting influence activities. The influence
    process consists of five activities: target audience analysis (TAA), plan, prepare, execute, and
    assess. Assess, like TAA, is a continual activity that affects the accuracy and efficiency of all
    influence activities. Recent operations underline the importance of influence activities as well
    as the challenges of integrating them into operational planning processes. Army Psychological
    Operations units conduct influence activities to create and reinforce desired behaviors in
    selected foreign individuals and groups. Psychological Operations units accomplish this by
    inducing changes in target audience (TA) attitudes, values, beliefs, and decision-making
    processes. This requires well defined objectives, robust and timely data collection, and analysis
    and evaluation.

INTRODUCTION TO ASSESS

     1-1. The Army’s operations process consists of the major command and control activities performed during
     operations: planning, preparing, executing, and continuously assessing the operation (ADP 5-0).
     Commanders use the operations process to—

          • Drive the conceptual and detailed planning necessary to understand their operational environment
                (OE).

          • Visualize and describe the operation’s end state and operational approach.
          • Make and articulate decisions.
          • Direct, lead, and assess operations.

     1-2. The influence process aligns with the operations process to enable commanders of Psychological
     Operations units to—

          • Understand their OE.
          • Visualize and describe the end state and operational approach through intermediate psychological

                objectives (IPO), supporting psychological objectives (SPO), and psychological objectives (PO).
          • Make and articulate decisions.
          • Direct, lead, and assess operations and activities.

     1-3. An assessment captures the progress toward accomplishing a task, creating a condition, or achieving
     an objective at any point in time. Assessments—

          • Validate and refine analysis and evaluation.
          • Deliberately compare forecasted outcomes to actual events.
          • Advise the commander.
          • Support planning activities.
          • Develop effective actions and messages.
          • Determine the optimum execution means.
          • Monitor target audience progression or regression toward an objective.
          • Determine effectiveness attributable to the influence effort.

     1-4. From an influence perspective, assessments incorporate measurements to quantitatively express a
     behavior change (that is, degree, percentage, or number), reduce uncertainty, and make informed decisions.
     For example, the inclusion of qualitative data, assumptions, unknowns, and the validity and reliability of
     measurements provide evidential value, reduce uncertainty, and increase confidence. The complexity of
     human behavior means forecasts and projections are continually refined or revised as information is gathered
     and the context or situation changes.

     1-5. Assessments occur episodically (connected to a specific episode/event and of limited duration, can be
     temporary or occasional) or periodically (repeated, occurs or recurs at regular intervals) as determined by
     the objectives. There are numerous competing influences on TA behavior. Consequently, determining the

UNDERSTANDING KEY TERMS FOR ASSESS

     1-7. Psychological Operations units use actions and messages to influence a foreign TA to achieve military
     and U.S. national objectives across a range of military operations. Assessing effects (behavioral or
     attitudinal changes), determining causes (triggers or motivations), and measuring effectiveness (level to

     context in which that behavior occurs. Specifically for influence activities there is a requirement for clear
     and precise terminology. This section provides clarity and precision in lexicon and frameworks for the
     assess activity. The following terms and concepts are described and delineated:

          • Perception, behavior, attitude, and cause.
          • Effect, effectiveness, and performance.
          • Objectives.

ASSESS

     1-8. In the context of the influence process, assess means determining the rate or amount of change and
     determining the value of indicators. Assess is a continuous activity that measures progress toward
     achieving influence objectives. The assess activity utilizes art and science in developing detailed analysis
     and informed evaluations. Soldiers are required to make a judgment based on available information.
     The assess activity becomes more difficult as the number of variables affecting a TA increase.
     Psychological Operations Soldiers assess influence activities by relying on systematic measures, analysis,
     and evaluation. Additionally, they also accounting for other factors influencing the TA to include—

          • Assessing in the influence context. This focuses on series effectiveness in relation to achieving
                higher approved objectives.

          • Assessing psychological effects to determine the intended and unintended effects on TA behavior
                during planned operations conducted by the supported unit, or spontaneous events that occur in the
                OE.

ASSESSMENT

     1-9. Assessment refers to the episodic or periodic determination of the progress made toward accomplishing a
     task, creating a condition, or achieving an objective at any point in time. In joint doctrine, assessment is a
     process. In the influence process, an assessment refers to the product that accounts for measures of
     performance and codifies evaluation and analysis of measures of effectiveness. Assess, assessing, and
     assessments are often used interchangeably in service and joint doctrine. This TM attempts to reserve the
     word assessment to describe a product.

     1-10. Assessments are categorized as formal or informal. A lack of dedicated analysis personnel will likely
     lead to informal assessments. At company echelon and below the reliance on operations reporting, personal

ANALYZE

     1-11. Analysis systematically studies something in detail to increase understanding of its structure,
     relationships, and/or patterns. Facts, data, or other relevant information are gathered from multiple
     research, observation, and intelligence products or sources. Soldiers analyze this information in relation to
     pre-determined indicators of performance or effectiveness. Soldiers may need support from social and/or
     behavioral scientists and/or data analysts in situations where analysis is complex, multi-faceted, or involves
     large data sets.

EVALUATE

     1-12. Evaluate means to determine the significance, extent, importance, value, quality, worth, or condition
     of something, usually by careful appraisal and study. Evaluation uses inputs from analysis (data and
     information) to form judgments or conclusions. This process involves subjectivity; however, using and
     clearly identifying multiple data points from various sources strengthens an evaluation and conclusions.
     Some evaluations can point to clear cause-and-effect relationships, while others must rely on the
     discernment and skill of the evaluator to estimate the degree of impact.

PERCEPTION

     1-13. The American Psychology Association defines perception as the process or result of becoming aware
     of objects, relationships, and events by means of the senses, which includes such activities as recognizing,
     observing, and discriminating. These activities enable organisms to organize and interpret the stimuli
     received into meaningful knowledge and to act in a coordinated manner. Messages and actions of a series
     introduce new information that the TA will process through their existing schema (that is, a mental
     organization of knowledge, experience, and understanding). These messages and actions can shape the
     TA’s knowledge, experience, and understanding to influence how they perceive the information presented.

BEHAVIOR

     1-14. Behavior is the measurable response of an individual or group to a stimulus or stimuli. Behaviors are
     observable patterns of actions among the target audience. Analysts must not confuse behaviors with
     behavioral outcomes. Behavioral outcomes are the results of behaviors, not the behaviors themselves.
     Behavior changes are the most reliable determinants for assessing the effectiveness of influence because
     they are directly related to objectives. See chapter 3 of this TM for a discussion of behavior and behavioral
     outcomes in further detail.

ATTITUDE

     1-15. The American Psychology Association defines attitude as an enduring and general evaluation of an
     object, person, group, issue, or concept on a dimension ranging from negative to positive. An attitude is the
     TAs (or local population) evaluation of something tangible or intangible (host-nation and/or partner nation
     troops, an event, or an adversary). Relevance of the causal link between attitude and behavior is discussed
     in chapter 3.

CAUSE

     1-16. Cause refers to a single stimulus or condition contributing to or leading to a TA’s behavior. Causes
     motivate or trigger a behavior.

EFFECT

     1-17. An effect is the result, outcome, or consequence of an action (JP 3-0). Effects may be intended or
     unintended and have positive, neutral, or negative consequences. Effects may be the result of deliberate
     activities, unintended actions, spontaneous events, or other factors.

     1-18. Observing effects and a determining, to the degree possible, the cause of the effect is critical in the
     analysis and evaluation of influence efforts. Soldiers conduct cause-and-effect analysis during TAA

EFFECTIVENESS

     1-19. Effectiveness refers to the degree to which influence activities are responsible for causing the desired
     effects. How the change occurred is just as important as why it occurred. It is insufficient to only note that a
     change has occurred; gaining insights into the cause of this change, either resulting from efforts or
     something else, is vital.

     1-20. A measure of effectiveness (MOE) is an indicator used to measure a current system state, with change
     indicated by comparing multiple observations over time (JP 5-0). Within the influence process, MOEs
     measure the extent to which the desired effect has been achieved because of influence activities.

PERFORMANCE

     1-21. A measure of performance (MOP) is an indicator used to measure a friendly action tied to measuring
     task accomplishment (JP 5-0). Performance refers to measuring how an activity was executed according to
     specific task standards. Measures of performance indicate whether the dissemination occurred at the
     selected time, location, frequency, manner, to the selected TA, and whether the TA understood the message
     as intended. MOP data is particularly relevant to the influence process activity of execute since it’s used to
     assess the distribution and dissemination activities. With both messaging and actions, an MOP is a simple
     measure of task performance and frequently yields a yes or no answer. An MOP informs us of task
     execution, where an MOE informs us whether it made a difference. Measuring performance is not
     sufficient within the assess activity. Deciding why an activity was performed to a specified degree can also
     be important.

OBJECTIVES

     1-22. An objective is the clearly defined, decisive, and attainable goal toward which an operation is
     directed (JP 5-0). For influence activities, all objectives tie to the behaviors of foreign individuals and
     groups selected for influence. Influence objectives explicitly address the behavioral component of a
     commander’s operational plans and objectives—how to get selected foreign individuals and groups to act
     in ways they would not do so unilaterally.

Psychological Objective

     1-23. A PO is a statement that reflects the desired psychological effect. A PO is a clearly defined and
     attainable behavior desired of a single, foreign target audience. Planners develop multiple POs to support
     achieving the supported commander’s objectives and desired end state. Soldiers use the criteria specific,
     measurable, achievable, relevant, and time or condition-bound (SMART) to write a PO. If any of these
     elements are lacking (either directly in the objective statement, or in the operational context), it is likely not
     a valid objective. Time or condition-bound POs are tied to distinct events, timelines, or measurable
     conditions or behaviors. Typically, the farther out in time the commander’s end state, the longer progress
     will need to be measured. For example, a PO tied to a single election would become invalid once the
     election cycle ends, whereas a PO tied to conditions that include repeated voting behaviors would require
     continuous analysis and evaluation until the condition could be considered enduring.

Supporting Psychological Objective

     1-24. An SPO is the desired behavior of a foreign target audience that contributes to accomplishing a
     psychological objective. Each SPO is a clearly defined and attainable behavior desired of a single, foreign
     TA and must assist in achieving the psychological objective. If two or more SPOs cannot be developed for
     a PO, then the PO is too narrowly focused and should be rewritten. The ability to write SMART SPOs
     helps validate the PO. If measurable indicators cannot be created, then the analysis used to create the SPO
     should be revisited.

Intermediate Psychological Objective

     1-25. An intermediate psychological objective is the desired change in the psychological conditions of a
     foreign target audience that progresses the audience toward an SPO. The IPOs cover the gap between the
     current TA behaviors and the SPO. This coverage addresses how to move the TA in incremental stages
     from the current behavior towards a desired behavior. IPOs directly relate to the hierarchy of psychological
     effects model (HPEM) and allow monitoring of a TA’s progression or regression for a desired behavior.
     IPOs are often written as informed estimates, behaviors indicative or suggestive of cognitive and attitudinal
     conditions, or precursory behaviors that are likely to occur before the behavior objective is achieved.
     Examples of behavior include those that indicate—

          • Awareness.
          • Understanding.
          • Preferred attitude.
          • Preference for action.
          • The intent to act.

     1-26. See chapter 3 for details on the application of the HPEM in the assess activity.

CRITERIA, INDICATORS, AND THRESHOLDS

     1-27. A criterion is a standard on which a judgment or decision may be based. Soldiers develop initial
     criteria during planning, often stated as MOP or MOE. For each desired effect, several criteria are
     developed that provide requirements for information and intelligence collection. More information on
     determining criteria is in chapter 2.

     1-28. Indicators are quantitative or qualitative factors or variables that signify, measure, or evaluate change.
     Quantitative indicators provide numerical or statistical measurements and are specific, measurable, and
     observable. Qualitative indicators are more descriptive and complex analyzing the individual and/or
     environmental context and/or conditions. Using qualitative indicators relies more on judgment and should
     include multiple data points that triangulate, or point, toward a particular conclusion. Qualitative indicators
     can provide detailed understanding, context, and support for quantitative data. Initial indicators establish a
     baseline from which to compare additional data and identify trends.

     1-29. Behaviors are the most concrete indicators, but indicators for attitude are obtainable in some
     circumstances. Sentiment and keyword analysis via web-based analytics have increased the validity and
     reliability of attitudinal indicators. Further, observation of suggestive or precursory behaviors can inform
     planners of attitudinal changes toward a behavior objective. For example, if the behavior objective is the
     withdrawal of hostile forces from a particular zone, the dismantling of an adversary encampment is a
     precursory behavior that might suggest a force has shifted its attitude and progressed to a preference to
     withdrawal. Further indicators will be required to verify as this behavior could also indicate a preference to
     advance. See chapter 3 for a discussion of causal links between attitude and behavior in greater detail.
     Analyzing indicators over time can show trends, reduce uncertainty, and inform planners whether IPOs and
     SPOs are achieved, or even achievable. If analysis and assessment indicate certain objectives are
     unachievable, then planners will have to revise them or develop new ones. Finally, in crisis and especially
     in armed conflict scenarios, the depth and breadth of required information is simply unavailable for a
     variety of reasons. For example, in the high operating tempo of large-scale combat operations, indicators
     for enemy combatants will overwhelmingly be behavioral rather than attitudinal due to the fast pace of
     operations and the fog of war.

     1-30. Thresholds are achievement levels set when the analysis and evaluation criteria are developed.
     Thresholds rarely consist of absolutes but rather degrees of achievement that, when reached, inform
     planners that an audience has progressed toward or regressed from a behavior objective. Thresholds define
     the intended amount of desired change. When the change in an attitude or behavior reaches a specified
     level, the influence effort meets the stated objective. Influence efforts then progress further along a
     continuum toward an objective. Thresholds may be articulated relative to another condition (for example,
     20 percent fewer attacks than last month) or as specific levels (for example, no more than two attacks per
     week).

FORECAST VERSUS PREDICT

     1-31. Forecasts are probability estimates, often with varying accuracy over time and as conditions change.
     Psychological Operations units provide the commander with behavior forecasts. Behavior forecast accuracy
     can improve over time as continual analysis and evaluation increases understanding of the OE and the
     degree to which variables influence TAs forecasts are not absolute.
     1-32. Predictions, unlike forecasts, are expressed in more definitive terms: what will happen, as opposed to
     what may happen. Predicting human behavior discounts the constant change in the environment and
     overstates the capability of behavior science application. Predictions are not used.

                                                 Chapter 2

                                     Assess

    Assess is the continual activity for measuring progress toward achieving objectives by
    analyzing indicators and evaluation of the overall impact of a series on TA behavior.
    Analysis, evaluation, and monitoring provides the information required to produce an
    assessment, which is an episodic or periodic means of reporting findings. At any given time,
    a series is only one of numerous competing influences on a TA’s behavior. Consequently,
    determining the impact of influence activities on TA behavior is complex and requires that
    objective measures be supplemented by a degree of subjective evaluation. This chapter
    discusses the assess methodology process and planning to produce timely and accurate
    assessments.

ASSESS AND THE INFLUENCE PROCESS

     2-1. Assess requires practical application in operations to be meaningful and useful. Assess is part of the
     operations process conducted in conjunction with planning, preparing, and execution—assess is not a
     stand-alone process, but integral to the others. Because it is integral to the rest of the operations process
     from beginning to end, it is continuous. This chapter discusses how assessing applies across the operations
     process. For more information on operation assessment, refer to ATP 5-0.3.

     2-2. The operations process is the Army’s framework for organizing and putting command and control into
     action. The major command and control activities performed during operations are planning, preparing,
     executing, and continuously assessing the operation (ADP 5-0). The influence process is closely aligned
     with the operations process enabling supported or supporting commanders of Psychological Operations
     units to understand their OE; visualize and describe the end state and operational approach through IPOs,
     SPOs, and POs; make and articulate decisions; and direct, lead, and assess operations and activities.
     The influence process is a systematic method for planning, preparing, executing, and assessing influence
     activities. It consists of the following five activities, two of which are continuous: TAA (continuous), plan,
     prepare, execute, and assess (continuous). Refer to FM 3-53 for additional information on the influence
     process.

     2-3. In general terms, assessing involves methodically comparing predicted outcomes with actual outcomes
     to determine the overall effectiveness of force employment and their activities. For influence activities like
     military information support operations (MISO) and deception, assessment evaluates the series’
     effectiveness to change the behavior of selected targets and target audiences. Assessment has two types of
     indicators, MOP and MOE. Psychological Operations units use both but focus more heavily on an MOE as
     they are harder to collect and analyze.

     2-4. The initial act of assessing captures the baseline data from which indicators are identified, set, and
     measured. The initial influence assessment captures the data in a product. Monitoring the current situation
     by observing indicators facilitates the evaluation of progress measured against approved objectives. This
     progress is then described in an assessment product highlighting how indicators have changed in relation to
     approved objectives. Assessing influence activities broadly consists of the following:

          • Monitoring the current situation to collect information relevant to objectives.
          • Evaluating progress toward a desired end state, achieving objectives, and performing tasks.
          • Recommending revisions to the influence plan.

STEP 1 – DEVELOP THE ASSESS APPROACH

     2-6. Developing the influence-focused approach is inherently linked to an overall operational approach,
     which includes POs, a potential TA list, and lines of effort or phasing, if applicable. Therefore, planners,
     unit leaders, and especially TA analysts outline the plan to assess influence activities during the plan
     activity to align with the supported organization’s assessment approach. This alignment requires integration
     with the J/G/S-3 and staff working groups to understand the approach used by the supported operational
     headquarters for operations in the information environment.

     2-7. An approach is the precursor or developmental stage of a plan. The approach for assessing defines the
     problem before developing solutions. The approach examines considerations and conditions that will shape
     and inform a plan.

     2-8. The approach identifies information requirements needed to monitor and analyze conditions associated
     with achieving objectives. In doing so, Psychological Operations Soldiers attempt to answer the following
     questions:

          • How will we know we are progressing toward POs?
          • What information do we need?
          • Who is best postured to provide that information?

     2-9. The approach provides the framework for the plan to assess, including initial decisions about
     organization and prioritization of analysis and evaluation efforts. The approach is an input to developing
     the plan to assess and matures as understanding of the OE is refined.

STEP 2 – DEVELOP THE PLAN

     2-10. Step two of the process is developing a plan to assess progress and publish assessments. This plan
     should follow the logic of the higher headquarters operation plan or order, which provides the POs and or
     SPOs that need measuring and monitoring. Developing plans to assess is complex and requires
     participation from various staff sections and should include any capability that can contribute to the effort.
     The plan to assess is developed using the following six steps:

          • Step 2a – Gather tools and assessment data.
          • Step 2b – Understand current and desired conditions.
          • Step 2c – Develop a framework.
          • Step 2d – Develop the collection plan.
          • Step 2e – Assign responsibilities for conducting analysis and generating recommendations.
          • Step 2f – Identify feedback mechanisms.

Step 2a – Gather Tools and Assessment Data

     2-11. Planning begins with receipt of mission. The receipt of mission alerts the staff to begin developing
     and updating their running estimates and gathering the tools necessary for mission analysis and continued
     planning. Specific tools and information gathered regarding assessment include the following:

          • The higher echelon headquarters’ plan or order, including the assessment annex if available.
          • Any current assessments and assessment products.
          • Relevant assessment of classified or unclassified products produced by civilian and government

                organizations.
          • Potential data sources, including academic institutions and civilian subject matter experts.
          • Target audience analysis worksheets (TAAWs).
          • Potential target audience list (PTAL) or refined list of selected target audiences once available.
          • Operation plan or order to include POs, SPOs, and IPOs, risk assessments, attribution methods,

                and approved target audiences, themes, symbols, and other relevant information.

Step 2b – Understand Current and Desired Conditions

     2-12. Understanding current conditions relies on information provided through the continuous activity of

     2-21. MOPs indicate how well Soldiers or systems execute an operation or activity. Such indicators could

   Applying the Hierarchy of Psychological Effects Model

    The HPEM was developed to mitigate the gap between the current TA behaviors and the SPO
    through the use of intermediate objectives. The HPEM was adapted from validated
    foundational research in communication theory to include expectancy value theory (EVT),
    the extension of expectancy value theory to media, and the theory of reasoned action. The
    latter expands EVT for greater emphasis on behavior intentions and perceived behavioral
    control. In simplest terms, the HPEM provides an adaptable framework of attitudinal and
    behavioral considerations for moving a target audience along a continuum toward a desired
    behavior articulated as a SPO. This framework is intended as a tool to assist in the thought
    processes of conducting influence activities. The following chapter outlines how HPEM can
    guide planning and direct analysis and evaluation into a logical feedback loop of plan,
    execute, assess, and revise.

HIERARCHY OF PSYCHOLOGICAL EFFECTS MODEL AND
OPERATIONAL APPLICATIONS

     3-1. The HPEM is a framework for influence practitioners. It enables tailoring a series of actions and
     messages that support a TA’s progression toward achieving the desired behavior IPOs, when developed
     based on the HPEM, become the assessment points or markers that are evaluated at defined intervals. These
     points or markers focus the analysis and evaluation during the assess activity. This model aligns the
     Soldiers’ practices with methods and theories in communication, learning, and motivation that support
     behavior change in broader populations.

     3-2. Various hierarchy of effects models have been applied since 1898, primarily as communication models
     used to encourage behavior change. The basic hierarchy of effects CAB model incorporates cognition (C),
     affect (A), and behavior (B) stages. The CAB model provides a starting point for considering sequenced
     approaches to influence. The HPEM is an expansion developed for complex audiences and/or behaviors
     and variable condition sets. This is particularly relevant when an audience is unfamiliar with the desired
     behavior captured in military objectives and articulated to the TA through influence efforts.

     3-3. Thinking about behavior as a series of actions allows an influencer to better understand how behavior
     changes over time and what influences or supports its continuation or abstinence. This understanding
     provides opportunities to target aspects of a behavior. The process can be thought of as a pathway of
     change in which a Psychological Operations Soldier works to identify and understand the steps or small
     behavior (or belief or attitude) changes needed to progress toward the desired behavior supporting the
     commander’s objective(s).

     3-4. Using the HPEM, Soldiers consider awareness, understanding, attitude, preference, behavior intention,
     and behavior achievement. The HPEM focuses influence efforts, narrows the scope of indicators needed at
     a given point in the series, and allows for greater accuracy and timeliness when determining progression or
     digression toward the behavior objective.

     3-5. TAA determines where on the HPEM a TA falls given the specified SPO. At a minimum, TAA should
     determine where the majority of the TA falls within the model. The most accurate analyses will determine
     what percentage of the TA falls within each stage of the model. For example, 10 percent of the audience is
     in awareness, 12 percent in understanding, 8 percent in attitude, 35 percent in preference, 20 percent in
     intent, and 5 percent in behavior achievement and 10 percent is unknown. The assess activity monitors TA
     progress along the HPEM continuum by observing indicators related to each stage of the series
     Implementing the hierarchy of psychological effects model requires focusing on objectives through each
     stage and requires continual analysis and evaluation of conditions. Progress indicators provide evidence
     that the TA is moving toward, away from, or not moving toward the behavior objective identified in the

     3-8. This section provides details on the HPEM stages. The HPEM is used in TAA to understand the TAs

INTERMEDIATE PSYCHOLOGICAL OBJECTIVES

     3-10. An IPO, when applying the HPEM, are the changes from a TA before moving from one HPEM stage
     to the next. Assessing an IPO informs and defines indicator and threshold achievement and monitors the
     TA’s progression, stagnation, or digression toward the SPO, thus providing insights into the effectiveness
     of series messages and actions. Where the PO and SPO are rigid, IPOs are fluid, in that they are
     continuously tracked, refined, and adapt to attitudinal and behavioral changes in the TA in relation to the
     SPO.

     3-11. Following the HPEM, IPOs begin with knowledge and attitudinal objectives, as needed, becoming
     increasingly behavioral in nature, until the desired behavior articulated as the SPO is achieved. When a
     series is examined in its totality, it can be difficult to determine when—and to what extent—an influence
     effort contributed to change. Assessing specific IPOs enable a greater understanding of the “why” behind
     TA behavior, allowing for greater precision when refining an influence effort and identifying external
     factors that may have contributed to the TA behavior.

     3-12. For example, if the PO is increase participation in the electoral process and a subordinate SPO is TA
     increases voter registration, indicators of increased attendance at political rallies does not necessarily
     indicate support. Determining if the preference is to vote or to deter others from voting requires a deeper
     analysis. Assessing TA awareness, understanding, and attitude may provide a clearer picture of whether the
     TA’s preference is positive, negative, or neutral. Influence efforts can then be directed at the TA either
     becoming positive in preferring to vote, or at least neutral so not to be a deterrent to others.

     3-13. Indicators and thresholds for each stage are established during planning and revised as needed based
     on results of assess. Soldiers prepare and execute a series of actions and messages based on the IPOs that
     move the TA from current behavior to desired behavior. These IPOs are continually monitored and
     evaluated as part of the assess activity.

     3-14. Assessing IPOs depends on identifying indicators that most accurately portray a TA in relation to a
     given SPO. As series achieve IPOs or not, Soldiers conducting the assess activity advise planners to assist
     in decisions to initiate series revisions like modifying execution timelines and adjusting approved IPOs.

Awareness

     3-15. Awareness consists of two interconnected parts, the awareness of messages and actions of a series
     and topical awareness related to the objective of the series. Performance indicators (MOP) for awareness
     determine if the TA has been exposed to the messages of the series. Topical awareness can be determined
     by analyzing TA behaviors such as relevant dialog posted on social media, statements made during protests
     or gatherings, or information-seeking behaviors related to the objective. For example, to determine topical
     awareness for an objective accept a peace treaty, requires determining first if the TA is aware that a peace
     treaty exists. If the TA is aware, the Psychological Operations Soldier must determine which information
     sources are having the greatest effect on the TA’s understanding. Indicators of awareness assist in
     identifying which information is influencing the TA.

Understanding

     3-17. The goal of a series for the understanding stage is to ensure attitudes toward an objective are
     informed by purposefully crafted messages that shape understanding favorable to SPO accomplishment.
     Assessing understanding relies on reviewing the TAAW in context of the objective. Members of a culture
     typically share similar values and norms as well as the same laws and general practices. An audience will
     often have similar experiences and shared information sources.

     3-18. Consequently, it is possible for groups to have very similar perceptions including cultural bias, group
     think, or other cognitive biases. Referencing the TAAW during assess provides the TAs information
     sources, degree of exposure, and perceptions of source credibility.

     3-19. Successful cross-cultural communications require the influence practitioner to develop relevant,
     accurate, appropriate, and meaningful messages. Consequently, ensuring a TA understands the message
     may mean first increasing the TA’s knowledge. For example, consider the SPO 70 percent of the TA
     registers to vote prior to January 2018. If the behavior objective of this SPO were introduced to a target
     audience that had never participated in an open and free election, the target audience may be aware of the
     messages about voting but have limited understanding of the concept to develop an attitude.

     3-20. The audience’s likely understanding of an objective’s topic should be initially evaluated on a scale of
     low, medium, or high based off data presented in the TAA. Further, this estimate of understanding should
     be reported by percentages of the audience. For example, 75 percent of the TA is assessed to have a low
     understanding of the objective with 25 percent estimated at a medium understanding. These estimations
     allow for thresholds to be established from which progress is measured. The TAs understanding can be
     gleaned from knowledge or experiences like the objective topic, becoming a point of reference from which
     the series can build increased understanding.

Attitude

     3-21. Assessing attitude yields sentiment information but if the analysis for awareness and understanding is
     absent, the cause(s) and motivation(s) underlying that sentiment will not be as robust or comprehensive.
     Each IPO related to HPEM stages informs the next IPO progressing the influence efforts and providing a
     feedback loop.

     3-22. Understanding the potential for a link between attitude and behavior enables a series to be planned
     with shaping actions to aid in the achievement of behavioral objectives. Attitude alone may be a weak
     indicator of eventual behavior change. If an attitude is assessed for a context-specific behavior objective, it
     can indicate a target audience’s preparedness to select one behavior over another under certain
     circumstances. The goal of the Psychological Operations Soldier is to understand, to the degree possible,
     the factors (conditions, motivators, culture, and barriers) that affect the intentions of a TA and ability to
     perform the behavior objective. This understanding is critical for those who want to influence a target
     audience’s decision-making processes.

     3-23. Attitude is assessed as negative, neutral, or positive. It can be recorded broadly by these three
     categories or scaled from negative to positive with zero being neutral, for example: (-1 -2 -3 -4 -5 0 +1 +2

     example, greater than 50 percent of the TA is assessed to be in an attitudinal range of +2 - +4. The
     objective determines threshold and effect size for a specific audience. Sometimes, the attitudinal goal might
     be to move an audience toward a negative attitude or to remain neutral.

     are those with a clear correlation between the objective and the motive of a behavior. Indirect indicators

Preference

     3-26. Preference is most closely related to attitude and is achieved when a target audience’s attitude
     changes to the point of liking one choice more than another. Preference is observed through precursor
     behaviors such as alignment of TA media selection, information gathering related to the behavior objective,
     public discourse, and the presentation of the TA’s own arguments in support of the objective. The degree of
     motivation to elaborate, or thoughtfully consider, messages is as important to influence as the strength or
     weakness of the arguments made in the messages. An audience highly motivated by the likely outcome of
     the behavior objective will expend more effort to understand the messages presented.

     3-27. The attitude required to reach the preference stage may be negative or positive, as both are strong
     motivators. An audience may have attitudes so negative towards something that they will develop an
     attitude of preferring to behave in opposition to it. To develop messages that best target an audience along
     their latitude of acceptance, the influence practitioner needs to assess degrees of susceptibility to a message.
     The three broad factors that contribute to susceptibility of a message are motivation, culture, and
     conditions. Degrees of susceptibility vary depending on the number of related beliefs held by the TA,
     attitude towards an issue dependent on perceived value and relevance.

Intention

     3-28. The intention stage is reached when the TA demonstrates or expresses a desire or willingness to
     perform the behavior of the SPO even when encountering specific barriers to the desired action. Continuing
     with the example SPO of the TA registering to vote, an example of expressed intention and performance
     barriers is a member of the target audience asking for assistance in getting to a voter registration site or
     expressing concerns over security at the registration site. The identified barriers of getting to the voting site,
     or security concerns, prevent achieving the behavior objective. The planner may have to arrange for
     transportation or security in addition to messages that complement this action so that the level of perceived
     control over the performance of the behavior objective remains stable.

     3-29. The intentions and perceived behavioral control are assessed to help determine the likelihood of the
     target audience conducting the behavior. The greater the perceived control the TA has over the behavior,
     the more accurate the behavior forecast may be.

Behavior

     3-30. The behavior stage is the portion of the series between intention and the TA achieving the behavior
     objective of the SPO to a degree required to be considered successful. It is critical for planners to ensure
     influence efforts continue until the objective threshold is met. The behavior objective will be written
     directly from the SPO with the inclusion of threshold information that defines achievement.

     3-31. Once the assess activity determines thresholds have been met that determine a behavior objective has
     been achieved, the effort for that behavior objective is either determined complete, as when the behavior
     was episodic like casting a vote, or efforts shift to monitoring and reinforcement. If the behavior objective
     is a behavior that needs to be sustained a new series may be developed to reinforce the achieved behavior
     and monitor the TA for any deviation.

                                   Research

    As previously stated at the beginning of this publication, assessment determines progress
    toward accomplishing a task, creating a condition, or achieving an objective. Any
    determination of progress requires an understanding of the desired end-state, the starting
    position, and indicators of change. Research, in the context of the assess activity, is the effort
    of systematically and continually gathering information to provide the understanding needed
    to measure progress. This chapter examines research conducted as part of military processes
    and how these processes provide a methodology of research applicable to assessment.

RESEARCH IN MILITARY PROCESSES

     4-1. Research begins with questions derived from the objective, an analysis of what is known, and an
     estimate of what needs to be known to answer the research questions and meet the objective. The objective
     provides the research topic, while the unknown provides the gaps that guide the research effort. During the
     military decision-making process (MDMP) and the joint planning process, Soldiers conduct research to
     gather information about the mission and OE, to include current behaviors that hinder, facilitate, or have no
     effect on operations and the potential TAs who may exhibit those behaviors.

     4-2. While conducting mission analysis, Soldiers research to gain a deeper understanding of the OE,
     including political, military, economic, social, information, infrastructure, physical environment, and time
     (PMESII-PT) factors that may impact the mission. They gather information on the capabilities and
     intentions of any potential adversaries or allies. During the center of gravity analysis, military planners
     identify the critical factors that support the adversary’s ability to operate effectively and determine how to
     undermine or attack those factors. In each of these examples, research remains focused on the objective.

     4-3. Analysts use clearly defined objectives in gap analysis to determine the relevance and breadth of
     information gathered during research. For the Psychological Operations Soldier, this means addressing
     gaps relating to objectives, MOPs, and MOEs. A systematic approach to research, employing thoughtful
     design and methods, enables the consideration of information through various perspectives and that the
     approach is purposeful and focused.

     4-4. Unfocused research increases the risk of fixating on superficial data and leaving information gaps
     unfilled. Further, understanding what research has been done to date provides Psychological Operations
     Soldiers with a baseline to determine what additional research is necessary without duplicating efforts. For
     example, the commander’s staff conducts research to understand the OE. There may be no need to
     duplicate this effort, but Psychological Operations Soldiers need to know where to find information on the
     OE and determine its relevance to influence.

     4-5. Research may be conducted simultaneously within various staff functions and at multiple echelons
     emphasizing the value of coordination and collaboration between Psychological Operations staff and other
     staff elements. Psychological Operations should be integrated early within planning teams for mission
     specific analysis. The ability to articulate influence objectives in terms that align directly with command
     objectives and staff processes increases interoperability with various staff elements. This also increases
     command understanding of the influence effort as it relates to command priorities.

     and question. Research methodology is the strategy or framework for conducting research and includes how to
     reach the target audience, sampling size and procedure, and how to gather and analyze data. In permissive
     environments, research methods such as observation, questionnaire, and interview provide techniques,
     procedures, and tools for collecting, analyzing, and interpreting data. In hostile and uncertain environments,

     4-7. Data is collected from primary and secondary sources. Primary data is collected directly from the main

     4-8. The assessment process often requires using primary and secondary research and data sources.

     upon in austere or combat environments due to communication difficulties, access, and rapidly changing

     question. After choosing research methods for primary data collection, the Psychological Operations

RESEARCH PLANNING

     4-10. Research planning is imbedded within Step 3, 4, and 5 of the Assessment Process. Planning research

     documenting/communicating results. The Psychological Operations Soldier first identifies questions that
     need answering. These questions can be associated with the commander or operation objectives, or they
     could be associated with the influence objectives MOP and MOE. Developing a research plan follows a
     cyclic pattern of plan, prepare, research, produce, and archive, as detailed in segment box on page 29.

RESEARCH METHODS

     4-11. Research methods include, but not limited to, observation, interview, focus group, questionnaire,
     tracking usage, or examining existing intelligence, databases, or other sources. Psychological Operations
     Soldiers utilize multiple research methods as appropriate for answering the research question(s). Research
     methods typically fall into the two categories of qualitative and quantitative. The research purpose and
     questions guide which research method(s) to use.

     4-12. Quantitative research methods measure variables and test hypotheses yielding data that is numbers-
     based, countable, or measurable through instrumentation and/or statistical or mathematical analysis.
     Quantitative examples include experimental research and survey research (such as, Likert scales or
     questionnaires with specified responses choices). Quantitative data tells us how many, how much, how
     often, or how effective in calculations. The researcher uses statistical analysis to understand quantitative
     data relationships and patterns to include averages, correlations, probabilities, and regression. Quantitative
     research generally uses deductive reasoning where a general idea is studied working toward specific
     conclusions.

4-13. Qualitative research methods study issues in depth and detail where the researcher plays a role as an
instrument (for example, researcher-as-instrument) and looks for meaning or explanation. Qualitative
examples include focus groups, interviews, and case study. Qualitative data is descriptive and narrative. It
aims to capture what people say about the issues being studied and includes quotations, observations, and
document excerpts. It provides details into someone else’s experiences in their own words. Evaluating
qualitative data requires inductive reasoning and some interpretation by the researcher, taking specific
observations and building toward general patterns. Qualitative data can help the researcher understand why,
how, or what happened behind certain behaviors.

4-14. To better understand the relationship between qualitative and quantitative data in research the
following example examines survey research. A survey collects data from participants who respond to a
series of questions about behaviors and opinions, often in the form of a questionnaire or an interview.
The standard survey format allows individuals a level of anonymity in which they can express personal
ideas. A survey is provided to a sample of a larger specific population. After determining how to sample
the population (that is, the sampling procedure) and selecting participants, the researcher develops a
specific plan to ask questions and record responses.

4-15. A common survey instrument is a questionnaire. Participants often answer a series of closed-ended
questions. The researcher might ask yes-or-no or multiple-choice questions (forced response) where
participants choose the response that best fits them. This kind of questionnaire collects data that can be
numerated (quantitative) and statistically analyzed into percentages or other statistical relationships.

4-16. Questionnaires can also ask more complex questions beyond “yes,” “no,” or checkbox options. These
types of inquiries use open-ended questions that require short answer or essay responses either in writing or
through verbal conversation. These inquiries provide more in-depth or detailed information about an issue
or subject such as personal religious beliefs, political views, goals, or morals. This type of personal
explanation is descriptive and narrative qualitative data. The researcher often receives or obtains a wide
range of responses. Consequently, qualitative data may be harder to organize and tabulate. However,
organizing extensive and detailed information into themes or contextual relationships, offers a more in-
depth or nuanced analysis. Qualitative data that is thematically organized can also be analyzed for trends
over time. The themes can be numerated, that is, converted to quantitative data. This requires clearly
recording which narrative comments constitute a particular theme. The assessment team determines rules or
criteria for inclusion into a theme.

RESEARCH OBSTACLES

     4-17. This section will briefly examine these obstacles and provide mitigation considerations. Bias and
     noise are obstacles to research that must be mitigated through awareness and understanding. Bias occurs
     when past experiences or beliefs impact how or what you research. Bias can manifest itself in ways as
     simple as past browsing history that results in an algorithm directing you to data like past findings. Bias is
     best mitigated by first recognizing it and then thinking critically about the research question, objective, and
     target audience. This entails examining each problem set or question from multiple viewpoints or
     perspectives and addressing ways that bias may be present in research.

     4-18. Noise is a result of information quantity, or information overload. Discerning between relevant and
     irrelevant information requires continuously revisiting the original research question and related objective.
     Sifting and sorting information is a critical skill for researchers. The digital environment has increased
     information volume and transmission speed influencing a person’s attention to and analysis of information
     sources, credibility, reliability, and validity. In addition, identifying sources of and objectives for
     information outputs may be challenging if noise is intentionally injected into the information environment
     to distract critical examination of a topic.

     4-19. It is impossible to remove all bias from research. However, awareness, planning, and actively
     practicing mitigation strategies results in greater accuracy and increased relevancy of findings. The
     following segment in an overview of examples of bias in research and strategies for reducing bias.

                                            Examples of Bias

          Cognitive bias. Errors in a person’s thinking that result from how information is
          processed. Types of cognitive bias include the following:

               • Anchoring bias. When the first piece of information received is given
                     excessive weight or value and it becomes a reference point (anchor) for
                     future information.

               • Availability heuristic. A common strategy for making judgments about
                     likelihood of occurrence in which the individual bases such judgments on the
                     salience of the information held in their memory.

               • Confirmation bias. Tendency to gather information confirms existing
                     expectations and rejecting or dismissing information that contradicts those
                     beliefs.

               • Framing effect. How information is presented (positively or negatively) can
                     influence our opinion and decisions.

               • Fundamental attribution error. Attributing behavior more to internal factors
                     such as personal characteristics as opposed to external or situational factors.

               • Hindsight bias. The tendency, after an event has occurred, to overestimate
                     the extent to which the outcome could have been foreseen.

               • Others to include self-serving bias, inattentional blindness, loss aversion,
                     gambler’s fallacy, availability cascade, and bandwagon effect.

          Interviewer bias. Refers to an interviewer’s expectations, beliefs, and prejudices as
          they influence the interview process and the interpretation of the data it provides.
          Publication bias. The tendency for study results that are published in journals or
          other outlets to differ from study results that are not published.
          Researcher bias. When the researcher’s beliefs or expectations influence the
          research design, data collection, or data interpretation.

RESEARCH AND ASSESSMENT

     4-21. The assessment process is anchored by the framework of the influence process. Likewise, research
     for assessing influence is guided by the POs, SPOs, and IPOs of the influence process. The influence
     process objectives help identify the research questions that need answers. By starting with the objective, the
     researcher can identify what is known and what gaps need to be filled through research. After articulating
     the questions, a design plan takes shape and is based on the methods best suited to discover indicators of
     TA progression or regression related to the objective.

     4-22. Measures of performance answer the question was a task performed properly or correctly? MOP may
     be simple yes/no answers or quantitative, depending on the relevant questions asked. Examples of MOP
     include was the appropriate TA selected, did the TA understand the message as intended, the frequency of
     exposure to an influence message, or completion of an action for psychological effect. The research design
     for MOP can rely heavily on secondary methods as reports from others will answer many of the research
     questions related to performance. MOE are more focused on assessing the overall success of the operation
     in achieving its broader objectives. Indicators of effectiveness rely on both primary and secondary sources
     to provide qualitative and quantitative data for determining progress. MOP informs analysts about proper
     task execution, where MOE indicates whether it made a difference.

     4-23. POs, SPOs, and IPOs of a series are written with defined thresholds of success. Research design
     should consider these thresholds when determining methods. Research design is MOE-centric and heavily
     focused on determining progress toward achieving objectives. The design may use quantitative and/or
     qualitative assessment depending on the objective, questions, and the information that best measures or
     assesses progress. For example, if an IPO threshold is a percentage of a TA exhibiting X-specific behavior,
     then the research question would be, what percentage of the population successfully exhibited that
     behavior. The research methods selected would intend to identify quantitative data reportable as indicators
     of effectiveness, for example: an observational count of those exhibiting the behavior. In contrast, if the
     researcher wants to understand if a TAs attitude is shifting toward X-specific behavior then qualitative data
     is required which changes the research method. Obtaining this data might entail forming a focus group that
     asks questions about knowledge, beliefs, or behaviors related to the X-specific behavior or outcomes, thus
     providing indicators that attitudes are shifting.

     4-24. This qualitative data is descriptive and, through analysis, may be categorized based on similar TA
     response patterns to create a quantitative measure of effectiveness. Attitudes can be measured using a
     yes/no response or a Likert scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree) which can be categorized and
     statistically analyzed. However, additional qualitative data may be necessary to understand these responses
     in more detail or to confirm data findings. Attitudes can also be measured by quantitatively measuring other
     indicative behaviors, behaviors that suggest a specific attitude, but qualitative measures will be required to
     confirm the data findings.

Assumption. A fact or statement taken for granted (Mirriam-Webster); a belief or idea

Case study. An in-depth investigation of a single individual, family, event, or other

Epistemology. The branch of philosophy concerned with the nature, origin, and
limitations of knowledge. It is also concerned with the justification of truth claims.
There are three levels, which include the following:

     • Objectivism. Objects have a truth and meaning independent of
          consciousness or experience.

     • Constructionism. Knowledge depends on interactions between humans and
          the world, the subject constructs reality of objects.

     • Subjectivism. Meaning exists within the subject and it is the subject that
          imposes meaning on an object.

Experimental research. Research utilizing randomized assignment of participants to
conditions and systematic manipulation of variables with the objective of drawing
causal inference.

Inference. A conclusion or opinion formed because of known facts or evidence
(Mirriam-Webster); in statistics, a conclusion about a population based on logical
reasoning from data gathered on a smaller population (American Psychology
Association [APA] Dictionary of Psychology).

Interview. A directed conversation in which a researcher, therapist, clinician,
employer, or the like (the interviewer) intends to elicit specific information from an
individual (the interviewee) for purposes of research, diagnosis, treatment, or
employment. Conducted face to face, by telephone, or online, interviews may be
either standardized, including set questions, or open ended, varying with material
introduced in responses by the interviewee. Their reliability is of particular concern,
and interviewers must be careful to minimize or eliminate personal judgment and
biases in evaluating responses. Interview methods include the following:

     • Focus group. An interview with a small group (typically 6-10 people) on a
          specific topic to obtain detailed data in a social context.

     • Group interview. An unstructured, informal group conversation.
     • Cross-cultural interview. Interviewing individuals from a different cultural

          group than the interviewer, often in the participants’ home country or region.

Research methodology. The strategy or framework for conducting research and
includes how to reach the target audience, sampling size and procedure, and how to
gather and analyze data.

Sampling. The process of selecting for study a limited number of units from a larger
set. The term most often refers to the selection of respondents, observations, or
cases for inclusion in experiments, surveys, interviews, or other research.
