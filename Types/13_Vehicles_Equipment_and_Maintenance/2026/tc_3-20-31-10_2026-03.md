                                 Preface

The purpose of TC 3-20.31-10 is to establish a standardized program for assessing and improving the
maintenance proficiency of armor crews on the M1A2 SEPv2, M1A2 SEPv3, M2A3, M2A4, and M2A4E1
and M2A3 platforms. Developed in accordance with AR 750-1 and utilizing the 8-step training model
outlined in FM 7-0, this book provides commanders with the tools necessary to plan, execute, and evaluate
Maintenance Skills Testing (MST). It details the origin and importance of MST for sustained readiness, and
offers practical, scenario-based performance checklists for critical maintenance tasks. Through consistent
application of this program, commanders can ensure their formations maintain a high level of technical
competence and operational capability.

The principal audience for TC 3-20.31-10 is all members of the profession of arms. Commanders and staffs
of Army headquarters serving as joint task force or multinational headquarters should also refer to applicable
joint or multinational doctrine concerning the range of military operations and joint or multinational forces.
Trainers and educators throughout the Army will also use this publication.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure that their decisions and actions comply with applicable United
States, international, and in some cases host-nation laws and regulations. Commanders at all levels ensure
that their Soldiers operate in accordance with the law of war and the rules of engagement. (See FM 6-27.)

The use of a trade or brand name does not constitute endorsement of any specific commercial product,
commodity, service, or enterprise by the United States Army.

TC 3-20.31-10 applies to the Active Army, Army National Guard/Army National Guard of the United States
and United States Army Reserve unless otherwise stated.

The proponent for TC 3-20.31-10 is the United States Maneuver Center of Excellence. The preparing agency
is the Department of Training and Doctrine, United States Maneuver Center of Excellence. Send comments
and recommendations on DA Form 2028 (Recommended Changes to Publications and Blank Forms) to

                                                 Chapter 1

                 Overview and Organization of MST

    Chapter one covers the origin of the concept of Maintenance Skills Testing (MST), explains
    why it is important for maintaining readiness in armored formations, and explains its structure.
    It also explains how to plan for and conduct MST according to the 8-step training model (see
    FM 7-0).

SECTION I – ORIGIN OF MST

     1-1. In September 2024 1st Armored Division (1AD) was assigned by III Armored Corps and the U.S. Army
     Armor School to lead the operator maintenance training line of effort in support of the Armor Training
     Standardization Initiative (ATSI). The operator maintenance training line of effort was added to the ATSI
     due to the III Armored Corps and U.S. Army Armor School commanders’ joint assessment that there is a
     lack of published operator maintenance training standards for the armored force. As the proponent for the
     operator maintenance line of effort in the ATSI, 1AD developed a program to establish standards for
     maintenance proficiency and certification by skill level for Soldiers in the 19C, 19D, and 19K military
     occupational specialties.

     1-2. MST is 1AD’s program to establish operator maintenance standards and was developed and piloted in
     the 1st Armored Brigade Combat Team (Ready First!) of the 1AD from October 2024 to March 2025.
     Development began with a thorough review of published doctrine on operator maintenance, which revealed
     that operator maintenance proficiency in Armored formations is only formally assessed during drivers
     training (with no standardized, vehicle specific, tables of equipment and organization [also called T&EO] on
     the preventative maintenance checks and services [PMCS] portion) and gunnery skills testing (robust
     platform specific tables of equipment and organization, limited to weapons systems). The standards for
     operator maintenance are well established and easily referenced in any equipment’s -10 technical manual,
     but 1AD assessed that there is a lack of a formal method for training and evaluating a Soldier’s ability to
     perform those operator maintenance tasks.

     1-3. The lack of a system for formal evaluation of operator maintenance proficiency has created a situation
     in today’s armored force where leaders and Soldiers frequently perform operator maintenance tasks, but are
     rarely trained to perform those tasks, and are never formally evaluated on their ability to perform those tasks
     to a doctrinal standard. The result is pockets of excellence across the armored force, carried throughout the
     force as tribal knowledge, rather than published best practice. There are few other examples of critical
     technical skills that are required for success in combat which are not formally trained or evaluated.

     1-4. Because the current operations tempo in the armored force is very high, and Armored brigade combat
     teams (ABCT) training calendars are generally packed with large-scale collective training events such as
     crew gunnery, platoon (PLT) situational training exercise/live fire exercise, combat training centers, and PLT
     services, the MST development team assessed that any operator maintenance training should be tied to an
     already existing event on ABCT training calendars, rather than creating an entirely new standalone event.

     1-5. The maintenance tasks and proficiencies targeted by the MST were developed in a series of working
     groups composed of master gunners, senior operators, and senior maintainers. After the maintenance skills
     to be trained were selected, the MST program was developed using the model of Gunnery Skills Testing
     (GST) to speed development and ease adoption. In November 2024, the first MST pilot evaluated the efficacy
     of conducting MST training and testing in conjunction with a normal gunnery Table 1 GST training event.
     This first pilot test validated that using GST as the model for MST resulted in engaging and effective training
     but revealed that gunnery Table 1 was already too congested to effectively integrate additional tasks.

     1-6. TC 3-20.31-9 details how to plan and conduct PLT services on M1 and M2 equipped PLTs by step of
     the 8-step training model but contains a noticeable gap on techniques for effectively conducting step 2:
     Training and Certifying Leaders prior to PLT services. Pilot number 2, in February 2025, tested the
     integration of MST into PLT services, as a sort of maintenance Table 1 prior to the collective training event

SECTION II – MST STRUCTURE

     1-7. MST is broken into three pillars: leader training, hands-on training, and written testing. These three
     pillars are meant to form a comprehensive approach to training operator maintenance skills. The pillars are
     complementary to one another, and the MST is meant to be conducted as a training event including elements
     of all three pillars.

PILLAR ONE: LEADER TRAINING

     1-8. Leader training in MST aims to achieve three objectives: enhance leader expertise, certify leaders in
     training Soldiers on MST hands-on tasks, and instill a sense of ownership in leaders regarding their
     equipment.

     1-9. The first objective is to provide leaders with tools, training, and study material to increase their technical
     expertise on their platform. MST assumes that by increasing leaders’ technical expertise on their platform,
     they will be better able to supervise, prioritize, and lead operator maintenance.

     1-10. The second objective of leader training is to build in a process for training and certifying leaders (step 2
     of the 8-Step technical manual) prior to MST hands-on training. Because MST is a new type of training event,
     but is conceptually very similar to gunnery skills testing, it is simple for leaders to execute without much
     preparation. Training and certifying leaders on MST hands-on are very similar to doing so for GST.

     1-11. The third objective is to help leaders take ownership of and responsibility for maintaining their
     platforms. MST leader training aims to educate leaders on how their actions, and those of their Soldiers,
     affect higher echelon maintenance systems. This understanding empowers leaders to take a more active role
     in improving systems.

     1-12. MST leader training also seeks to reduce the impact of the black-box effect that is common amongst
     junior Soldiers and leaders. The black-box effect is the tendency for operators to attribute the origin of faults
     on their vehicle to something that is outside their ability to control, understand, or prevent. This effect is
     caused by the technical complexity of combat platforms like the M1 or M2, dense and difficult to reference
     technical manuals, and generally low operator mechanical literacy. MST leader training increases operator
     agency in maintenance operations by training leaders on the linkage between their and their subordinates’
     actions and commonly occurring faults on their platform.

PILLAR TWO: HANDS-ON TRAINING AND TESTING

     1-13. Hands-on training in MST is most directly modeled on the already commonly understood GST
     conducted in ABCTs. Hands-on training uses the same approach as GST by targeting training to address the
     highest risk (defined by both frequency of occurrence and severity of consequences) operator maintenance
     tasks. Training these tasks to the standards dictated in the equipment’s technical manual increases operator
     maintenance proficiency by reducing instances of improperly performed operator maintenance resulting in
     additional faults or misdiagnosis of an existing fault.

     1-14. Hands-on training is meant to be conducted by all crewmembers assigned to an M1 or M2 platform.
     Experienced operators and maintainers are the recommended trainers for hands-on training tasks. All MST
     hands-on training tasks, conditions, and standards can be found within the MST draft booklet.

PILLAR THREE: WRITTEN TESTING

     1-15. Written testing is meant to assess leaders and Soldiers on critical operator maintenance, administrative,
     and diagnostic skills that are difficult or impossible to assess using hands-on training.

     1-16. Work is underway on the development of written tests (multiple choice and short answer) that will form
     the bulk of this pillar in the future. At end state, commanders will be able to choose from a bank of questions,
     grouped by area of concern, to automatically generate and proctor a written test addressing the maintenance
     concerns most relevant to their formation.

SECTION III – PLANNING AND CONDUCTING MST

     1-18. This section of the chapter will provide detailed guidance for planning and conducting MST according
     to the 8-step technical manual.

STEP 1: PLAN THE TRAINING

     1-19. MST, like GST, should be planned and coordinated through the annual training calendar, quarterly
     training briefs, and routine training meetings. The MST should be tied to an already existing event on ABCT
     training calendars, with large-scale collective training events such as Gunnery, PLT situational training
     exercise/live fire exercise, combat training centers, and PLT Services.

     1-20. MST is a training program that is designed to be planned at the battalion/squadron level for execution
     by their subordinate company/troop. MST recommends a 5-day model to conduct all critical leader and
     hands-on training. Planning MST at the battalion/squadron enables units to pull the best possible instructors
     from across the formation to ensure that MST is a success. It also provides units with capability to perform
     an internal evaluation similar in scope and rigor to an external evaluation, similar to GST. Despite being
     designed for planning at the battalion/squadron level, because MST is based on a familiar training model, it
     can easily be planned as a cohesive training event at the company/troop level. Portions of the MST (individual
     tasks, for example) can also easily be conducted as PLT or even crew level training events.

     1-21. The training audience for MST is all crewmembers (including leaders) assigned to M1 and M2
     platforms. All crewmembers will be trained and evaluated on their ability to meet the standards for the
     hands-on training. Leaders, defined as vehicle commanders and higher, take part in the MST leader-training
     in addition to MST hands-on training.

     1-22. The recommended trainers for MST are senior operators, master gunners, and senior maintainers in the
     formation. Pilot tests in 1AD revealed that hands-on training is best facilitated by senior operators and master
     gunners, while the leader training is most valuable if the trainers are senior maintainers.

     1-23. The doctrinal template for MST developed in 1AD recommends allocating five training days to
     complete MST hands-on and leader training. In a compressed schedule, Day 4: hands-on practice, can be cut
     to save time. MST written testing has not been integrated into this doctrinal template. The five-day MST
     training model is broken down as follows:

          • Day 1: Leader technical training.
          • Day 2: Leader hands-on validation and rehearsal.
          • Day 3: Soldier hands-on instruction/demonstration.
          • Day 4: Soldier hands-on practice.
          • Day 5: Soldier hands-on testing.

STEP 2: TRAIN AND CERTIFY LEADERS

     1-24. Training and certifying leaders in MST have two goals: to certify leaders on their ability to train and
     evaluate Soldiers on MST hands-on tasks, and to train and certify the ability of leaders to supervise and
     manage operator level maintenance. To achieve these goals, the doctrinal template for MST allocates two
     days for MST leader certification.

     1-25. Day one of leader training is oriented on certifying leaders to supervise and manage operator
     maintenance. The first portion of day one consists of classroom instruction oriented on the Army’s
     maintenance management system functions at echelon, and how operator maintenance actions interact with
     that system. The latter portion of day one focuses on increasing leader technical expertise on their platform
     by introducing leaders to instructional material that will increase their understanding of how their platform
     functions and how to evaluate whether their subordinates have done operator maintenance tasks (specifically
     PMCS) to standard.

     1-27. Day two of leader training is oriented on certifying leaders to train and evaluate Soldiers on the MST

     and that the flow of traffic between stations and timeline enables efficiently completing all training in the
     given time period.

STEP 3: RECON THE SITE

     1-29. MST is designed to be conducted in a unit’s own motorpool, with minimal set-up or additional
     resources required. MST leader training is facilitated by having vehicles in a maintenance facility with their
     powerpacks removed, but this is not required. The only requirement to conduct MST is a minimum of three
     of the appropriate combat platforms, access to the correct technical manuals, and the basic issue item (BII)
     for the equipment.

     1-30. The unit will conduct a safety assessment for the test site according to the unit standard operating
     procedure (SOP), installation, and division requirements. Safety must come before all training needs.

STEP 4: ISSUE ORDERS

     1-31. It is recommended that all MST products (booklets, station setup guides, after operations maintenance
     (also called AOM) checklists, concept of operations, and so on) except the theory of operations worksheets
     are distributed to the crew level as far in advance as possible to allow subordinates the maximum amount of
     time to plan. The Theory of Operations Worksheets should be held at the company/troop level as they will
     be used to evaluate leaders, and the digital files contain the answers by default.

STEP 5: REHEARSE

     1-32. Rehearsing MST Leader Training is crucial to a successful MST training period. MST leader training
     rehearsal should serve as a validation by the owning commander of all classes to be taught, an approval of
     the leader training station concept and timeline, and a validation of the expertise of all SMEs who will teach
     leader training classes. A simple conditions-check meeting a few days prior to leader training, with all SMEs
     and the owning and participating subordinate commanders present is sufficient rehearsal for MST leader
     training if the above topics are covered.

STEP 6: EXECUTE THE TRAINING

     1-33. Execution of MST hands-on training, as the main component of the MST, should be done in the same
     manner as rehearsed on day two of MST leader training. Pilot tests in 1AD have shown that a round robin
     style execution of hands-on training, where pre-determined groups of Soldiers move between stations on a
     set schedule is the best model for the execution of leader training. Having an open concept where Soldiers
     move between stations on their own is not recommended as some stations generate very long lines.

     1-34. It is recommended that each testing Soldier have a copy of their platform’s chapter of the MST booklet
     which they will present to graders prior to beginning testing. For crew level tasks, the vehicle commander’s
     booklet will be used. The grader will then annotate GOs/NO-GOs in this booklet as the Soldier/crew is
     evaluated. Graders will terminate testing as soon as a Soldier is a NO-GO, inform them of their NO-GO, and

                                 M1A2 SEPv2

    Maintaining combat readiness of the M1A2 SEPv2 and M1A2 SEPv3 requires a highly skilled
    and proficient crew. This chapter details the standardized procedures for assessing those skills
    through the Commander’s Armor Maintenance Skills Test Program. Specifically, this chapter
    focuses on practical, task-based evaluations covering critical maintenance functions on the
    M1A2 SEPv2 and M1A2 SEPv3, including track maintenance, emergency start procedures,
    recovery preparation, and inspections of vital systems like fire suppression and air induction.
    Commanders will utilize these procedures to objectively verify crewmember proficiency in
    accordance with established standards, ensuring a consistently high level of maintenance

SECTION I – TASK 1: MAINTAIN TRACK

ACTION

     Maintain the track on an M1A2 SEPv2 and M1A2 SEPv3.

CONDITION

     Given a fully operational, parked M1A2, and TM 9-2350-388-10-4 (TM 9-2350-412-10-4 for M1A2 SEPv3).

STANDARD

     Within 5 minutes the crewmember will—
          • Open front fender.
          • Inspect outside end connector.
          • Inspect inside end connector.
          • Inspect center guide.
          • Inspect cooling tubes.
          • Inspect track pad.
          • Close front fender.

EVALUATION PROCEDURES

   • Administrative process—At this task, the evaluator logs information about each crewmember on a
        roster. The evaluator uses the crewmember with all required material and equipment, displaying
        them according to the guidance in this manual. The evaluator uses the performance measures to
        record the crewmember’s performance. Afterward, the evaluator tells the crewmember how well
        they performed on this task and directs the crewmember to the next task or to further training. One
        evaluator can test on—and only one- crewmember at a time.

   • Procedures used when personnel receive a NO-GO—If a crewmember fails to achieve the standard
        on the performance measures, they receive a NO-GO. When this happens, the evaluator explains to
        the crewmember what they did wrong and how to correct their mistakes. Then, the crewmember
        retests according to the local SOP.

TEST PLANNING TIME

   Administrative time:               5 minutes
   Test time:                    + 5 minutes
   Total time (per crewmember):
                                      10 minutes

INSTRUCTIONS TO THE CREWMEMBER

   To evaluate crewmembers, the following instructions will be provided: “In front of you is an M1A2 Abrams
   tank. You must open the front fender, inspect the serviceability of the track, and close the front fender within
   five minutes. When inspecting the serviceability of the track you must state the name of all track components
   and describe what would make the component unserviceable. Do you understand the requirements of this
   test?” (Answer questions.) “You may begin.” (Start the time.)
