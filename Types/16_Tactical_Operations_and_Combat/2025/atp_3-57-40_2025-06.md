                                 Preface

This manual describes the military’s role as an instrument of national power in the framework of transitional
governance. Civil Affairs forces support transitional governance through the execution of military
government operations. The doctrinal principles presented in this manual are intended to be used as a guide
and are not to be considered prescriptive. Civil Affairs forces conduct missions within military government
operations that are essential to the commander’s ability to consolidate gains to ensure the appropriate
establishment, control, support, and continuation of government and governance functions. Normally the
responsibility of civilian authorities, Civil Affairs forces provide expertise in civilian sector functions until
they establish conditions necessary for transfer back to civilian control. The goal for military government
operations during or post conflict, is to return governance from military control to the appropriate civil
authorities as soon as possible while maintaining basic levels of government administration and essential
services. Civil Affairs forces support the ground commander’s execution of military government operations
or enabling existing governments as necessary, enhancing existing government functions and services
affecting the civil population.

The principal audience for ATP 3-57.40 is Civil Affairs forces and planners that leverage the civil component
of the operational environment while enabling, enhancing, or providing governance. ATP 3-57.40 is
applicable to officers and senior non-commissioned officers who lead Civil Affairs forces or who serve as
the (J-9), assistant chief of staff, Civil Affairs operations (G-9), or the brigade or battalion Civil Affairs
operations staff officer (S-9).

The military government operations described in ATP 3-57.40 can only be used for operations and support
activities in foreign countries and territories. There is not an application for this material for any domestic
operations or defense support to civil authorities. Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure their decisions
and actions comply with applicable United States, international, and in some cases, host-nation laws and
regulations. Commanders at all levels ensure their Soldiers operate according to the law of war and the rules
of engagement. (See FM 6-27.)

ATP 3-57.40 uses joint terms, where applicable. Selected joint and Army terms and definitions appear in
both the glossary and the text. Terms for which ATP 3-57.40 is the proponent publication (the authority) are
marked with an asterisk (*) in the glossary. Definitions for which ATP 3-57.40 is the proponent publication
are boldfaced in the text. For other definitions shown in the text, the term is italicized, and the number of the
proponent publication follows the definition.

ATP 3-57.40 applies to the Active Army, the Army National Guard/Army National Guard of the United
States, and the United States Army Reserve unless otherwise stated.

                              Introduction

Since World War II, U. S. defense policy requires the Army to maintain the capability to occupy enemy
territory and provide for the establishment of military government. Department of Defense policy also directs
the responsibility of establishment and conduct of military government to Civil Affairs. However, the Army
has not fully exercised the use of military government since World War II. With the focus of the U. S.
military shifting to winning in large-scale combat operations, the difficulty is answering the question: “What
do we do after the enemy is defeated and military forces are required to execute military government?”

Civil Affairs enables a commander to fulfill the military contribution to transitional governance through
military government operations. All Civil Affairs forces, regardless of unit of assignment, environment of
engagement, or phase of continuum, need to train and prepare to conduct the missions within military
government operations: Support to foreign assistance, support to civil administration in host-nation territory,
and support to civil administration in occupied territory. Civil Affairs forces conduct military government
operations to support the Army operating concept of multidomain operations. Commanders use Civil Affairs
capabilities as a primary means to achieve unified action.

Civil Affairs forces conduct military government capabilities in competition to further national policies and
objectives; in conflict to assist military operations and consolidation of gains; and in post conflict to assist
the occupying commander to fulfill the obligations to the civil population imposed by international law. Civil
Affairs forces apply government and governance functions using applicable United States, partner nation,
and international laws to assist established, interim, or reconstructed governments. When conducting military
government operations, Civil Affairs forces integrate all Civil Affairs operations with the six Army stability
operations tasks. The integrated efforts enhance consolidation of gains, support from the indigenous
population, and enable the transition of operations to the legitimate civil authorities.

Chapter 1 provides an overview of military government operations including the history, authorities, and
definitions of transitional governance, military government, and military government operations. Chapter 1 discusses
the framework of transitional governance and the role of the military within that framework. The chapter
flows into discussing the execution of military government through the Army’s stability operations tasks and
discusses how those operations nest within the stabilization framework. Next, the chapter discusses the use
of transitional military authority; the missions of support to civil administration in hostile territory, support
to civil administration in host-nation territory, and support to foreign assistance. The chapter concludes by
discussing the integration of other core competencies of civil network development and engagement, civil
knowledge integration, and civil-military integration.

Chapter 2 explains the planning process and tools Civil Affairs forces use for assessing governance and
consolidating gains throughout the competition continuum. The governance assessment tools include the
governance assessment process, the Civil Affairs areas study, and Civil Affairs assessments. In addition, this
chapter discusses the ability of the commander to establish a joint civil-military operations task force to
consolidate gains during stability operations.

Chapter 3 provides a discussion of Civil Affairs forces and military government operations in the conflict
phase of the competition continuum.

Chapter 4 provides a discussion of Civil Affairs forces and military government operations in the crisis
phase of the competition continuum.

Chapter 5 provides a discussion of Civil Affairs forces and military government operations in the
competition phase of the competition continuum.

HISTORY OF MILITARY GOVERNMENT

     1-1. Military government operation’s (MGO’s) historical significance predates the founding of America as a
     nation. Historically, the U.S. Army established a military government in the aftermath of various conflicts.
     Army officers and supporting personnel conducted military government functions during regional
     pacification efforts in the United States. The U.S. Army conducted reconstruction with military government
     after combat operations in Mexico, California, U.S. Southern states, Cuba, Puerto Rico, Panama, China, the
     Philippines, Germany, and Japan. Civil War reconstruction and military control ended with the Compromise
     of 1877, which manifested into the Posse Comitatus Act. The Posse Comitatus Act ended the concept of
     military government on U.S. soil, but not as it pertained to foreign wars. The United States performed military
     government in various locations following the Spanish American War but found itself unprepared for the
     task following World War I. Colonel Erwin Hunt wrote a scathing report citing how ill-prepared the Army
     was to conduct military government in 1919 post-conflict Europe. At the onset of World War II, U.S. war
     planners used the lessons learned from Hunt’s exposé and General George Marshall’s understanding that the
     post-conflict occupation was going to be equally as challenging as the war itself. General Marshall created
     military government and CA units to facilitate the military occupation and military government in Germany
     and Japan, and the liberation of formerly occupied friendly territories as well. The occupation of
     Europe exemplifies success and is a model for the execution of military government and transition to
     civilian authorities.

AUTHORITIES AND DIRECTIVES

     1-2. Military government authority is derived from three sources: International law, the statutory authorities
     found in Title 10 and Title 22, USC, and the regulatory authority within DODD 5100.01, DODD 3000.05,
     and DODD 2000.13. Only the President of the United States and Congress can authorize the implementation
     of military government and assertion of transitional military authority (TMA).

INTERNATIONAL LAW

     1-3. Hague Convention of 1907 and customary international humanitarian law contain the rules applicable
     to belligerent occupation. Article 42 of the Hague Regulations is the accepted definition of what amounts to
     an occupation for the purposes of international law: territory is considered occupied when it is placed under
     the authority of the hostile army. The occupation extends only to the territory where the hostile authority
     establishes itself and is exercising authority.

     1-4. The Geneva Convention of 1949, Article 2, expanded the definition of occupation to that which
     encounters no armed resistance. The Hague Regulation, Article 42, outlines that for an occupation to exist,
     for the purposes of international law, hostile troops must have some degree of control over a foreign territory,

          • The unconsented-to presence of foreign forces.

     1-5. Once a foreign territory is under occupation, the occupying power becomes bound by the rules of
     international humanitarian law, whether customary or treaty based. International humanitarian law applicable
     in international armed conflict governs any armed confrontation between the armed forces of the occupied
     state and the occupying power and armed groups operating under its overall control, in addition to the rules
     of occupation. International humanitarian law governs armed groups operating under the occupying power’s
     control. International humanitarian laws appliable to international armed conflict govern any armed
     confrontations among the occupied state’s armed forces, the occupying power, and the armed groups under
     the occupying power’s control.

     1-6. The 1907 Hague Regulations (Articles 42-56), the Fourth Geneva Convention, and Additional Protocol I
     to the Four Geneva Conventions outlines the occupying power’s duties. An occupant does not have
     sovereignty over an occupied territory; therefore, the occupation is temporary. The occupant has an obligation
     to ensure the well-being of the population and is prohibited from making permanent changes to the territory
     in the judicial, economic, or social spheres. The main duties of the occupying power under international law
     are listed below:

          • The occupying power must respect the laws in force while in the occupied territory unless they
                constitute a threat to its security or are an obstacle to the application of the international law
                of occupation.

          • The occupying power must take measures to restore and ensure, as far as possible, civil life and
                public order and safety.

          • The occupying power must ensure sufficient hygiene and public health standards. The occupying
                power must also provide food and medical care to the occupied population.

TITLE 10, UNITED STATES CODE

     1-7. Title 10, USC states that Congress provide an Army that is capable, in conjunction with the other armed
     forces, of preserving the peace and security, and providing for the defense of the United States, the
     Commonwealths and possessions, and any areas occupied by the United States.

     1-8. Title 10 directs Army forces to conduct prompt and sustained land combat combined with the Army’s
     operating concept and unified land operations requirements. This law supplies the manpower and
     organization for all the operations the Army leads and supports. The Army asserting TMA to execute military
     government is a follow-on action linked to the sustained land combat and the protection of occupied areas.

     1-9. Among the command responsibilities specified in Title 10, Section 161 the primary duties of the
     commander of a combatant command are—

          • To create plans for the armed forces to execute national defense strategies and respond to significant
                military contingencies.

          • To take actions, as necessary, to deter conflict.
          • To command United States Armed Forces as directed by the Secretary and approved by the President.

TITLE 22, UNITED STATES CODE

     1-10. Army forces conduct operations to support civil administration in host-nation territory and to support
     foreign assistance during competition and crisis under the authority of Title 22, USC, which includes foreign
     humanitarian assistance guidance. Title 22 also establishes support for military assistance programs, foreign
     military financing programs, and international military education training programs. The Foreign Assistance
     Act (Title 22 USC 2151) establishes support for economic support funds, peacekeeping operations,
     international narcotics control and law enforcement programs; and nonproliferation, antiterrorism, demining,
     and related programs. The Foreign Assistance Act mandates close coordination and cooperation between the
     Department of Defense (DOD) and U.S. civilian agencies at all levels of the security assistance process.

TRANSITIONAL GOVERNANCE AND COMPETITION CONTINUUM

     1-18. MGO is how CA forces support Army commanders with military government responsibilities
     described in Army and Joint doctrine. Army doctrine describes the army commander’s military government
     responsibility but does not describe the capabilities required to perform military government. In the absence
     of higher Army doctrine, this ATP draws on Joint doctrine for guidance regarding transitional governance
     and military government.

     1-19. Joint doctrine describes the operational environment of a competition continuum. Rather than a world
     at peace or at war, the competition continuum describes three broad categories of strategic relationships—
     cooperation, competition below armed conflict, and armed conflict. Each relationship is defined as between
     the United States and another strategic actor relative to a specific set of policy aims. Cooperation, competition,
     and even armed conflict commonly occur simultaneously in different parts of the world. Due to this
     competition continuum, the strategic needs of others affect the needs of combatant commanders and Army
     component commanders in the same area. (See JP 3-0.)

             Note. This manual uses the term competition to mean competition below armed conflict.

     1-20. Transitional governance combines national policy and theater strategy exercised simultaneously
     through the instruments of national power. The level of involvement for each of the four instruments is not
     set in any phase of the competition continuum. If one instrument has less involvement, the involvement of
     another instrument must increase to keep the environment in balance. The military leads when U.S. national
     command authority directs military intervention due to crisis, disaster, or a decision that diplomatic efforts
     are ineffective or appropriate. (Figure 1-1 visualizes the malleable nature of the interdependent relationships
     among the four instruments of national power across the competition continuum.)

     1-21. The U.S. DOS leads all U.S. diplomatic initiatives such as engaging with other states and foreign groups
     to advance U.S. values, interests, and objectives, and to solicit foreign support for U.S. military operations.
     Diplomacy is the primary instrument of national power employed during competition and crisis.

     1-22. Information throughout the continuum remains an important supporting instrument. The informational
     instrument is a strategic resource critical to national security, which uses information to further U.S. causes
     and undermine those opposed to the USG and our allies.

     1-23. The economic instrument can be both positive (aid programs) and punitive (sanctions) in shaping
     U.S. objectives. The Department of the Treasury collaborates with various USG agencies, foreign
     governments, and international financial institutions. Their collective efforts aim to foster economic growth,
     enhance living standards, and proactively anticipate and mitigate economic and financial crises to the best of
     their abilities.

     1-24. The military instrument of national power can be applied in a scalable manner that varies in purpose,
     scale, risk, and combat intensity across the competition continuum, connecting the tactical, operational, and
     strategic levels of war. The United States uses the range of military operations to provide insight into the
     various usages of military power from a strategic perspective. Waging war should involve the use of all
     instruments of national power that one government can bring to bear against another particularly at the
     higher levels. Leaders must not consider the use of military force in isolation.

TRANSITIONAL MILITARY AUTHORITY AND
MILITARY GOVERNMENT

     1-26. TMA and military government play critical roles in managing transitions and maintaining stability in
     complex environments. Essentially, it is the difference between authority to control and the mechanism for
     applying control.

TRANSITIONAL MILITARY AUTHORITY

     1-27. Transitional military authority is the supreme authority the military exercises by force or agreement
     over the lands, property, and indigenous populations and institutions of allied, neutral, or enemy territory,
     therefore, substituting sovereign authority under rule of law for the previously established government.
     When directed, military forces assert TMA to establish a military government, supplanting the authority of a
     sovereign government. In the absence of a functioning government, or when military operations prevent a
     government from administering to the public sector, military forces use military government to ensure the
     continuation of government functions. Military forces support stabilization activities through the execution of
     stability operations tasks until directed to transfer operations to other military forces or designated civil authorities.

     1-28. The U.S. President authorizes the use of TMA, and the Secretary of Defense and the joint force

MILITARY GOVERNMENT

     1-30. When TMA is directed and instituted, a temporary military government is required. A military
     government is a necessary proactive measure to promote stability and prevent an insurgency after conflict.
     Army forces conduct stability operations tasks in conjunction with large-scale combat operations to lead the
     initial stabilization effort. Military forces achieve unified action through civil-military integration (CMI)
     when performing stabilization activities. Stabilization activities include governance and participation, and
     stability operations tasks include support to governance. These activities are established on a temporary basis
     and transitioned to another authority when directed. Military government can draw assistance from
     experienced interagency personnel. These agencies and organizations possess the expertise to establish a
     system of government that fosters the gradual transition to a legitimate host-nation authority. Military forces
     lead until they stabilize the security situation and can safely transition responsibility for the stability sectors
     to legitimate civil authority and civil control.

     1-31. Military government is a temporary military organization exercising governance and the functions

1-34. Based on the tenets of international law, the occupying force must maintain an orderly government in
the occupied territory and must have, as its goal, the creation of a legitimate and effective civilian

     • Military governor. A military governor is the military commander or other designated person who,
          in an occupied territory, exercises supreme authority over the civil population subject to the laws
          and usages of war and to any directive received from the commander’s government or superior.

     • Military governor proclamation. The proclamation, propagated through media outlets, informs the
          populace of the occupation, the intent of the military operation, and military government ordinances.
          While the proclamation seeks to allay fears and encourage cooperation from the occupied
          population, it also instructs them to obey the existing laws and military government ordinance.

     • Military government ordinance. A military government ordinance is an enactment on the authority of
          a military governor promulgating laws or rules regulating the occupied territory under such control.

1-35. Stabilization is an inherent responsibility of government, and when asserting TMA, a military
requirement. The degree of military participation in long-term stabilization efforts is a political decision of
the President or other Executive Department leaders. When asserting military government, military leaders
and Soldiers may be appointed as governors in a broad sense, influencing events and circumstances normally
outside the bounds of the military. In theater planning for MGO, from strategic to tactical, CA capabilities
prevent harm to civilian populations and their associated property, mitigate friction between the military and
civil component, engage the civil component to set the conditions for stability, and assess civil considerations
necessary to achieve understanding of the operational environment (including civil society). The intent of
MGO is to return the operational environment to stability and competition. The key to the stability effort is
a detailed conflict assessment, and an assessment of how the local population perceives U.S. activities and
the support of the emerging indigenous government. Successful stability efforts require the support of the
populace and confirmation that the U.S. mission and the actions of the emergent government are
legitimate in the eyes of the populace. There is no mechanism to measure the sentiment of the
population. Some indicators include the outward willingness of the populace to participate in governance
through public debate or voting. An indicator can be the result of a third-party polling or survey. One clear
measure of effectiveness is the abandonment of armed conflict to impose or shape policy.

1-36. Stability planning efforts nest within those of the DOS. (ADP 3-07 discusses nesting stability tasks
with stability activities including the stability sectors.) Nesting tactical to strategic efforts requires an
understanding of the joint stability functions at the strategic echelon. At the operational and tactical echelons,
planners use the Army’s stability operations tasks.

1-37. The stability sectors form a framework for executing stability operations that represents the five key
areas in which civil-military efforts focus on building host-nation capacity. Individually, the stability
operations tasks encompass the distinct yet interrelated tasks that constitute reform activities in a functional
sector. Collectively, they are the pillars upon which the government frames the possible reconstruction tasks
required for nations torn by conflict or disaster. Although U.S. forces execute some tasks sequentially,
success necessitates an approach that focuses on simultaneous actions across an operational area. These tasks
are inextricably linked; positive results in one sector depend on integrating and synchronizing tasks across
other sectors successfully.

1-38. CA forces provide functional specialists and military government officers that align with the stability

are available to accomplish the stability operations tasks that set the conditions for stabilization. To ensure
that no lapse in governance occurs, CA forces perform tasks that a civil authority usually performs.

FORMS OF MILITARY GOVERNMENT

     1-41. An operational form of military government expands in authority as operations continue. In the
     territorial form of military government, the joint force commander establishes a separate organization under
     the direct command of the joint force commander or an authorized subordinate.

OPERATIONAL FORM OF MILITARY GOVERNMENT

     1-42. The responsibilities and geographic area over which a military government exercises civil
     administration expand as operations continue. Commanders oversee civil functions of government in their
     respective operational areas. This includes ensuring the safety, security, and well-being of the local populace
     as well as providing humanitarian assistance in conjunction with other USG agencies foreign assistance
     operations. Under the operational form of military government, the chain of command retains the
     responsibility for authority and is supported by its staff structure.

     1-43. Concentrating authority and responsibility in the commander ensures that activities related to civil
     administration remain integrated with ongoing operations. These activities include relationships between the
     military force and civilians. By ensuring the integrity of unity of command in an operational area,
     commanders mitigate much of the friction associated with operations in and among the local populace.
     As the situation permits, the responsibility for civil administration transfers to the local civil authority to
     assist the nation’s return to self-governance. Using a transitional authority or civilian advisory groups helps
     to accelerate this transfer of authority.

     1-44. The rate of military activities frequently tempers advantages of a military government’s operational
     form. Generally, the higher the tempo within the operational area, the less the commander can address the
     requirements of military government. In areas where the tempo of operations and civil situations are
     consistently dynamic, civil administration policies may change frequently. Finally, operational headquarters
     are not always assigned operational areas corresponding to known political subdivisions. Even after
     hostilities, conformance of these areas to political boundaries may prove impossible, however desirable.

TERRITORIAL FORMS OF MILITARY GOVERNMENT

     1-45. In the territorial form of military government, commanders establish a separate organization to exercise
     civil administration functions. The organization may be under the direct command of the joint force
     commander or an authorized subordinate, or it may report directly to the Secretary of Defense or the
     President. The military governor may command subordinate military governors assigned to political
     subdivisions throughout the occupied territory. Usually, the territorial form of the military government
     follows a chain of command separate from operational forces.

POPULACE AND RESOURCES CONTROL

     1-53. To provide governance (security, order, and essential services) to a population, there must be a level of
     control over the populace and resources. To secure and stabilize the area and set conditions ideal for the
     consolidation of gains, the occupying force implements populace and resources control measures.
     These measures aim to secure and control the population and promote stability. In theaters where pre-existing
     treaties exist, the occupation of enemy territory results in a coalition of nations providing governance.

     1-54. Populace and resources control measures result from policy developed at national and theater strategic
     levels and are implemented at the operational and tactical levels. Populace and resources controls are
     operations which provide security for the populace, deny personnel and materiel to the enemy, mobilize
     population and materiel resources, and detect and reduce the effectiveness of enemy agents. Populace control
     measures include curfews, movement restrictions, travel permits, registration cards, and resettlement of
     civilians. Resource control measures include licensing, regulations or guidelines, checkpoints (for example,
     roadblocks), ration controls, amnesty programs, and inspection of facilities.

SOVEREIGNTY ENCROACHMENT

     2-21. Sovereignty encroachments are areas that are not subordinate or accountable to the national
     government. A group challenges the legitimacy and stability of the state when they declare in words or actions
     that the government no longer holds jurisdiction over them. Sovereignty requires a government that resident
     populations recognize as the supreme authority that has recognized borders and a monopoly on the use of
     force within those borders. Challenges to sovereignty include—

          • Supplanting the government.
          • Declaring a separate state within the territory of a recognized state.
          • Refusing to recognize the rule of law and instead implementing different laws.
          • Limiting central governance within a section of the country or running a shadow or alternative government.

     2-22. Examples include cities or regions forging independent internal and even international policies and
     trade agreements based on their economic power, drug cartels establishing their own de facto rule over certain
     areas, and violent extremist groups taking over so-called ungoverned areas within the boundaries of states.
     External actors may easily exploit countries whose sovereignty is challenged by forging agreements with
     either the government or the challengers to gain advantage.

CROSS-BORDER ENCROACHMENT

     2-23. Spillover effects from proximity to sources of instability. Conflict, illicit activity, and effects on the
     environment in one country may spill across the border due to proximity, sociocultural and historical ties, or
     deliberate efforts. Cross-border effects are largely the result of neighboring governments’ inability to manage
     internal dynamics and maintain rule of law. For example, insurgents, violent extremist groups, or other
     criminal elements may cross borders to avoid authorities and draw the people or government of that area into
     the conflict or illicit activity. Groups can exploit cross border encroachment to gain advantage over people,
     groups, or governments through threats, withholding of resources and benefits, or direct interference in
     sovereign territories.

INEFFECTIVE GOVERNMENT

     2-24. The inability of a government to provide for a society’s essential needs. A government is deemed
     ineffective when it is unable to sufficiently provide the necessary public goods and services for its people to
     function in society, thus, failing to motivate public trust and participation. Ineffective governments range on
     a continuum. Examples of ineffective governance include an inability to impartially adjudicate legal cases or
     maintain public order, a legislature that cannot perform its representational duties, or bureaucracies that are
     not fulfilling basic needs for their population such as functioning hospitals, schools, utilities, and public
     transportation. Corruption, including nepotism, cronyism, kleptocracy, and other forms of predatory behavior
     are often symptoms of an ineffective government. Corruption may enhance perceptions of government failure
     or fill gaps in governance to advance their own interests to exploit government ineffectiveness.

     of money and number of people involved. Illicit networks are a pervasive feature of the operational
     environment and include drug cartels, radical ideological groups, human trafficking networks, unlicensed
     resource extraction ventures, and neighborhood gangs. Actors establish linkages with illicit networks to gain
     freedom of movement to pursue their interests by undermining governmental, economic and law and order
     systems.

RESOURCE SCARCITY

     2-28. Resource scarcity occurs when the demand for a resource (or resources) exceeds the available supply.
     Economic decisions must be made to manage both the availability and the proper distribution of resources to
     meet specific needs. There are four distinctive types of scarcity: demand-induced, wherein demand for a
     resource increases, but the supply stays the same; supply-induced, wherein demand for a resource is
     consistent but the supply decreases; structural, wherein the resource is unable to be harvested or distributed
     for structural reasons; and government interference, wherein policy restricts the resource, which causes slow
     or interrupted supply chains. When scarcity occurs, it can lead to competition for resources among different
     groups. Scarcity of arable land can create competition and conflict among groups, labor shortages can force
     populations to economically migrate, or a fixed supply of transport networks may produce massive
     congestion. Actors exploit resource scarcity by exacerbating or mitigating the deficit to sew instability or to
     curry favor with the government or population.

MOBILIZING IDEOLOGIES

     2-29. Unifying belief systems threaten the status quo. Ideologies that unify a critical mass of people in
     defiance of political authority can be a major source of instability in any political system. Unifying belief
     systems are most potent when they incentivize large segments of a population to act collectively in pursuit
     of an alternative socio-political configuration. Ideological belief systems that threaten the political status quo
     may be rooted in religious, communal, historical, or other group-identity forms of solidarity. Belief systems
     often invoke tropes and symbols that harken to an idealized past in which the imagined bonds of communal
     solidarity were either deeper, more easily understood, or better aligned with the sociocultural foundations or
     realities of a given population. Actors can exploit mobilized ideologies in various ways to destabilize a
     country, particularly by strengthening mobilized movements through misinformation or other
     information campaigns.

DIVIDED SOCIETIES

     2-30. Competing values or identities create factions within communities. Societies are divided when
     differences between groups create perceptions of deep, insurmountable divisions. The factions that emerge
     may be based on politics, culture or ethnicity, and religion. The inability to communicate through a shared
     language can create division. Pronounced sociocultural divisions can foster intolerance, competition, friction,
     and sometimes high levels of violence. Groups can use strategic information campaigns to easily divide
     societies and exacerbate divisions, especially in areas with longstanding historical conflicts. Even in areas
     with burgeoning conflicts, actors who understand the differences between opposing groups can easily exploit
     them for their own ends.

DEMOGRAPHIC PRESSURES

     2-31. Significant changes within a population can cause stress. Significant changes to the demographic
     structure of populations create social and political pressures that often lead to internal competition and
     conflict. These pressures manifest as stress on the economic, health, environmental, political, and security
     well-being of populations with their form depending on existing social structures. Demographic shifts include
     substantial imbalances in demographic pyramids (such as youth bulges, aging populations, and lost
     generations), or changes to the distribution of a given population (such as urbanization and emigration).
     Shifts in demographic structures often occur in response to broader changes in the socio-political
     environment. Migration, for instance, may be rooted in armed conflict, environmental strain, or new
     economic opportunities, whereas changes to demographic pyramids may occur through improvements in
     health care, increased prosperity, or government policies limiting population growth. Demographic pressures
     may strain the environment’s ability to support a population and in doing so create social tensions and
     divisions. Actors may leverage these challenges to their advantage by easing some of the pressures and thus
     gaining favor with populations and governments.

TECHNOLOGICAL ADVANCES WITHIN SOCIETY

     2-32. Mass adoption of disruptive technologies, trends, and significant technological changes can rapidly
     upend established sociocultural norms. Many societies are becoming increasingly reliant on the digitalization
     of many aspects of life. Widespread access to information and communication via the Internet of Things
     creates more informed and connected populations. Private encryption technology enables users to bypass
     institutional systems to conduct business, manage wealth, and communicate. Automation, including artificial
     intelligence, is replacing human labor and technology, and increasingly controlling and operating vital
     systems. This technification of society may exacerbate social inequities, creating additional divisions in
     societies. Access to new technologies varies globally and is primarily available to those with control over its
     distribution and use. Actors exploit technologically controlled systems through actual or threatened intrusion,
     damage, and destruction potentially leading to denial of essential services, economic, and risk to public
     health. Additionally, actors may seek to use perceived failures or shortcomings against a government as
     leverage or to foment unrest among affected populations.

     2-35. CA forces execute MGO using multiple types of assessments. Capacity and functions are continually

     2-36. When forces organize consolidation of gains efforts across the competition continuum, distinct
     purposes emerge for competition, crisis, and large-scale combat operations. In competition, consolidation of

     success at later stages while decreasing costs and risks. Accordingly, the preponderance of consolidation of
     gains investments should occur in the competition stage to maximize success in a crisis and a victory in conflict.

GOVERNANCE ASSESSMENT PROCESS

     2-37. The GAP provides the framework for assessing governance by analyzing capability, evaluating the

     platform at every level. A Soldier with a notebook can execute the GAP at the tactical level, which requires
     minimal resources, or as an advanced analytical tool that automated systems and capabilities at higher levels

MILITARY GOVERNMENT OPERATIONS DURING CONFLICT

     interests. Armed conflict can range from irregular warfare to conventional warfare and combinations of both.
     Entering and terminating armed conflict is a political decision. Army forces may enter conflict with some
     advanced warning during a prolonged crisis or with little warning during competition. How well Army forces

     3-2. One of the four strategic roles of the Army is to prevail in large-scale ground combat. The ability to

     U.S. Army participates in occurs among the civilian populations inhabiting that land.

     3-3. As the joint force quickly transitions to armed conflict, CA forces mobilize civil networks. This enables

     of the population.

REAR AREA OPERATIONS

     3-25. The rear area is the portion of the land commander’s area of operations that may be designated to
     facilitate freedom of action, consolidate gains, and set conditions to transition the area of operations to follow
     on forces or other legitimate authorities. As governance transitions into the rear area, the goal is to transition
     more of the responsibilities of governance from military forces back into the control of civilian authorities.

     network is vital for the ultimate transition to legitimate, civil authorities as the culmination of consolidation
     of gains.

     control of governance. A task force achieves this transfer of authority through conduct of the stability
     operations tasks (minus conducting security cooperations) and the integration of military elements, other
     governmental agencies, and local civil authorities.

     establishment of civil security and civil control, the restoration of essential services, and the control of
     populace and resources, frees lines of communication for the movement of supplies, personnel, and

                  Governance Assessment Process

    The GAP provides a methodology for analyzing governance capability. The GAP identifies
    governance gaps to evaluate the populace’s view of that government’s legitimacy. The process
    also identifies modeling strategies for addressing governance gaps within the civil component
    of an area of operations. Once CA forces identify governance gaps, they develop and prioritize
    implementation strategies to target those gaps. The GAP is a tactical level assessment process
    that is platform agnostic and does not require advanced analytical systems or capabilities to
    complete. CA forces complete the GAP using minimal resources during large-scale combat
    operations or in hostile, denied, or politically sensitive environments.

GOVERNANCE ASSESSMENT PROCESS

     B-1. Information analysis and evaluation of the civil component is vital to achieving situational
     understanding and planning successful operations. When conducting civil knowledge integration, CA forces
     analyze and evaluate information from the civil component to inform the warfighting functions and increase
     the commander’s situational awareness. Through the GAP, the analysis of governance information creates
     governance knowledge. Governance knowledge allows CA planners to develop and evaluate options for
     affecting legitimacy of relevant governing bodies.

     B-2. Governance is the state’s ability to serve the citizens through the rules, processes, and behavior by
     which interests are articulated, resources are managed, and power is exercised in a society (JP 3-24).
     Government is the structure that is used to conduct governance. Government structure includes all the public
     systems used to create laws, administer justice, provide essential services, and maintain order.

     B-3. The legitimacy of relevant governing bodies is based on the populations’ perception of their ability to
     provide governance. According to JP 3-24, all governments govern through a combination of consent,
     influence, and to some degree, coercion. The host-nation government generally needs some level of
     legitimacy among the population to retain the confidence of the populace and an acknowledgment of
     governing power. ADP 3-07 states, “Legitimacy is a condition based upon the perception by specific
     audiences of the legality, morality, or rightness of a set of actions and of the propriety of the authority of the
     individuals or organizations in taking them.”

     B-4. The GAP is a process specifically designed to analyze the legitimacy of relevant governing bodies
     within an area of operations by identifying gaps in the provision of government services for a target
     population. The populace views a relevant governing body that is providing for their needs as legitimate and
     stable. According to JP 3-07, “…ultimately, stability results from government effectiveness and perceptions
     of its legitimacy by the people it represents.” Identification of governance gaps and development of options
     to address those gaps provides CA forces with the civil knowledge they need to ensure appropriate control
     and the continuation of government services across military operations. (Figure B-1, page 62, depicts where
     the GAP as a continuing process feed into the civil knowledge integration process.)

     B-11. A governance gap is the delta between what a target population expects, needs, or demands from its

          • Government structure.

     the identification of those governance gaps and development of CAO to address the governance gaps.

IMPLEMENTATION STRATEGIES

CHARACTERISTICS OF MILITARY GOVERNMENT OPERATIONS

C-1. The MGO necessitate distinctive qualities that are unique to CA forces. Collectively,                  these
characteristics are keys to mission success. The characteristics of MGO are outlined below:

     • Demonstrate cultural competency.
     • Promote government for the local population by the local population.
     • Enable local authorities as much as practicable.
     • Implement sustainable solutions with local standards.
     • Plan transfer criteria.

DEMONSTRATE CULTURAL COMPETENCY

C-2. A competency is a combination of skill, knowledge, and experience. Cultural competency is a
combination of understanding culture and cultural adaptability.

C-3. Understanding culture is defined as understanding the different dimensions of culture, how cultures
vary according to key elements such as interpersonal relations, concept of time, attitude towards interpersonal
space, thinking style, tolerance, and authority as well as values, beliefs, behaviors, and norms. Cultural
adaptability is the gathering and interpreting of information about people and surroundings and adjusting
behavior to interact effectively with others. CA forces must understand people’s actions in relation to their
culture. In addition, CA forces must show respect for the people and their culture to maintain a
positive rapport.

GOVERNMENT FOR THE LOCAL POPULATION BY THE LOCAL POPULATION

C-4. CA forces must understand the importance of supporting the government through the local population.
Searching for the local solution or using the local population or local government as the face of a project or
operation to achieve success avoids fixing what is not broken or providing advice and assistance too soon.
The local population’s participation and execution fosters a sense of ownership or buy-in, adding to the
legitimacy of the government. When describing legitimacy in government, there are three subtopics of
legitimacy: legal authority, traditional authorities, and charismatic authority, all viewed through the lens of
the population. Legitimacy is important for all regimes or governments to sustain political stability.

C-5. The government systems where local government is extremely important includes public safety
(police, law enforcement administration, fire protection, emergency and rescue, and penal systems), public
health (clinics, hospitals, pharmacies, emergency services, and so on), and environmental management
(plans, policies, procedures). (See the following vignette.)

     depending upon the conditions on the ground where forces execute the operations. Until civil security and
     civil control are restored, making it possible to restore essential services, CA forces must enforce more
     restrictive populace and resources controls. As the security situation stabilizes, less restrictive measures are
     necessary when dealing with the civil populace. This facilitates a civil administration in the occupied territory
     and sets the conditions for the diplomatic treaties and agreements necessary for a civil administration in
     host-nation territory, and ultimately foreign assistance. The following factors should be considered:

          • The baseline amount of governance and levels of essential services in existence before the crisis or
                armed conflict.

          • The attitudes of the people toward their local security (police or military forces), toward
                United States or friendly forces, and their degree of cooperation with these forces.

          • The degree of movement toward pre-conflict conditions regarding industrial, economic, political,
                and social development; and the political, diplomatic, and military policy of the U.S. Government
                toward the government in the transitional area of operations.

     C-18. Force may be used to the extent necessary by local law enforcement, security forces, or by the
     U.S. military as a last resort to prevent the escape of prisoners or persons suspected of crimes. Persons
     accused are entitled to a fair trial before the imposition of punishment. Local pre-conflict laws should be
     examined for fairness and equity and followed as closely as possible. The maximum punishment should not
     be awarded automatically. The nature of sentences to be imposed and whether they should be carried out in
     public, depends in part upon the customs and habits of the population and the types of punishment which
     have been found most effective in the locality.

     C-19. Arrests, the imposition of collective fines, or the carrying out of judicial punishment become military
     necessities in some situations but should be taken only as an unavoidable last resort to induce a hostile
     population to desist from attacking United States. or United States-related interests. Local law enforcement
     must ensure safe, judicious, and effective control of the inhabitants as operations move to stability and transition.
     The following vignette illustrates how performing even the smallest actions can garner respect and build
     legitimacy within the populace for the occupying power.

RETENTION OF EXISTING LAWS, CUSTOMS, AND POLITICAL SUBDIVISIONS

     C-20. Theater commanders should consider the U.S. Women, Peace, and Security Act of 2017 and its strategy
     to fulfill a whole of government approach to military government. Commanders must also allow diplomacy
     to set the rules for government participation to avoid imposing western or U.S. laws when implementing
     military government. Any attempted changes or reforms contrary to local custom may result in the
     development of active or passive resistance and thereby handicap theater operations and consolidation of
     gains. For similar reasons, when possible, it is advisable to retain existing territorial divisions and
     subdivisions. Laws and customs in one political division of a country may differ widely from those in another
     and the inhabitants may be accustomed to decentralization of governmental authority, which usually parallels
     such divisions.

     C-21. Theater commanders should not attempt to impose western or U.S. laws when conducting military
     government. CA forces conducting MGO to support military government must not attempt to impose western
     or U.S. laws. Any attempted changes or reforms contrary to local custom may result in the development of

     accustomed to decentralization of governmental authority, which usually parallels such divisions.

RETENTION OF LOCAL GOVERNMENT DEPARTMENTS AND OFFICIALS

     C-22. Offices which are unnecessary or detrimental to the host nation or United States interests should be
     temporarily discontinued or suspended, if possible. In some areas, this may be the case with entire
     departments or bureaus of the government. Such legislative bodies still in existence are usually suspended.

     C-23. Sometimes it is necessary to remove high ranking political officials from office. This action could
     include the removal of the nominal and actual heads of the government, from national to local levels, and
     may include cabinet ministers and the heads of principal political divisions. All recommendations for
     replacement during MGO support to military government is brought to the appropriate echelon commander
     and the populous so that a replacement may be selected or elected. No permanent appointments to such
     positions should be made by a CA element and no elections should be held without approval of a higher
     authority because of the political implications of such appointments. Willful failure of retained host-nation
     officials at any level to perform their duties satisfactorily should be regarded as a serious offense against the
     people and the host-nation government.

     C-24. In some cases, former officials may disappear into the civilian population due to initial fears that forces
     may persecute them for their role in the previous government. Some civil officials may flee as large-scale
     combat operations begin, or when it is inexpedient or unsafe for them to continue in office. Eventually, forces
     locate them through civilian leaders and civil network engagement.

     C-25. Some of the selections for key posts may be trusted nonprofessionals. The U.S. commander vets the
     officials’ choice of subordinates. So far as practicable, subordinate officials and employees of the local
     government should be retained in their offices and made responsible for the proper discharge of their duties.

     C-26. CA personnel should attempt to deal with the inhabitants at each echelon in the area of operation
     through retained or appointed employees of the local government. When officials are replaced, replacements
     should be sought from among the inhabitants who by training and experience are qualified to take over the
     duties of the office. In the selection of government officials, careful consideration should be given to their
     reliability, their positions in the community, and their other qualifications for the position.

ESTABLISH CIVIL SECURITY

     D-3. Establishing civil security involves providing for the safety of the host nation and its population,
     including protection from internal and external threats. (See ADP 3-07.) Establishing a safe, secure, and
     stable environment is key to obtaining local support for large-scale combat operations and any other
     military action.

     D-4. Civil security enables freedom of maneuver to allow the USG and our unified action partners to
     concentrate on consolidating gains. Civil security sets the conditions for all other stability tasks, as no other
     stability task can be effective in high-threat environments while allowing for the transition of authority to
     legitimate, stable, and sustainable institutions.

     D-5. The CA civil security functional specialty area is concerned with civil security and public order which
     generates the foundation for stability within the operational environment. This function relates to the
     development and administration of policies, planning mechanisms, and training partner nation public officials
     to respond to threats and hazards (manmade, natural, internal, and external) across all levels of government.
     When required, the capabilities within this functional specialty area assist with the establishment and
     maintenance of a military government.

     D-6. CA functional specialists support civil security by performing CR, CE, and CND in MGO to support
     civil security. Civil security specialists provide knowledge, experience, and technical expertise in public
     safety, including protection from internal and external threats. Civil security specialists analyze, assess, and
     evaluate in a multitude of security topics including cessation of large-scale violence, public order, DDR,
     border control, territorial security, explosives and hazards clearance, and key terrain, personnel, and facilities
     protection. Civil security concentrations include law enforcement administration, application of the rule of
     law, the legitimate monopoly of force, and science-based investigative methodologies.

     D-7. The following paragraphs describe the CA functional specialties, and the responsibilities of the military
     government officers, that are available to support the establishment of civil security.

LAWS, REGULATIONS, AND POLICIES

     D-8. The laws, regulations, and policies focus area is part of the rule of law functional specialty which
     includes expertise in political systems and legal frameworks. These specialists support stable and inclusive
     host nation governance and non-violent conflict management to shape the policies that support the DDR of
     partisans and combatants. They work with commanders and host nation representatives to establish,
     strengthen, and reform legislative and political structures to achieve these goals.

Corrections

     D-9. The corrections functional specialty includes specialists with expertise in conducting and managing
     the administration of corrections operations. Corrections functional specialists advise on corrections systems
     that result in the safe and humane detention and incarceration of prisoners for the maintenance of public
     order. Corrections specialists works to resolve the disposition of persons that the previous government
     imprisoned for political or racial reasons only.

Law and Border Enforcement

     D-10. The law and border enforcement functional specialty include experts with knowledge and experience
     of conducting transitional law enforcement. These specialists are instrumental to the process of resettling
     dislocated civilians. They design and improve operational and ministerial responses to illicit power networks,
     implement community security approaches, and mitigate violence targeted to vulnerable populations. (See
     FM 3-57 for more information on the civil security functional specialty focus areas.)

     for governance transition to legitimate authorities. Public trust and civil order require legal systems that are

RESTORE ESSENTIAL SERVICES

     D-16. The restoration of essential services ultimately contributes to achieving a stable democracy, a
     sustainable economy, and the social well-being of the population. Restoring essential services reduces the
     risk of a humanitarian disaster, which could disrupt operational momentum and the consolidation of gains.

     D-17. CA functional specialists perform CR, CE, and CND to support the restoration of essential services in
     MGO to provide knowledge, experience, and technical expertise in an immediate and sustainable social
     well-being. Essential services specialists assess, analyze, and evaluate fundamental needs of the populace,
     beyond the aspects of security, for the eventual and sustainable transition to legitimate authorities following
     large-scale combat operations and any other military action. Immediate and emergency needs include food,
     water, shelter, and medical support. Restoring of essential services enables the reestablishment of stable
     economic systems, education, public health, and resettlement. The full spectrum of social needs must be
     addressed and transitioned to capable institutions to prevent future destabilization and security deterioration
     in rear areas. Essential services concentrations include specialties in food relief, water security, sanitation,
     public works, power, education administration, public health, transportation and distribution,
     communications, and human dislocation and resettlement.

SUPPORT TO GOVERNANCE

     D-22. Support to governance addresses the processes, systems, institutions, and actors that enable a state to
     function; effective, legitimate governance ensures that these are transparent, accountable, and involve public
     participation (ADP 3-07). Support to governance activities leverage the successful implementation of civil
     security, civil control, and essential services (consolidation of gains). In addition, support to governance sets
     critical conditions for the execution of economic and infrastructure development and the conduct of
     security cooperation.

     D-23. The role of CA governance experts is to synchronize the efforts of government. Government experts
     perform CR, CE, and CND in military operations to support this activity. They provide knowledge,
     experience, and technical expertise in public administration, cultural relations, public information, and
     environmental management. Governance officers develop programs, policies, systems, and institutions to
     enable a state’s ability to serve the citizens and recommend rules, processes, and behavior by which the
     citizenry can articulate interests, participate in nonviolent political processes, and enjoy the collective benefits
     and services of the nation. When required, the capabilities within this functional specialty area assist in the
     establishment and maintenance of a military government.

     D-24. A crucial component of the government functional specialty addresses the former combatants within
     the population. Without a monopoly on the use of force, a state has few ways to uphold the rule of law and
     protect the inalienable rights of citizens. By definition, a conflict-affected state has lost this monopoly, and
     must restore or establish it by disbanding nonstate, armed actors and reconstituting statutory forces.

     D-25. A complex DDR process is implemented to achieve reconciliation and renewal of legitimacy in the
     government. DDR is a confidence-building program to induce confidence and legitimacy in government and
     mitigate reprisals and animosity between government, factions, and the population. DDR efforts strive to
     increase stability by disarming and demobilizing former combatants (for example, government and rebel
     forces). These former combatants are then reintegrated into civil society or incorporated into the new armed
     forces and police. Reintegration is the process through which former combatants, belligerents, and displaced
     civilians receive amnesty, reenter civil society, gain sustainable employment, and become contributing
     members of the local populace (ADP 3-07).

     D-26. A successful DDR program helps establish sustainable stability. A DDR program considers the
     different needs of the population associated with armed forces and groups, as well as those of civilians forced
     to flee their homes after violent conflict or disaster. A failed or partially implemented DDR effort can stall
     security sector reform, disrupt peace processes, and destabilize communities socially and economically.

SUPPORT TO ECONOMIC AND INFRASTRUCTURE DEVELOPMENT

     D-35. Support to economic and infrastructure development addresses a host-nation’s economic sector and
     critical infrastructure and how it affects sustainable economic development. The economic viability of a state
     is among the first elements of society to exhibit stress and ultimately fracture as conflict, disaster, and internal
     strife overwhelm the government (ADP 3-07). Building a host-nation’s capacity within the economic sector
     requires an integrated approach to achieve sustainable growth. Appropriate civilian or host-nation
     organizations can accomplish much of this effort at the macro level through development mechanisms, but
     they may look to military forces for security, in conjunction with the other stability operations tasks.

     D-36. Economic development function specialists support this activity by performing CR, CE, and CND in
     military operations. The specialists provide knowledge, experience, and technical expertise in economic
     generation, industry and production, price and currency stabilization, control over an illicit economy, and
     individual economic stability. Specialists must address these tasks early in their planning. They cannot
     achieve full potential and implementation until civil security, civil control, restoration of essential services,
     and the reestablishment of governance is in place. These programs are crucial to the progression from stability
     to stabilization and reconstruction, and ultimately to the transfer to other legitimate authorities.

     D-37. The following paragraphs describe the CA functional specialties, and the responsibilities of the military
     government officers that work in concert with civil security, civil control, and essential services and
     governance functions.

Economic Development

     D-38. The economic development functional specialty places emphasis on the existing national to localized
     economic network. These specialists have expertise and experience in the development, implementation,
     regulation, and enforcement of interstate and international policies and programs. These policies and
     programs focus on macroeconomics, market(s) development, and microeconomics. This functional specialty
     team is prepared to assume the administrative and supervisory roles of the national and subnational
     institutions when conditions allow.

Industry and Production

     D-39. The industry and production focus area are part of the economic development functional specialty,
     which includes expertise and experience monitoring and assessing an indigenous economy, economic
     systems, and the potential for commercial activities. These specialists can determine a host nation’s economic
     capabilities, the impacts of labor throughout the economy, and the nation’s impact on governance and the
     rule of law.

Energy

     D-40. The energy focus area is also part of the economic development functional specialty. The specialists
     work within the public works and utility’s function. Specialists have expertise and experience in the
     production and distribution of electric, petroleum, natural gas, coal, nuclear, and other renewable sources.
     These specialists build, sustain, and improve host nation capabilities to establish, strengthen, or rehabilitate
     systems to produce energy to meet the needs of the population.

Finance, Money, and Banking

     D-41. The finance, money, and banking focus area are part of the economic development functional specialty.
     Specialists in this specialty possess expertise and experience in the technical aspects of monitoring and
     assessing banking, financial activities, agencies, services personnel, and resources. These specialists can
     create programs and policies to build, sustain, and improve host nation capabilities to develop financial sector
     programs and policies to stabilize and enhance the development of a host nation. These specialists work
     directly with military finance officers to analyze and evaluate an economic environment to identify potential
     vulnerabilities through threat financing. They can build capacities and capabilities to monitor monetary
     regimes, fiscal policy, domestic economic environment, foreign direct investment, foreign lending, and grant
     assistance. Monetary regimes include interest rates, exchange rates, currency black markets and
     counterfeiting, foreign exchange reserves, traditional banking, cryptocurrencies and cryptocurrency
     exchanges, and digital and informal value transfer systems). Fiscal policy includes taxes and government
     spending. Domestic economic environment is price controls; price discrimination; quotas; subsidies; tariffs;
     inflation, deflation, and hyperinflation; monopoly and oligopolies; employment and unemployment; and
     informal economy.

Commerce and Trade

     D-42. The economic development functional specialty includes the commerce and trade focus area. is part
     of. Commerce and trade include expertise and experience in the administration of economic controls related
     to monetary and fiscal policy. These specialists can assess the existing or former national to local economic
     networks, compare them to competing systems, and create policies to strengthen economic and industrial
     stability within the operational environment.

Technology and Telecommunications

     D-43. The technology and telecommunications focus area is part of the public communication functional
     specialty. The technology and telecommunications specialists have expertise and experience in the
     development, implementation, regulation, and enforcement of interstate and international policies for radio,
     television, wire, wireless, satellite, cable communication, and postal means.

Transportation

     D-44. The public transportation functional specialty includes the transportation focus area. Specialist in this
     focus area have expertise and experience in the development, reconstruction, and maintenance of
     transportation infrastructure. The specialists work to support the civil security functional specialty because it
     is deeply intertwined with the state’s ability to secure and serve the populace through populace and resources
     control. Specialists work to develop plans, policies, and procedures to build, sustain, and improve host nation
     capabilities. The specialists develop and maintain the national network to enforce interstate and international
     policies for the physical movement of commerce and people across ground and water and through air conveyances.

Agri-business and Food

     D-45. The agri-business and food focus area nests under the food and agriculture functional specialty. These
     specialists can create programs and policies to build, sustain, and improve host nation capabilities to
     implement national local agriculture systems, agencies, services, personnel, methods, resources, institutions,
     and facilities. These include any competing systems and their collective impacts upon conditions of the
     operational environment and effects on stability. Specialists in this focus area provide for the structuring,
     resourcing, organizing, rehabilitation, and implementation of the curriculum and education, facilities,
     technology, processes, and personnel development required to implement and sustain food and agriculture
     activities. (See FM 3-57 for more information on the economic development functional specialty focus areas.)

  SECTION II – TERMS

civil administration

          An administration established by a foreign government in (1) friendly territory, under an agreement
          with the government of the area concerned, to exercise certain authority normally the function of the
          local government; or (2) hostile territory, occupied by United States forces, where a foreign
          government exercises executive, legislative, and judicial authority until an indigenous civil
          government can be established. (JP 3-05)

civil engagement

          A targeted, planned, and coordinated meeting with known or potential contacts in a civil network that
          is designed to develop or maintain relationships and to share or collect information. (FM 3-57)

civil network

          A collection of formal and informal groups, associations, military engagements, and organizations
          within an operational environment that interact with each other with varying degrees of frequency,
          trust, and collaboration. (FM 3-57)

civil network development

          The planned and targeted action in which civil affairs forces develop networks within the civil
          component of the operational environment to influence populations and manage local resources in
          order to extend the operational reach, consolidate gains, and achieve military objectives. (FM 3-57)

civil reconnaissance

          A targeted, planned, and coordinated observation and evaluation of specific civil aspects of the
          environment such as areas, structures, capabilities, organizations, people, or events. (JP 3-57)

close area

          The portion of the commander’s area of operations where the majority of subordinate maneuver forces
          conduct close combat. (ADP 3-0)

consolidating gains

          Activities to make enduring any temporary operational success and to set the conditions for a
          sustainable security environment, allowing for transition of control to other legitimate authorities.
          (ADP 3-0)

crisis

          An emerging incident or situation involving a possible threat to the United States, its citizens, military
          forces, or vital interests that develops rapidly and creates a condition of such diplomatic, economic, or
          military importance that commitment of military forces and resources is contemplated to achieve
          national and/or strategic objectives. (JP 3-0)

governance

          The state’s ability to serve the citizens through the rules, processes, and behavior by which interests are
          articulated, resources are managed, and power is exercised in a society. (JP 3-24)

                                                                                                                                          Glossary

*military government
          (Army) A temporary military organization exercising governance and the functions of civil
          administration in the absence of a legitimate civil authority.

*military government operations
          The actions taken by Civil Affairs forces to support government functions and governance throughout
          the competition continuum.

reintegration
          The process through which former combatants, belligerents, and displaced civilians receive amnesty,
          reenter civil society, gain sustainable employment, and become contributing members of the local
          populace. (ADP 3-07)

support to civil administration
          Assistance given by United States Armed Forces to stabilize or enhance the operations of the
          governing body of a foreign country by assisting an established or interim government. (FM 3-57)

*support to civil administration in a host-nation territory
          Support provided to a host nation to restore or enhance governance capability.

*support to civil administration in an occupied territory
          Support provided to indigenous governing bodies during a military occupation.

*support to foreign assistance
          Civil or military aid and activities rendered to legitimate indigenous authorities of a foreign nation to
          support governance, promote stability, and create conditions for long-term stabilization to support
          United States Government objectives. Support to foreign assistance includes development assistance,
          humanitarian assistance, and security assistance.

unified action
          The synchronization, coordination, or integration of the activities of governmental and
          nongovernmental entities with military operations to achieve unity of effort. (JP 1, Vol 1)

*transitional governance
          The transition of foreign governing authorities and functions based on a United States Government
          policy decision or directive.

*transitional military authority
          (Army) The supreme authority the military exercises by force or agreement over the lands, property,
          and indigenous populations and institutions of allied, neutral, or enemy territory, therefore, substituting
          sovereign authority under rule of law for the previously established government.
